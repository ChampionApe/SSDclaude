\section{Numerical Methods}\label{sec:numerical}

This section describes how the politico-economic equilibria of sections \ref{sec:argentina}--\ref{sec:esc} are computed, with the algorithms and the code in the technical documentation \parencite{BergGE26doc}. The quantitative politico-economic literature computes a time-invariant policy function \parencite{KrusellRR99a1,Forni05a1,Song12a1} or compares steady states across demographic scenarios \parencite{Galasso99a1}. Along a demographic transition, however, the Markov policy function is date-specific, since the policymaker at $t$ faces its own mix of cohorts, $\nu_t$, and a continuation policy set further along the transition. The distinction is immaterial in the equilibrium of proposition \ref{prop:LOG:PEE}, which carries no state, but not under CRRA preferences or with informal savers, where the continuation reaches the tax through the state, or in the design choice, where a design chosen at $t$ is weighed against the electorate of $t+1$.\footnote{In our applications, the distinction has a modest effect on results: a time-invariant policy function, solved at each date's own $\nu_t$ and applied along the projected path, misses the tax rate by roughly 0.2 p.p. and misplaces the design by up to 0.059.} We therefore compute date-specific policy functions by backward induction from a date $T$ (appendix \ref{app:T}), the end of a finite horizon or the start of a steady state in an infinite one. For a large $T$ the two agree away from $T$. We report the finite-horizon solution, which approximates the Markov equilibrium in the limit of finite-horizon economies, the class in which \textcite{mkvss08} establish uniqueness.

In the general case with CRRA preferences, the distribution of labor supply remains independent of policy (part ii of proposition \ref{prop:LOG:EE}) and the distribution of past savings is pinned down by the current tax together with past aggregate savings (appendix \ref{app:EE:CRRA}). While the distribution of past savings is indeed relevant for the political problem, we exploit that it only depends on the current tax and past aggregate savings (appendix \ref{app:EE:CRRA}) to eliminate it from the state space, keeping only the level of past savings $s_{t-1}$: we work with the first order condition in $\tau_t$ derived with the distribution of past savings held fixed, and then replacing it with the one consistent distribution for each candidate tax and for each state $s_{t-1}$. Informal savers add the ratio $s_{t-1,0}/s_{t-1}$ of informal to formal savings to the state space, which also depends on $\tau_{t-1}$ (appendix \ref{app:PEE:LOG_s0}).

When the policy function features one or more states, the current tax moves the states and affects future policy choices. We measure the marginal effect by numerical differentiation of the $t+1$ solution grid, which the backward induction has already computed. A first order condition can have several roots, some of them minima, and the optimum can lie at a corner where it has none, so we evaluate the condition on a grid of taxes and test the corners and all its roots. A candidate passes if it maximizes the political objective with its own consistent distribution held fixed.\footnote{Several passing candidates would be ranked by payoff, an assumption about equilibrium selection that none of our reported equilibria requires: exactly one passes at every state and date of every solve that records the count (\oa{num-selection}).}

Each reported path starts from the steady state under the first period's policy. Parameters whose targets are equilibrium outcomes, such as the discount factor and the political weight of the old, are calibrated by re-solving the equilibrium path at each trial value until the targets of sections \ref{sec:argentina} and \ref{sec:oecd} hold in the calibration year.

Under log preferences the choice of the design in section \ref{sec:esc} adds no state either. The design chosen at $t$ applies from $t+1$ and depends neither on the design chosen in the previous period, which sets current benefits, nor on aggregate savings, and the tax at each candidate design is found as above, without a state. Each date's choice is therefore a search over a grid of designs. Under CRRA preferences neither property survives: the equilibrium is a backward recursion over policy functions of the state $(s_{t-1},\theta_t)$.
