\subsection{Robust policy search with bounds}\label{\refsec:RobustRoot}
The politico-economic equilibria below are identified from first order conditions, and a root problem inherits none of the safeguards of the maximisation it came from: a root may be a minimum, there may be several, and the optimum may sit at a corner where no root exists. This subsection collects the machinery that restores them, stated for a generic first order condition and implemented in \texttt{python/gridsearch}.

Throughout, let $f(\tau)$ denote a first order condition, the derivative of a political objective $\mathcal{W}$ with respect to the tax rate, that it is safe to evaluate on an interval $[l,u]$ with $0\leq l < u < 1$, and let $z(\tau) = f\left([\tau]_{[l,u]}\right)$ denote its bounded evaluation, where $[\tau]_{[l,u]} \equiv \min(u, \max(l,\tau))$.

\smalltitle{A bounded-root reparameterization.}
A gradient-based solver searches all of $\mathbb{R}$, while $f$ is only meaningful on $[l,u]$. The bounded root can instead be found from the unconstrained auxiliary problem
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:root}
    h(z,\tau) = z - |z| \underbrace{\left[a_0\min\left(\tau - l,0\right) + a_1\max\left(\tau - u, 0\right)\right]}_{\equiv g(\tau)} = 0,
\end{align}
with $a_0, a_1>0$. It equals $f(\tau)$ on $[l,u]$ and is affine with a weakly negative slope outside it, so $f$ is never evaluated outside the interval and the bounded solution is $\tau = [\tilde{\tau}]_{[l,u]}$. Corners are roots too, at $\tilde{\tau} = u+1/a_1$ when $f>0$ throughout and at $\tilde{\tau} = l-1/a_0$ when $f<0$ throughout, and a grid-based root detector sees them if the grid carries two outer nodes
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:extendedGrid}
    \tilde{\tau}^{(0)} = l-\frac{1}{a_0}-\delta, \qquad \tilde{\tau}^{(M+1)} = u+\frac{1}{a_1}+\delta,
\end{align}
for a small $\delta>0$, at which a corner appears as an exact zero of $h$ rather than as a sign change; this is why the root detection counts exact zeros as crossings. The solvers of this model use neither device: the selection rule below places $l$ and $u$ among the candidates directly.

\smalltitle{The grid.}
Let $\mathcal{T} = \left\lbrace \tau^{(1)},...,\tau^{(M)}\right\rbrace$ with $\tau^{(1)} = l$ and $\tau^{(M)} = u$ denote a grid on the interior, on which $z$ is evaluated everywhere.

\smalltitle{Selecting among several roots.}
In every application below $z$ is evaluated with a predetermined state substituted consistently with the point of evaluation (the savings shares $s_{t-1,i}/s_{t-1}$ at the tax that savers anticipated), so that, writing $\mathcal{W}(\tau; D)$ for the objective at a frozen state $D$ and $D(\tau)$ for the consistent one,
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:consistentFOC}
    z(\tau) = \partial_{\tau}\mathcal{W}\left(\tau; D\right)\big|_{D = D(\tau)}.
\end{align}
A zero of $z$ is a stationary point of $\mathcal{W}(\cdot\,; D(\tau^{\star}))$, which is the object wanted. But $z$ is not the derivative of any one function of $\tau$: its slope at a crossing, $z' = \mathcal{W}_{\tau\tau} + \mathcal{W}_{\tau D}D'$, is not the second-order condition, and its integral between two crossings is neither the objective at a frozen state nor the consistent one. Candidates are therefore classified and ranked on the objective at their own state.

\emph{Candidates.} With $\hat{z}$ the piecewise-linear interpolant of $z$ through the nodes of $\mathcal{T}$, the candidate set is
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:candidates}
    \mathcal{C} \equiv \left\lbrace l,u \right\rbrace\cup\left\lbrace \tau\in(l,u)\mbox{ }:\mbox{ }\hat{z}(\tau) = 0\right\rbrace,
\end{align}
every crossing in either direction together with the two endpoints. Each candidate $c$ comes with its own predetermined state $D_c \equiv D(\tau_c)$, the one savers would have chosen had they anticipated it.

\emph{The objective at a frozen state.} For each candidate the first order condition is re-evaluated on the whole grid with the state frozen at $D_c$, and integrated by the trapezoidal rule through the nodes,
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:objectiveProfile}
    \mathcal{V}_c(\tau) \equiv \mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{old}}\left(l; D_c\right) + \int_l^{\tau}\partial_{x}\mathcal{W}\left(x; D_c\right)dx, \qquad \tau\in\mathcal{T},
\end{align}
where $\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{old}}$ is the formal retirees' term, a closed form. Then $\mathcal{V}_c(\tau) = \mathcal{W}(\tau; D_c)$ up to terms at $l$ that do not involve the state and are common to all candidates, so values compare across candidates as the objectives do. The integral carries the same numerically smoothed derivatives as $z$ (\cref{\refsec:PEE}), whereas raw utility levels would reintroduce the kinks the smoothing removed. Only the retirees' term is rebuilt per candidate, with $D_c$ formed from the candidate's tax and from hours interpolated linearly at $\tau_c$; every other term of $z$ is already on the grid.

\emph{The equilibrium test.} A candidate is an \emph{equilibrium} at the state if it is the global maximiser of its own frozen objective,
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:equilibriumTest}
    \mathcal{V}_c(\tau_c) \geq \max_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{V}_c(\tau),
\end{align}
up to a tolerance of $10^{-9}$ relative to the scale of $\mathcal{V}_c$. At a corner $\mathcal{V}_c(\tau_c)$ is its node value. At an interior crossing it is read from the quadratic through the three nearest nodes, and two readings at the grid's resolution also pass it: its nearest node attains the maximum of $\mathcal{V}_c$, or, where the stencil touches an infeasible node and only a linear interpolant is available, the maximising node lies within one cell of it. Candidates that pass and lie within one cell of each other are one equilibrium at the grid's resolution, represented by the interior crossing, which is located at sub-grid precision and moves continuously with the data. An upward crossing thus fails at its own state, a downward crossing passes only if no other point beats it there, and a corner passes exactly when its own objective peaks at the corner, which subsumes the boundary check.

\emph{Selection, and what it assumes.} The equilibria at a state need not be unique, and the multiplicity is intrinsic rather than numerical: even when $\mathcal{W}(\cdot\,;D)$ is strictly concave for every $D$, the map $\tau\mapsto\arg\max\mathcal{W}(\cdot\,;D(\tau))$ can have several fixed points, because the shares savers choose depend on the tax they anticipate. When more than one candidate passes \eqref{\refeq:equilibriumTest} we select the one with the highest value of the political objective in its own equilibrium, $\max_c \mathcal{V}_c(\tau_c)$. This selection is part of the definition of the equilibrium being computed: the continuation policy that date-$t$ savers and the date-$t$ electorate take as given is built from the selected policy, so the solution is unique only given the rule. The solvers report, for every state and period, the number of candidates and of equilibria; a run in which the latter never exceeds one is a run in which the rule never bound. Should no candidate pass, which the grid can produce at a tangential crossing, the state is flagged and the global maximiser of $\int_l^{\tau}\hat{z}$ over the endpoints and the downward crossings is returned, so that the recursion is not stopped by one state. When a feasibility mask excludes part of $\mathcal{T}$ (\cref{\refsec:PEE}), everything above is applied to the feasible sub-grid, with $l$ and $u$ replaced by its endpoints.

In the solvers of this model the formal retirees' shares are the state frozen per candidate, while the informal savings ratio and, under CRRA, the level of savings are grid states, constant along the tax grid within a column.

\smalltitle{Root problems that are not maximisations.}
The state fixed points of \cref{\refsec:PEELOG} and \cref{\refsec:PEE} are self-consistency conditions in which \emph{any} crossing is a solution; for those, neither the equilibrium test nor the selection above is applied, and where several crossings exist the lowest is taken and the count reported.

\smalltitle{Two implementation conventions.}
Convergence of a root found by a library routine is judged on its residual against an explicit tolerance, not on the routine's success flag. And since at a corner $z\neq 0$ is the correct answer, residual diagnostics of $z$ are restricted to interior selections.
