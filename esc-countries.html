C7. The UK and France calibrated in full

Besides the U.S., the UK and France are the countries of the paper’s cross-country test with the household data to calibrate the model in full: France in appendix F of the paper, the UK in B6. Each is calibrated under log preferences to its own pension spending, demography and design, with its own propensities to vote, the UK’s households grouped at its own income cuts and France’s at the U.S. income percentiles. This deep test gives each country the U.S. cost parameter λ itself (table 4 of the paper), not the elasticity \(\varepsilon\) that the cross-country test carries across countries (appendix C.2 of the paper), recalibrates its political weight of the old under that cost and reads the design its electorate chooses for 2020. It also calibrates each country’s own λ, the value at which its electorate re-elects its observed design.

The design under the U.S. cost

Under the U.S. λ both electorates choose designs between their observed ones and the U.S. design: the UK’s well above its observed design, France’s below its own. The three designs keep their observed order, the UK’s lowest and France’s highest, with the U.S. one between them. The U.S. parameter thus reproduces the direction in which each country departs from the U.S. design and part of the distance, a smaller part for the UK than for France. The own parameters rank as the observed designs do, the UK’s below the U.S. one and France’s above it, at more than twice the U.S. value. A higher parameter, under which flat benefits lose more revenue, moves the design away from the Beveridgean corner: the U.S. λ is above the UK’s own and below France’s, and it raises the UK’s design and lowers France’s.

The UK’s cost parameter

Table C7.2 calibrates the UK’s own λ at each IES, as table 4 of the paper does for the U.S. As in the U.S., the parameter falls steeply as the IES rises; it lies well below the U.S. value at the two lower elasticities and slightly above it at the highest. Under log preferences the UK’s observed design, much flatter than the U.S. one, loses about the same share of its revenue, but a flat benefit would lose far less than in the U.S.: the UK’s dispersion of relative incomes is about half the U.S. one and its cost parameter lower, although its system is slightly larger. As in the U.S., the cost raises the calibrated political weight of the old at every IES (figure B1.1).
