\section{Calibration -- the U.S., France, and the UK}\label{app:US}\setcounter{equation}{0}
This appendix documents the data and the household heterogeneity behind the calibrations of section \ref{sec:oecd}, whose parameters are in table \ref{table:US:Calib}. Each country has three income groups of men aged 25 to 60. The tables report, group by group, the population share $\gamma_i$, the taste for leisure $X_i$ and the productivity $\eta_i$ that the calibration assigns, and the propensity to vote $\mu_i$ taken from the data. Under the calibration of section \ref{sec:oecd} the taste for leisure is common across groups, so the $X_i$ row repeats one number, fixed by the country's average workweek, and the income distribution is carried by $\eta_i$ alone; the \oa{oecd-leisure} describes the alternative, with one $X_i$ per group identified from relative hours.

The parameters in the lower panel of table \ref{table:US:Calib} are common to the three countries. The discount factor $\beta$ matches the U.S.\ thirty-year gross interest factor $R_{2020} = 2.443$, the gross interest rate net of average multifactor productivity growth estimated by \textcite{mkvss08}, and is imposed on France and the UK. The Frisch elasticity is $\xi = 0.30$, as for Argentina, and the capital share $\alpha = 0.30$.\footnote{Averaging adjustments 2 and 3 of \textcite{Gollina1} gives capital income shares close to 0.3 for France and the U.S.\ and slightly lower for the UK.} Pension spending is public cash spending on old-age and survivors pensions in 2020 from the OECD Social Expenditure Database, the measure of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}. The number of workers per retiree $\nu_t$ is thirty-year gross growth of the total population in the estimates and medium-variant projections of the United Nations' World Population Prospects 2024, corrected for France as described below. The \oa{oecd-ies} reports the calibration at every intertemporal elasticity of the model's grid.

\subsection{The U.S.}
For the U.S.\ we use the CPS March Supplement 2019. The low and medium income groups are cut so that their mean incomes are half the mean and the mean, which is what identifies $\theta$ from the replacement rates at those two income levels reported in \textcite{OECDpen}; the remaining households form the high income group. The propensity to vote is from the U.S.\ Census, Voting and Registration in the Election of November 2020. Medicare is left out of spending and of $\theta$, as is public health spending in France and the UK, where it is not confined to retirees, so that spending and $\theta$ measure pensions alike in the three countries. The \oa{oecd-medicare} reports the U.S.\ under the alternative reading, in which Medicare, $2.9\%$ of GDP in the 2020 Data Book of the Medicare Payment Advisory Commission, counts among the transfers to the old as a benefit unrelated to income.\footnote{\textcite{BethencourtG08a1} treat public health care and pensions as political complements, which supports that reading.} The tax rate is then $14.7\%$, and $\theta$ falls to $0.522$, below the UK's $0.560$.

Table \ref{table:a_US:CalibUS} shows the two features that set the U.S.\ apart. Productivity rises steeply across the groups, from $0.82$ in the low group to $2.54$ in the high one, and the propensity to vote rises with income, from $0.47$ to $0.77$, so the political objective weighs high-income households well above their population share. The low group holds $58\%$ of households.

\subsection{France}
For France we use the 2018 wave of the Luxembourg Income Study. France's system is fully Bismarckian, so $\theta = 1$ whatever the income cuts, and the groups need not be cut at half-mean and mean income. We cut them at the U.S.\ percentiles instead, which puts the two countries' household types in one-to-one correspondence and makes the counterfactuals of section \ref{sec:oecd} a type-by-type replacement. The propensity to vote is from the Comparative Study of Electoral Systems.\footnote{\url{https://cses.org/}.} Since the normal retirement age of men retiring in 2020 was 64.5 years in France, against 66 in the U.S. \parencite[figure 3.7]{OECDpen}, we take France's population growth in 2020 to be
\begin{align*}
\frac{\mathrm{Population}\ 2020 \times (30-1.5)/30}{\mathrm{Population}\ 1990 + \mathrm{Population}\ 2020 \times 1.5/30},
\end{align*}
and the other dates in the same way, with a gap of 1.5 years in 1990 and 2020 and of 1 year from 2050 on, the gap between the normal retirement ages of men entering the labor market in 2020, 66 against 67.

At the same percentiles France's productivity profile is flatter than the U.S.\ one (table \ref{table:a_US:CalibFR}): it runs from $1.09$ to $2.51$, a ratio of the high to the low group of $2.29$ against $3.08$ in the U.S.\ (table \ref{table:US:Calib}). The propensity to vote is nearly flat, between $0.81$ and $0.86$. The common taste for leisure is $6.64$ against $4.16$ in the U.S., which is how the calibration accounts for France's shorter workweek.

\subsection{The UK}
For the UK we use the Harmonised British Household Panel Survey, wave 17, which refers to 2006--07 and is distributed by the UK Data Service.\footnote{Later waves were collected at least in part during the Great Recession.} Income groups are cut as for the U.S., at half-mean and mean income. The propensity to vote is from the Comparative Study of Electoral Systems.

The UK's cuts put fewer households in the low group than the U.S.\ cuts do, $29\%$ against $58\%$, and most of them in the medium group (table \ref{table:a_US:CalibUK}). The ratio of high to low productivity is close to the U.S.\ one, $3.06$ against $3.08$, and the propensity to vote lies between the other two countries, rising from $0.61$ to $0.74$. Table \ref{table:US_ESC:country} also reports the UK with its households regrouped at the U.S.\ income percentiles, by a linear fit of the UK's group means rather than a recount of the survey, which understates the UK's dispersion of relative incomes (appendix \ref{app:escResults:calibration}); the \oa{oecd-regroup} documents that grouping.

\subsection{France at the UK's income groups}\label{app:US:calibFRUK}
Since France's system is fully Bismarckian, its income groups can also be cut at the UK's own income percentiles without disturbing the identification of $\theta$, which puts France's and the UK's household types in one-to-one correspondence exactly as the U.S.\ percentiles do above. Table \ref{table:a_US:CalibFRUK} reports France's household heterogeneity on those groups; the \oa{oecd-hosts} imposes them on the UK.\footnote{Income, hours and group sizes are the same Luxembourg Income Study sample as table \ref{table:a_US:CalibFR}, regrouped. Voting propensities are only observed at the U.S.\ cuts; at the UK's cuts each group carries the population-weighted average of the observed values it overlaps with.} At the UK's cuts France is the more unequal of the two: the productivity of its top group relative to its bottom group is about $3.3$ against the UK's $3.06$, whereas at the U.S.\ cuts France is well below the U.S.\ (table \ref{table:US:Calib}). The regrouping moves none of France's aggregates: its political weight of the old and its tax rate are those of table \ref{table:US:Calib}.
