\section{Endogenous Pension Design}\label{sec:esc}
Up to this point the electorate chooses the size of the pension system while its design is calibrated. In this section it chooses the Bismarckian index $\theta$ as well as the tax rate, with the same political objective $\mathcal{W}_t$, in the economy of section \ref{sec:oecd} without informal households. Appendix \ref{app:esc} holds the derivations, appendix \ref{app:escResults} further results, and the technical documentation the algorithms \parencite{BergGE26doc}.

\subsection{The deadweight cost of redistribution}\label{sec:esc:cost}
Left to choose the design in the model as it stands, the electorate chooses flat benefits. For a given revenue, flattening benefits shifts income from retirees who earned more than the average when young to those who earned less, and the political objective, which weighs retirees by their marginal utility, counts the gains of the poorer for more than the losses of the richer. Proposition \ref{prop:esc:corner} in appendix \ref{app:esc:corner} makes the argument exact: as long as the rich are not sufficiently more likely to vote than the poor, an electorate that sets the design and the tax at the same time picks the Beveridgean corner, $\theta_t = 0$, whatever the tax rate, the demography and the income distribution. In the U.S.\ the rich do vote more often, which could in principle reverse the result, but at the U.S.\ calibration each timing of the choice that we examine still ends at a corner; this also holds if we let the electorate choose pension designs in advance or permanently (appendix \ref{app:esc:corner}). 

The model does contain a cost of flat benefits. A flat benefit does not reward an extra hour of work, and at the Beveridgean corner the workweek is about two hours shorter than at the observed U.S.\ design (section \ref{sec:oecd}). When the design is chosen ahead of the benefits it governs, the electorate weighs this cost, and retirees, whose benefits are levied on current labor, favor an earnings-related design because of it. At U.S.\ inequality the cost is too weak to hold the electorate back. What the model misses is a mechanism through which flat benefits cost more than earnings-related ones on margins other than hours.

The empirical literature points to such margins. \textcite{Disney04a1} splits public pension contributions in OECD countries into a part matched by benefit entitlements and a part that is not, and finds that the second lowers the labor force participation of women while the first raises it. \textcite{KumlerVF20a1} find that after Mexico's 1997 pension reform tied benefits more closely to the wages reported to the social security agency, the under-reporting of wages fell among the younger workers the reform affected. Benefits that are loosely tied to contributions thus erode the base on which contributions are levied, through participation in the formal labor market and through the reporting of earnings, two margins the model does not contain.


\textcite{Summers89a1} explains why the link matters. The part of a payroll contribution that buys the contributor a benefit is closer to deferred pay than to a tax, while the part that buys nothing distorts like a tax. Specifically, a retiree of type $i$ receives $\nu_t w_t h_t\tau_t\left[\theta_t y_i + 1-\theta_t\right]$ on past earnings $w_{t-1}\eta_i h_{t-1,i} = w_{t-1}y_i h_{t-1}$, that is
\begin{align*}
    \Pi_t\,\tau_t\left[\theta_t + \frac{1-\theta_t}{y_i}\right] \text{ per unit of past earnings}, \qquad \Pi_t \equiv \frac{\nu_t w_t h_t}{w_{t-1}h_{t-1}},
\end{align*}
where $\Pi_t$ is the pay-as-you-go return factor: a purely earnings-related system paying the same revenue returns $\Pi_t\tau_t$ per unit of earnings to every type. The flat component moves type $i$ away from that benchmark by
\begin{align}\label{eq:esc:implicitTax}
    t_i \equiv \Pi_t\,\tau_t\,(1-\theta_t)\left(1-\frac{1}{y_i}\right)
\end{align}
per unit of earnings. 

We do not model these extensive participation and reporting margins explicitly. Instead, we borrow a principle from the literature on the deadweight cost of taxation, that the loss a tax causes rises with the square of its rate \parencite{Harberger64a1}. Applied to the implicit tax of each type, we model this by taking a loss out of the revenue that reaches retirees, so that the budget becomes (see appendix \ref{app:esc:cost} for the derivation)
\begin{align}\label{eq:esc:budget}
    \nu_t w_t\tau_th_t\, f(\theta_t,\tau_t) &= \sum_i \gamma_i b_t^i, \\
    f(\theta_t,\tau_t) &= \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\lambda\,\tau_t\,\tilde{V}\,(1-\theta_t)^2\right), \qquad \tilde{V} \equiv \sum_i\gamma_i\frac{(y_i-1)^2}{y_i}, \nonumber
\end{align}
where $\lambda \geq 0$ is a cost parameter to be calibrated, $1-f$ the share of revenue lost and $\tilde{V}$ the dispersion of relative incomes.\footnote{The exponential keeps $f$ between zero and one for any size of the system and gives back the model without the cost at $\lambda = 0$; to first order, $1-f$ is the quadratic loss itself.} We see this form as a reasonable place to start rather than as an exact account of participation and reporting: it has the properties that the principles above suggest, and it leaves the size of the loss to the calibration. The loss vanishes when the system redistributes nothing, at $\theta_t = 1$ or when all incomes are equal, since flat and earnings-related benefits then pay everyone the same. Its share of revenue grows with the tax rate, so each unit of redistribution costs more in a larger system. And since the loss is quadratic in the flat share, the first unit of redistribution costs nothing at the margin, and the cost alone cannot take an electorate to a fully Bismarckian system.

The electorate chooses the design one period ahead: at $t$ it sets $\theta_{t+1}$, which governs the benefits of the current young. A design chosen together with the tax would only re-split benefits that current retirees have already earned, and so would move no decision, while a design chosen once and for all would require a commitment that the Markov equilibria of section \ref{sec:model} rule out. Reforms of benefit formulas, moreover, usually apply to benefits not yet in payment. Formally, the cost only replaces $\theta_t$ and $1-\theta_t$ by $f\theta_t$ and $f(1-\theta_t)$ in the equilibrium of the previous sections (appendix \ref{app:esc:equilibrium}). Since it scales both components of the benefit alike, it cancels from the ratio of replacement rates that identifies the design in section \ref{sec:oecd}, and the observed U.S.\ design is still $\theta^{\ast} = 0.738$. In the tax condition it adds one factor: a larger system loses a larger share of its revenue, so a retiree gains less from a higher tax than the revenue it raises. And a more earnings-related design now also enlarges the benefits the young will receive, because less of the revenue that pays them is lost; this gain is what can stop the electorate short of the Beveridgean corner. Under log preferences, the design chosen at $t$ depends neither on the design inherited nor on savings (proposition \ref{prop:esc:separability} in appendix \ref{app:esc:log}), so each date's choice is a search over designs without a state (section \ref{sec:numerical}).

\subsection{Calibration and the determinants of the design}\label{sec:esc:calibration}\label{sec:esc:results}
\smalltitle{Calibration.}
We calibrate the cost parameter so that the electorate re-elects the observed design. Concretely, the target is $\theta_{2020}$, set by the electorate of 1990 on an equilibrium path along which every design from the first period on is chosen, and $\beta$ and $\omega$ are recalibrated at every trial value of $\lambda$, so that the targets of section \ref{sec:oecd} still hold with the cost in place. Only the design of 2020 is targeted; every other design along the path is an outcome of the model. 

Table \ref{table:US_ESC:calibration} reports the calibration at each intertemporal elasticity. The table shows that at the calibrated design, the loss amounts to a relatively small share of revenue, no more than 4\% ($1-f(\theta^{\ast})$). However, the calibrated cost parameter $\lambda$ varies by roughly an order of magnitude across the reported range, and so does the share of revenue that a completely flat benefit would lose ($1-f(0)$). The cost parameter falls as the elasticity rises, because a higher elasticity strengthens the forward-looking stakes in an earnings-related design, and less cost is needed to keep the electorate at the observed level. The parameter calibrated on the U.S.\ accounts for much of the UK's distance from the U.S.\ but none of France's, whose corner no value of the parameter reproduces (see appendix \ref{app:escResults:calibration}).

First, the results from section \ref{sec:oecd} about the determinants of the size of the pension system, savings and labor supply remain mostly unchanged. The biggest change is that ageing with an endogenous design now crowds out private savings more, in particular for low values of $\rho$. Second, the panel on the pension design shows that ageing consistently ties benefits more closely to earnings, that the French voting profile moves the design towards more flat benefits, and that a more equal income distribution moves the design little except at $\rho = 2$.

Ageing consistently ties benefits more closely to earnings through two forces. First, ageing gives retirees more weight in the political objective, and retirees prefer a design that raises current labor supply, on which their benefits are levied, which is the more earnings-related one. Second, the higher tax rate associated with ageing raises the loss on every unit of redistribution, since the share of revenue lost grows with the size of the system. Thus, the higher tax increases the incentive to make pension benefits more earnings-related, consistent with the evidence from figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}.\footnote{This last channel supplies about half of the response: under a cost attached to the design itself, which does not grow with the size of the system, the design rises only to $0.778$, see \oa{esc-scale}.}

A more equal income distribution moves the design through opposing forces, whose balance depends on the intertemporal elasticity of substitution. On one hand, compressing incomes shrinks the gain that poorer young voters expect from flat benefits in their own retirement, while the stake in an earnings-related design that works through labor supply does not depend on the dispersion of incomes, which pushes the design towards more Bismarckian benefits. On the other hand, compressing incomes makes flat benefits cheaper, since the share of revenue lost scales with the dispersion of relative incomes, which France's distribution halves, from $0.436$ to $0.218$; this pushes the design towards more Beveridgean benefits. The second force is strongest at low elasticities, where the calibrated cost parameter is largest (table \ref{table:US_ESC:calibration}), and the first at high elasticities, where the stake through labor supply is strongest. A more equal distribution therefore lowers the design slightly at $\rho = 0.5$, raises it slightly at $\rho = 1$, and raises it by much more at $\rho = 2$.


We return to figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}, in which pension spending and the Bismarckian index both rise as population growth falls and neither is related to income inequality. With the design given, section \ref{sec:oecd} accounts for the pattern in spending. With the design chosen as well, one political process accounts for both: ageing raises the tax rate and ties benefits more closely to earnings at every intertemporal elasticity, while the income distribution moves the tax rate little and moves the design in a direction that depends on the elasticity, which leaves no systematic relation between inequality and the design.
