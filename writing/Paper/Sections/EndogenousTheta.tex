\section{Endogenous Pension Design}\label{sec:esc}
Up to this point the electorate chooses the size of the pension system while its design is calibrated. In this section it chooses the design $\theta$ as well as the tax rate, with the same political objective $\mathcal{W}_t$, in the economy of section \ref{sec:oecd} without informal households. Appendix \ref{app:esc} holds the derivations, appendix \ref{app:escResults} further results, appendix \ref{app:oecd26} the data and the robustness of the cross-country test that closes the section, and the technical documentation the algorithms \parencite{BergGE26doc}.

\subsection{The deadweight cost of redistribution}\label{sec:esc:cost}
Left to choose the design in the model as it stands, the electorate chooses flat benefits. For a given revenue, flattening benefits shifts income from retirees who earned more than the average when young to those who earned less, and the political objective, which weighs retirees by their marginal utility, counts the gains of the poorer for more than the losses of the richer. Proposition \ref{prop:esc:corner} in appendix \ref{app:esc:corner} makes the argument exact: if the rich are no more likely to vote than the poor, an electorate that sets the design and the tax at the same time picks the Beveridgean corner, $\theta_t = 0$, whatever the tax rate, the demography and the income distribution. The same appendix extends the result to propensities to vote that rise with income by proportionally less than consumption in retirement. In the U.S.\ the rich do vote more often, which could in principle reverse the result, but at the U.S.\ calibration the electorate still picks the Beveridgean corner at every intertemporal elasticity we check, whether it sets the design together with the tax or one period ahead of the benefits it governs (appendix \ref{app:esc:corner}). A design chosen once and for all, an exercise outside the Markov equilibria of section \ref{sec:model}, is also a corner: the Beveridgean one under log preferences and the Bismarckian one at higher intertemporal elasticities.

The model does contain a cost of flat benefits. A flat benefit does not reward an extra hour of work, and at the Beveridgean corner the workweek is about 1.7 hours shorter than at the observed U.S.\ design (section \ref{sec:oecd}). When the design is chosen ahead of the benefits it governs, the electorate weighs this cost, and at a given anticipated tax retirees, whose benefits are levied on current labor, favor an earnings-related design because of it. At U.S.\ inequality the cost is too weak to hold the electorate back. What the model misses is a mechanism through which flat benefits cost more than earnings-related ones on margins other than hours.

The empirical literature points to such margins. \textcite{Disney04a1} splits public pension contributions in OECD countries into a part matched by benefit entitlements and a part that is not, and finds that the second lowers the labor force participation of women while the first raises it. \textcite{KumlerVF20a1} find that after Mexico's 1997 pension reform tied benefits more closely to the wages reported to the social security agency, the under-reporting of wages fell among the younger workers the reform affected. Benefits that are loosely tied to contributions thus erode the base on which contributions are levied, through participation in the formal labor market and through the reporting of earnings. The model contains neither margin; the cost we introduce below stands for the first (appendix \ref{app:esc:cost}).


\textcite{Summers89a1} explains why the link matters. The part of a payroll contribution that buys the contributor a benefit is closer to deferred pay than to a tax, while the part that buys nothing distorts like a tax. Specifically, with $y_i \equiv \eta_i h_{t-1,i}/h_{t-1}$ the earnings of type $i$ relative to the mean, constant over time by part ii of proposition \ref{prop:LOG:EE}, a retiree of type $i$ receives $\nu_t w_t h_t\tau_t\left[\theta_t y_i + 1-\theta_t\right]$ on past earnings $w_{t-1}y_i h_{t-1}$, that is
\begin{align*}
    \Pi_t\,\tau_t\left[\theta_t + \frac{1-\theta_t}{y_i}\right] \text{ per unit of past earnings}, \qquad \Pi_t \equiv \frac{\nu_t w_t h_t}{w_{t-1}h_{t-1}},
\end{align*}
where $\Pi_t$ is the pay-as-you-go return factor: a purely earnings-related system paying the same revenue returns $\Pi_t\tau_t$ per unit of earnings to every type. The flat component moves type $i$ away from that benchmark by
\begin{align}\label{eq:esc:implicitTax}
    t_i \equiv \Pi_t\,\tau_t\,(1-\theta_t)\left(1-\frac{1}{y_i}\right)
\end{align}
per unit of earnings. 

We do not model participation and formality explicitly. Instead, we borrow a principle from the literature on the deadweight cost of taxation, that the excess burden of a tax rises with the square of its rate \parencite{Harberger64a1}. Applied to the implicit tax of each type, it gives a loss that we take out of the revenue that reaches retirees, as a revenue-equivalent excess burden,\footnote{The implicit taxes are a pure transfer in the aggregate, $\sum_i\gamma_i y_i t_i = 0$, so with a common elasticity the first-order responses of the contribution bases of taxed and subsidized types cancel in revenue. What remains is the excess burden, a welfare cost that subsidized types bear as well as taxed ones; charging it against the revenue that reaches retirees is a choice of reduced form rather than a consequence of the argument.} so that the budget becomes (see appendix \ref{app:esc:cost} for the derivation)
\begin{align}\label{eq:esc:budget}
    \nu_t w_t\tau_th_t\, f(\theta_t,\tau_t) &= \sum_i \gamma_i b_t^i, \\
    f(\theta_t,\tau_t) &= \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\lambda\,\tau_t\,\tilde{V}\,(1-\theta_t)^2\right), \qquad \tilde{V} \equiv \sum_i\gamma_i\frac{(y_i-1)^2}{y_i}, \nonumber
\end{align}
where $\lambda \geq 0$ is a cost parameter to be calibrated, $1-f$ the share of revenue lost and $\tilde{V}$ the dispersion of relative incomes.\footnote{The exponential keeps $f$ between zero and one for any size of the system and gives back the model without the cost at $\lambda = 0$; to first order, $1-f$ is the quadratic loss itself.} We see this form as a reasonable place to start rather than as an exact account of participation and formality: it has the properties that the principles above suggest, and it leaves the size of the loss to the calibration. The loss vanishes when the system redistributes nothing, at $\theta_t = 1$ or when all incomes are equal, since flat and earnings-related benefits then pay everyone the same. Its share of revenue grows with the tax rate, so each unit of redistribution costs more in a larger system. And since the loss is quadratic in the flat share, the first unit of redistribution costs nothing at the margin: under log preferences the slope of the political objective at the Bismarckian corner, and the tax chosen there, are the same at every value of $\lambda$, so an electorate whose objective falls towards that corner without the cost does not choose it at any value of $\lambda$ (appendix \ref{app:esc:cost}).

The electorate chooses the design one period ahead: at $t$ it sets $\theta_{t+1}$, which governs the benefits of the current young, and the loss that the design causes is taken out of those benefits.\footnote{The participation and formality responses that the loss stands for occur earlier, when the young work, and erode the base that pays the retirees of that period. Booked there, the loss of a design would fall on current retirees rather than on the cohort whose benefits it governs, and each period's budget would depend on the design and the tax of the next period. We book it in the period of the benefits, where the implicit tax \eqref{eq:esc:implicitTax} falls, so that each budget depends on the policy of its own period. The choice bears on the weight the political objective gives the loss, since current retirees and the young enter it with different weights.} A design chosen together with the tax would only re-split benefits that current retirees have already earned, and so would move no decision, while a design chosen once and for all would require a commitment that the Markov equilibria of section \ref{sec:model} rule out. Reforms of benefit formulas, moreover, usually apply to benefits not yet in payment. Formally, the cost only replaces $\theta_t$ and $1-\theta_t$ by $f\theta_t$ and $f(1-\theta_t)$ in the equilibrium of the previous sections (appendix \ref{app:esc:equilibrium}). Since it scales both components of the benefit alike, it cancels from the ratio of replacement rates that identifies the design in section \ref{sec:oecd}, and the observed U.S.\ design is still $\theta^{\ast} = 0.728$. In the tax condition it adds one factor: a larger system loses a larger share of its revenue, so a retiree gains less from a higher tax than the revenue it raises. And at a given anticipated tax, a more earnings-related design now also enlarges the benefits the young will receive, because less of the revenue that pays them is lost; this gain is what can stop the electorate short of the Beveridgean corner.\footnote{The anticipated tax responds to the design as well, and the equilibrium accounts for the response (appendix \ref{app:esc:equilibrium}). At the U.S.\ choice under log preferences it adds to the gain: of what offsets the pull of the design towards flat benefits at a given loss share and tax, the saving on the loss supplies about 70\% and the response of the anticipated tax about 30\%.} Under log preferences, the design chosen at $t$ depends neither on the design inherited nor on savings (proposition \ref{prop:esc:separability} in appendix \ref{app:esc:log}), so each date's choice is a search over designs without a state (section \ref{sec:numerical}).

\subsection{Calibration and the determinants of the design}\label{sec:esc:calibration}\label{sec:esc:results}
\smalltitle{Calibration.}
We calibrate the cost parameter so that the electorate re-elects the observed design. Concretely, the target is $\theta_{2020}$, set by the electorate of 1990 on an equilibrium path along which every design from the first period on is chosen, and $\beta$ and $\omega$ are recalibrated at every trial value of $\lambda$, so that the targets of section \ref{sec:oecd} still hold with the cost in place. Only the design of 2020 is targeted; every other design along the path is an outcome of the model. 

Table \ref{table:US_ESC:calibration} reports the calibration at each intertemporal elasticity. At the calibrated design the loss is a small share of revenue, at most $4.2\%$ ($1-f(\theta^{\ast})$), because it is quadratic in the flat share and the observed design is close to the Bismarckian corner. What the calibration pins down is the cost at the margin, and there it bites harder: at the tax rate of 2020, a design $0.1$ flatter would lose $7.7\%$ of revenue at $\rho = 0.5$, $3.5\%$ at $\rho = 1$ and $0.8\%$ at $\rho = 2$. Across the three elasticities the calibrated cost parameter $\lambda$ varies by a factor of about nine and a half, and the share of revenue that a completely flat benefit would lose ($1-f(0)$) by a factor of about seven, since the share lost rises less than in proportion to $\lambda$. The cost parameter falls as the elasticity rises, because a higher elasticity strengthens the forward-looking stakes in an earnings-related design, and less cost is needed to keep the electorate at the observed level. At every elasticity $\lambda$ lies more than an order of magnitude above what participation and taxable-income elasticities alone would imply, so the cost also stands in for administrative, evasion and political costs of redistribution that the model does not contain (appendix \ref{app:esc:cost}). Section \ref{sec:esc:deepTest} takes the parameter calibrated on the U.S.\ to the UK and France.

\smalltitle{Determinants of the pension design.}
Figure \ref{fig:US_ESC:overview} takes the counterfactuals of section \ref{sec:oecd} to the economy with the endogenous pension design: acute ageing and France's income distribution, voting patterns and taste for leisure, each imposed on the U.S.\ calibration over the whole horizon and read in 2020. Every counterfactual is read twice, with the design pinned at $\theta^{\ast}$ and with the design chosen. Because the cost is present in both, the pinned reading departs from figure \ref{fig:US:overview}. Ageing still moves the tax rate several times more than any other characteristic,\footnote{With the design pinned at $\rho = 1$, acute ageing raises the tax rate by $7.8$ p.p.\ rather than $8.7$ p.p., since the cost makes a larger system worth less to retirees at the margin.} but France's income distribution lowers it by only $0.7$ p.p.\ at $\rho = 0.5$ and $1$, about half as much as there, and at $\rho = 1$ by less than France's voting profile raises it.

First, the results from section \ref{sec:oecd} about the determinants of the size of the pension system, savings and labor supply remain mostly unchanged. Ageing, however, crowds out private savings more with the design chosen than with it pinned, in particular for low values of $\rho$. Second, the panel on the pension design shows that ageing consistently ties benefits more closely to earnings, that the French voting profile moves the design towards more flat benefits, and that a more equal income distribution moves the design little except at $\rho = 2$.

Ageing consistently ties benefits more closely to earnings through two forces. First, ageing gives retirees more weight in the political objective, and at a given anticipated tax retirees prefer a design that raises current labor supply, on which their benefits are levied, which is the more earnings-related one. Second, the higher tax rate associated with ageing raises the loss on every unit of redistribution, since the share of revenue lost grows with the size of the system. Thus, the higher tax increases the incentive to make pension benefits more earnings-related, consistent with the evidence from figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}.

How much of the response the second force supplies depends on the form of the cost. The cost parameter absorbs the ratio of the pay-as-you-go return factor to the market return, $\Pi_t/R_t$, which falls as the population ages (appendix \ref{app:esc:cost}). A cost that keeps this ratio grows less with ageing, and under it acute ageing raises the design by about 70\% as much as under the cost \eqref{eq:esc:budget}, at each of the three elasticities. A cost attached to the design itself (\oa{esc-costs}) does not grow with the size of the system at all. Under it acute ageing raises the design by $0.035$ at $\rho = 1$, against $0.109$, so the size of the system accounts for about two thirds of the response there, for more at $\rho = 0.5$ and for about a third at $\rho = 2$. Under all three costs, mild and acute ageing make the design more Bismarckian at every elasticity.

A more equal income distribution moves the design through three forces, whose balance depends on the intertemporal elasticity of substitution. First, compressing incomes shrinks the gain that poorer young voters expect from flat benefits in their own retirement, while the stake in an earnings-related design that works through labor supply does not depend on the dispersion of incomes, which pushes the design towards more Bismarckian benefits. Second, compressing incomes makes flat benefits cheaper, since the share of revenue lost scales with the dispersion of relative incomes, which France's distribution halves, from $0.436$ to $0.218$. Third, it lowers the tax rate, and with it the share of revenue that every unit of redistribution loses. The second and third forces push the design towards more Beveridgean benefits and are strongest at low elasticities, where the calibrated cost parameter is largest (table \ref{table:US_ESC:calibration}), and the first is strongest at high elasticities, where the stake through labor supply is strongest. A more equal distribution therefore lowers the design slightly at $\rho = 0.5$, where the first two forces nearly offset each other and the third supplies most of the fall, raises it slightly at $\rho = 1$, and raises it by much more at $\rho = 2$.\footnote{Imposing France's distribution in three steps separates the forces: first everywhere but in the cost, whose share of revenue lost at each design is held at its U.S.\ value, then on the dispersion of relative incomes in the cost, and last on the tax rate in the cost. The first step raises the design by $0.128$, $0.149$ and $0.216$ at $\rho = 0.5$, $1$ and $2$. The second lowers it by $0.130$, $0.113$ and $0.031$, and the third by $0.017$, $0.015$ and $0.003$. The split depends on the order of the steps: imposed alone, France's dispersion in the cost lowers the U.S.\ design by $0.192$ at $\rho = 1$.}


\subsection{The UK and France}\label{sec:esc:deepTest}
We test the calibrated cost in two ways: deeply, on the two countries calibrated as fully as the U.S., and broadly, on 26 OECD countries (section \ref{sec:esc:crossSection}). In the deep test the UK and France are calibrated at $\rho = 1$ as the U.S.\ is, each to its own pension spending, demography and design and with its own propensities to vote, the UK's households grouped at its own income cuts and France's at the U.S.\ income percentiles (appendix \ref{app:US}, table \ref{table:US:Calib}). Each takes the cost from the U.S.\ in one of two forms. Carried as $\lambda = 11.345$ (table \ref{table:US_ESC:calibration}), the cost parameter is the same in every economy. Carried as $\varepsilon = 20.445$, the elasticity of the Harberger loss in appendix \ref{app:esc:cost} (not the universal pension $\epsilon$ of section \ref{sec:model}), it is the parameter of the cost that keeps the ratio $\Pi_t/R_t$ (section \ref{sec:esc:results}) and gives each economy $\lambda_{i,t} = \varepsilon\,\Pi_{i,t}/R_{i,t}$ at its own ratio, so that at the U.S.\ ratio of 2020 the two forms are the same cost. The cross-section carries the second, and we read the deep test under it, with the first beside.

Table \ref{table:US_ESC:country} reports the designs the two electorates choose for 2020. Under $\varepsilon$ the UK, observed at $0.560$, chooses $0.673$, and France, observed at the Bismarckian corner, chooses $0.743$; under $\lambda$ they choose $0.690$ and $0.795$. Either way the three designs are ordered as observed, with the U.S.\ at $0.728$ between them. Under $\varepsilon$ the U.S.\ parameter accounts for a third of the distance from the U.S.\ design to the UK's and for little of the distance to France's, against about a quarter of each under $\lambda$. Both countries have a lower ratio $\Pi/R$ in 2020 than the U.S., $0.529$ for the UK and $0.432$ for France against $0.555$ for the U.S., so the U.S.\ $\varepsilon$ charges them less than the U.S.\ $\lambda$ does, and both choose flatter designs under it, which brings the UK closer to its observed design and takes France further from its own. The UK's own parameter, which re-elects its observed design, is $7.740$ as $\lambda$, a third below the U.S.\ value, and $14.655$ as $\varepsilon$, a little more than a quarter below; table \ref{table:UK_ESC:calibration} in appendix \ref{app:escResults:calibration} reports $\lambda$ at the other elasticities. No value of either parameter makes France re-elect its corner (appendix \ref{app:esc:cost}).

The UK also shows what the parameters held common in the cross-section do to a single country. Measured from the OECD earnings data at the U.S.\ income percentiles, with the U.S.\ propensities to vote and with its political weight of the old calibrated to its spending as in the deep test, the UK chooses $0.791$ under the U.S.\ $\varepsilon$ in the second layer of the cross-section, above the U.S.\ design of $0.735$ at those data, where the deep test places it below the U.S.\ one (table \ref{table:OECD26:reconcile}). At its own propensities to vote, which rise less with income than the U.S.\ ones, the cross-section places it below the U.S.\ as well (\oa{esc-oecd26-turnout}), and under $\lambda$ the two tests place it on the same sides as under $\varepsilon$, at $0.690$ and $0.805$. For the UK the propensities to vote thus decide on which side of the U.S.\ design its predicted design falls, and the transport of the cost does not.


\subsection{The OECD cross-section: the design}\label{sec:esc:crossSection}
We return to figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}, in which pension spending and the Bismarckian index both rise as population growth falls and neither is related to income inequality. Section \ref{sec:oecd} takes the model to spending in 26 of its countries with the design given; with the design chosen as well, whether the political process of section \ref{sec:esc:results} reproduces both relations is a quantitative question.

The index of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata} and the design are two scales for one object. A retiree's replacement rate at relative income $y$ is $\Pi_t\tau_t\left[\theta_t + (1-\theta_t)/y\right]$ (section \ref{sec:esc:cost}), so the ratio of the replacement rates at mean and at half mean earnings is $I = 1/(2-\theta_t)$. It runs from $1/2$ at the Beveridgean corner to $1$ at the Bismarckian one, and neither the size of the system nor the cost enters it. The two scales agree only at the corners: a unit of $\theta$ is worth $I^2$ units of the index, about $0.6$ at the U.S.\ design. On the index, acute ageing raises the U.S.\ design by $0.07$ to $0.08$ across the three elasticities, and the projected demography raises it by about $0.06$ from 2020 to 2110 (appendix \ref{app:escResults:path}), against a cross-section that spans $0.50$ to $1$.

We take the model to the same 26 countries. Each enters with its own workers per retiree, income distribution and observed design, measured in the same way for every country, the U.S.\ included (appendix \ref{app:oecd26}). For each country the model predicts the tax rate of 2020 at the observed design and the design in force in 2020, chosen by the electorate of 1990, which is the design that the calibration of $\varepsilon$ targets.\footnote{Under log preferences the design chosen depends neither on the inherited design nor on savings (proposition \ref{prop:esc:separability}), so the observed design enters the test only through the tax rate. The design chosen in 2020, which binds from 2050, lies one period further along each country's path.} In the first layer of the test every country takes the U.S.\ political weight of the old; in the second its weight is calibrated to its pension spending, which the model then matches. Everything else is held at its U.S.\ value.

The test is therefore a conditional one. Its slopes are what demography and the income distribution alone produce at a common cost of redistribution, common preferences and common propensities to vote, and the UK shows that the parameters held common can move a country's design to the other side of the U.S.\ one (section \ref{sec:esc:deepTest}). The cost is carried as $\varepsilon$ rather than $\lambda$ because $\lambda$ absorbs the ratio $\Pi/R$, which falls as the population ages, so that a $\lambda$ held common would amount to a larger $\varepsilon$ in older countries and tie a held parameter to the regressor of the test. Data and model are regressed on the variables of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}, with the model's design converted to the index.

Figure \ref{fig:OECD26:layer1} sets the first layer beside the data, and table \ref{table:OECD26:slopes} reports the slopes of both layers. The model reproduces the sign of the three relations that are significant in the data. Spending, evaluated at the observed designs, falls with population growth as it does without the cost (figure \ref{fig:OECD26:spending}), here at a slope of $-16.75$ against $-12.84$ in the data. The index falls with population growth too, about half as steeply as in the data, $-0.23$ against $-0.45$. Spending therefore rises with the index, as in the data, although at a given demography a more Bismarckian design lowers the tax: in the model the relation runs through demography, which moves the size of the system and its design together. Its slope is $2.6$ times that of the data, since in either layer the model's designs vary much less than the observed ones: in the second layer they run from $0.618$ in Ireland to $0.924$ in Finland (table \ref{table:OECD26:omega}), and the first layer's over a somewhat wider range (figure \ref{fig:OECD26:layer1}), while in the data two countries sit at the Beveridgean corner and six at the Bismarckian one, a corner the cost cannot by itself deliver (section \ref{sec:esc:cost}). The second layer keeps the signs of the first, with the index falling with population growth at $-0.15$ and spending rising with the index, both significantly. The model thus reproduces the joint rise of spending and the earnings link as populations age in sign, with half the data's response of the index to population growth in the first layer and less dispersion in the design than the data show.

Inequality is where the model and the data part, and where the test depends most on what it holds common. In the data neither spending nor the index is related to the Gini, and in the model spending is not related to it either. At the U.S.\ propensities to vote, however, the model's index falls with the Gini in the first layer, $-0.67$ against $0.89$ in the data, and significantly: at the U.S.\ political weight, cost and propensities to vote and at an elasticity of one, a more unequal country chooses a less Bismarckian design, just as France's more equal distribution raises the U.S.\ design at $\rho = 1$ in figure \ref{fig:US_ESC:overview}. In the second layer the relation is $-0.24$ and not significant. At each country's own propensities to vote it is about zero in the first layer, $0.06$, and takes the data's sign in the second, $0.40$, though not significantly (table \ref{table:OECD26:robustness} in appendix \ref{app:oecd26}). The model's relation between the design and inequality is thus conditional on the propensities to vote that the test imposes, while its relation with population growth holds at either.

Appendix \ref{app:oecd26} varies the parameters that the test holds common, the transport of the cost, the rule for $\beta$ and the propensities to vote (table \ref{table:OECD26:robustness}). In every variant, and on all 29 countries of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata} (\oa{esc-oecd26-all}), the index falls with population growth and spending rises with the index, as in the data, while the relation between the design and inequality depends on the propensities to vote.
