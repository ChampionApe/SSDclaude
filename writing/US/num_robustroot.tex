\subsection{Robust policy search with bounds}\label{\refsec:RobustRoot}
The politico-economic equilibria below are identified from first order conditions rather than by maximising political objectives directly. This is paramount to keeping the state space small --- it is what allows the closed-form reduction of the predetermined savings shares to do its work --- but it converts a maximisation problem into a root problem, and a root problem inherits none of the safeguards of the maximisation it came from: a root may be a minimum, there may be several, and the optimum may sit at a corner where no root exists at all. This subsection collects, once, the machinery that restores those safeguards. It is stated for a generic first order condition and is implemented as a standalone package (\texttt{python/gridsearch} in the repository); the model enters only through the function being evaluated.

Throughout, let $f(\tau)$ denote a first order condition --- in our applications the derivative of a political objective $\mathcal{W}$ with respect to the tax rate --- that it is safe to evaluate on an interval $[l,u]$ with $0\leq l < u < 1$, and let $z(\tau) = f\left([\tau]_{[l,u]}\right)$ denote its bounded evaluation, where $[\tau]_{[l,u]} \equiv \min(u, \max(l,\tau))$.

\smalltitle{A bounded-root reparameterization.}
Gradient-based solvers are efficient when they apply, but they search all of $\mathbb{R}$ and $f$ is only meaningful on $[l,u]$. Instead of solving $f(\tau) = 0$ subject to bounds, we solve the unconstrained auxiliary problem
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:root}
    h(z,\tau) = z - |z| \underbrace{\left[a_0\min\left(\tau - l,0\right) + a_1\max\left(\tau - u, 0\right)\right]}_{\equiv g(\tau)} = 0,
\end{align}
where $a_0, a_1>0$ are technical parameters. This gives three regions:
\begin{align*}
    h\left(z,\tau\right) = \left\lbrace \begin{array}{ll} f(l)-|f(l)| a_0 (\tau - l), & \tau<l \\
    f(\tau), & \tau\in[l,u] \\
    f(u)-|f(u)|a_1(\tau-u), & \tau>u\end{array}\right., && \dfrac{\partial h}{\partial \tau} = \left\lbrace \begin{array}{ll} -|f(l)|a_0, & \tau<l \\
    \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial \tau}, & \tau\in[l,u] \\
    -|f(u)|a_1, & \tau>u\end{array}\right.
\end{align*}
$f$ is never evaluated outside $[l,u]$, yet $h$ is defined everywhere --- affine with a weakly negative slope outside the interval --- so an unconstrained solver can search freely. If $f$ identifies an interior optimum, solving $h\left(z(\tilde{\tau}),\tilde{\tau}\right) = 0$ and reporting the bounded solution $\tau = [\tilde{\tau}]_{[l,u]}$ recovers it.

The construction also detects corners. Suppose $f(\tau)>0$ throughout $[l,u]$ with $\partial f/\partial\tau <0$: a gradient-based algorithm started in the interior finds no root there, but for $\tau\geq u$ the auxiliary problem still has one, at $\tilde{\tau} = u+1/a_1$, which bounds to the corner solution $\tau = u$. Symmetrically, $f(\tau)<0$ throughout with $\partial f/\partial \tau>0$ yields $\tilde{\tau} = l-1/a_0$ and thus $\tau = l$.

\smalltitle{The grid, and the extended grid.}
When $f$ is not known to be well behaved --- and a political first order condition generally is not --- we evaluate it on a grid rather than trusting a solver's path through it. Let $\mathcal{T} = \left\lbrace \tau^{(1)},...,\tau^{(M)}\right\rbrace$ with $\tau^{(1)} = l$ and $\tau^{(M)} = u$ denote a grid on the interior, and let the \emph{extended} grid $\tilde{\mathcal{T}}$ add two outer nodes
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:extendedGrid}
    \tilde{\tau}^{(0)} = l-\frac{1}{a_0}-\delta, \qquad \tilde{\tau}^{(M+1)} = u+\frac{1}{a_1}+\delta,
\end{align}
for a small $\delta>0$. The outer nodes sit at (a hair beyond) the two boundary solutions of \eqref{\refeq:root}. Evaluating $h$ at $\tilde{\tau} = l-1/a_0$ gives $h = f(l)+\left|f(l)\right|$, which is identically zero whenever $f(l)<0$; at $\tilde{\tau} = u+1/a_1$ it gives $h = f(u)-\left|f(u)\right|$, identically zero whenever $f(u)>0$. A corner solution is thus encoded as an \emph{exact} zero at an outer node rather than as a sign change, and a pure sign-change test of the form $h^{(k)}h^{(k+1)}<0$ would not detect it; the root detection must treat exact zeros as first-class citizens. The offset $\delta$ shifts the node just outside the root so that the outermost cell does exhibit a sign change; since $h$ is affine in $\tilde\tau$ outside $[l,u]$, linear interpolation on that cell returns $\tilde\tau = l-1/a_0$ (resp.\ $u+1/a_1$) exactly, and hence $\tau = l$ (resp.\ $\tau = u$) after bounding. Note that $a_0, a_1$ thereby serve double duty --- as the penalty slopes in \eqref{\refeq:root} and as the placement of the outer nodes --- and the two uses must be kept consistent.

\smalltitle{Selecting among several roots.}
Solving $z = 0$ is only a necessary condition for the political optimum: an upward crossing of $z$ is a local \emph{minimum} of $\mathcal{W}$, several downward crossings may exist, and the optimum may sit at a corner where no root exists. The selection has to respect one more thing. In every application below $z$ is evaluated with a predetermined state substituted consistently with the point of evaluation --- the savings shares $s_{t-1,i}/s_{t-1}$ at the tax that savers anticipated --- so that, writing $\mathcal{W}(\tau; D)$ for the objective at a frozen state $D$ and $D(\tau)$ for the consistent one,
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:consistentFOC}
    z(\tau) = \partial_{\tau}\mathcal{W}\left(\tau; D\right)\big|_{D = D(\tau)}.
\end{align}
A zero of $z$ is a stationary point of $\mathcal{W}(\cdot\,; D(\tau^{\star}))$, which is exactly the object wanted, and locating it from the interpolant of $z$ is legitimate: the state moving along the search is a property of the function whose root is sought, not an error in finding it. But $z$ is not the derivative of any one function of $\tau$. Its slope at a crossing, $z' = \mathcal{W}_{\tau\tau} + \mathcal{W}_{\tau D}D'$, is not the second-order condition, and its integral between two crossings, $\int z\,d\tau = \Delta\left[\mathcal{W}(\tau;D(\tau))\right] - \int \mathcal{W}_D D'\,d\tau$, is neither the objective at a frozen state nor the consistent one. An earlier version of this machinery classified and ranked candidates by the direction and the integral of $z$, and so compared candidates at different predetermined states; what follows replaces it.

\emph{Candidates.} With $\hat{z}$ the piecewise-linear interpolant of $z$ through the nodes of $\mathcal{T}$, the candidate set is
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:candidates}
    \mathcal{C} \equiv \left\lbrace l,u \right\rbrace\cup\left\lbrace \tau\in(l,u)\mbox{ }:\mbox{ }\hat{z}(\tau) = 0\right\rbrace,
\end{align}
every crossing in either direction together with the two endpoints. Each candidate $c$ comes with its own predetermined state $D_c \equiv D(\tau_c)$, the one savers would have chosen had they anticipated it.

\emph{The objective at a frozen state.} For each candidate the first order condition is re-evaluated on the whole grid with the state frozen at $D_c$, and integrated by the trapezoidal rule through the nodes,
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:objectiveProfile}
    \mathcal{V}_c(\tau) \equiv \mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{old}}\left(l; D_c\right) + \int_l^{\tau}\partial_{x}\mathcal{W}\left(x; D_c\right)dx, \qquad \tau\in\mathcal{T},
\end{align}
where $\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{old}}$ is the retirees' term, a closed form. Then $\mathcal{V}_c(\tau) = \mathcal{W}(\tau; D_c)$ up to the young's term at $l$, which does not involve the state and is the same number for every candidate, so values compare across candidates as the objectives do. The integral is now legitimate, since nothing moves along it but the tax. It is also the consistent choice of object: where the condition carries numerically smoothed derivatives (\cref{\refsec:PEE}), $\mathcal{V}_c$ carries the same ones, whereas the raw utility levels would reintroduce the kinks the smoothing removed and place their maximum a few cells away from the crossing. Nothing expensive is recomputed: the terms of $z$ that do not involve the predetermined state --- the young's, and every numerical derivative --- are already on the grid, and the retirees' term is a closed form in $(\tau, D_c)$ given hours. $D_c$ is a function of the candidate's tax and of hours there, formed from the grid values interpolated linearly at $\tau_c$.

\emph{The equilibrium test.} A candidate is an \emph{equilibrium} at the state if it is the global maximiser of its own frozen objective,
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:equilibriumTest}
    \mathcal{V}_c(\tau_c) \geq \max_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{V}_c(\tau),
\end{align}
with $\mathcal{V}_c(\tau_c)$ at an interior candidate read from the quadratic through the three nodes nearest it, up to a relative tolerance of $10^{-9}$, and at a corner equal to its node value. Two forms at the grid's resolution supplement the reading for an interior candidate, and neither applies to a corner: the candidate passes if its nearest node attains the maximum of $\mathcal{V}_c$ (a quadratic read a hair off a node can fall below that node's exact value by more than the tolerance), and, where its stencil touches an infeasible cell and only a linear interpolant is available, if the maximising node lies within one cell of it. An earlier version applied the one-cell form to every candidate; a corner next to a crossing within two cells then passed although its own objective was maximised at the adjacent node, out-ranked the crossing at its own state, and put a resolution-dependent jump into the policy that the calibration differentiated through (found on the Argentine instances, 2026-10-02). Candidates that pass and lie within one cell of each other are one equilibrium at the grid's resolution, represented by the interior crossing, which is located at sub-grid precision and moves continuously with the data; the counts below are of such equilibria, with the number merged reported alongside. This is the definition: the electorate, taking the state as given, prefers $\tau_c$ to every other tax, and the state is the one consistent with $\tau_c$. An upward crossing fails at its own state; a downward crossing passes only if no other point beats it there; a corner passes exactly when the objective at the corner's own state is maximised at the corner, which subsumes the boundary check.

\emph{Selection, and what it assumes.} The equilibria at a state need not be unique, and the multiplicity is intrinsic rather than numerical: even when $\mathcal{W}(\cdot\,;D)$ is strictly concave for every $D$, the map $\tau\mapsto\arg\max\mathcal{W}(\cdot\,;D(\tau))$ can have several fixed points, because the shares savers choose depend on the tax they anticipate. When more than one candidate passes \eqref{\refeq:equilibriumTest} we select the one with the highest value of the political objective in its own equilibrium, $\max_c \mathcal{V}_c(\tau_c)$. This is an equilibrium selection, and it is part of the definition of the equilibrium being computed rather than a numerical convenience: the continuation policy $\tau_{t+1}(\cdot)$ that date-$t$ savers and the date-$t$ electorate take as given is built from the selected policy, so the backward induction assumes, at every state and date, that households expect the selected equilibrium to be played, and the solution is unique only given the rule. The solvers therefore report, for every state and period, the number of candidates in \eqref{\refeq:candidates} and the number that pass \eqref{\refeq:equilibriumTest}; a run in which the latter never exceeds one is a run in which the rule never bound. Should no candidate pass --- which the grid can produce at a tangential crossing --- the state is flagged and the earlier integral criterion, $\arg\max_{\mathcal{C}}\int_l^{\tau}\hat{z}$, is returned for it, so that a recursion is not stopped by one state while the flag shows where the answer is not an equilibrium. A selection that switches between well-separated equilibria across neighbouring states puts a jump into the policy function that the calibration then differentiates through; the per-state counts are what make it visible. When a feasibility mask excludes part of $\mathcal{T}$ (as it will in \cref{\refsec:PEE}), everything above is applied to the feasible sub-grid, with $l$ and $u$ replaced by its endpoints.

\smalltitle{Root problems that are not maximisations.}
Not every root problem below is a first order condition. The state fixed point of \cref{\refsec:PEE} is a self-consistency condition in which \emph{any} crossing is a solution; there, neither the equilibrium test nor the selection above is applied. Keeping the two kinds of problem distinct matters, because the safeguards appropriate to one are errors in the other. The design choice of \cref{\refsec:ESC} sits at the opposite end and is not run through this machinery either, for its own reason given there: its objective is evaluated and maximised directly, because whether that choice is interior is itself the question being asked. It shares the discipline, not the machinery: every candidate design is evaluated at one frozen predetermined state, for the reason the equilibrium test above gives.

\smalltitle{Two implementation conventions.}
First, convergence of any gradient-polished solution is judged on $\max\left|z\right|$ directly, against an explicit tolerance, rather than on the solver's own success flag --- the flags reported by standard library routines are not consistent across methods, and a residual check is both stricter and portable. Second, a residual is only a meaningful convergence measure at an \emph{interior} optimum: at a corner, $z\neq 0$ is the correct answer, not an error, so residual diagnostics are restricted to periods (or states) whose selected policy is interior.
