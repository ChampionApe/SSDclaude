\begin{abstract}
\noindent


The design of social security systems shapes the political support for intergenerational transfers. In an overlapping generations model with heterogeneous households and probabilistic voting, we study how contributive vs.\ universal pensions and the link between benefits and earnings affect the social security tax rate, labor supply and capital accumulation. Taxes are lower when benefits are earnings-related, and a universal pension raises them when informal retirees are poorer than formal ones. Calibrated to Argentina, the model accounts for about three fifths of the rise in pension spending over the 2005--2010 expansion of coverage. Calibrated to the U.S., we show that pension spending is primarily determined by ageing and the earnings link. Testing it against 20 OECD countries, we show that with a given pension design the model correctly predicts that pension spending increases with ageing and not with income inequality. The model with a given pension design is seemingly at odds with the data in predicting that earnings-related benefits are associated with lower taxes. We reconcile this by letting the electorate also choose the design: we show that an interior choice requires redistribution through pensions to be costly unless the rich vote much more often than the poor. Testing it on the 20 OECD countries, we show that spending and the earnings link in the model rise together as population growth falls and are still unrelated to inequality, as in the data.

\end{abstract}
