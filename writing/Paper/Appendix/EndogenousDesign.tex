\section{Endogenous pension design}\label{app:esc}\setcounter{equation}{0}
This appendix collects the derivations behind section \ref{sec:esc}: the corner that costless redistribution produces, the deadweight cost of redistribution, the equilibrium with the cost, and the state independence of the design choice under log preferences. Throughout there are no informal households, $\gamma_0 = 0$, so a retiree of type $i$ receives the benefit $\nu_t w_t h_t\tau_t\left(1+\theta_t\left[y_i-1\right]\right)$, with $y_i$ the relative labor income of appendix \ref{app:PEE:LOG} and $\sum_i\gamma_i y_i = 1$.

\subsection{Costless redistribution}\label{app:esc:corner}
We consider two timings of the choice. If $\theta_t$ is chosen at $t$ together with $\tau_t$, it re-splits benefits that current retirees have already earned. By \eqref{eq:LOG:formalSol_i} the labor supply and savings of the young depend only on the design that applies to their own benefits, $\theta_{t+1}$, so $\theta_t$ moves no decision and no state, and its marginal effect on the political objective \eqref{eq:LOG:PolObj} runs entirely through current retirees. With $c_{2,t}^i = R_ts_{t-1}D_{t,i}$ as in appendix \ref{app:PEE:LOG},
\begin{align}\label{eq:esc:seqFOC}
   \dfrac{\partial \mathcal{W}_t}{\partial \theta_t} =\omega\,\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\,\tau_t\sum_i\gamma_i\mu_i\frac{y_i-1}{D_{t,i}}, \qquad D_{t,i} = \frac{s_{t-1,i}}{s_{t-1}}+\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\tau_t\left(1+\theta_t\left[y_i-1\right]\right).
\end{align}
The numerator is the change in the benefit of type $i$ and averages to zero across types: a more Bismarckian split moves a given total from retirees below mean income to retirees above it. With equal propensities to vote the sum therefore weights the gains of the rich by a lower marginal utility than the losses of the poor.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:esc:corner}
Assume log-GHH preferences, no informal households and no deadweight cost of redistribution (section \ref{sec:esc:cost}), and let the design $\theta_t$ that applies to the benefits paid at $t$ be chosen at $t$ together with $\tau_t$. Let the propensities to vote be positive and not rise with relative income, $\mu_i \leq \mu_j$ whenever $y_i > y_j$, equal propensities included, and let relative incomes not all be equal. Then, for every $\omega > 0$ and $\tau_t > 0$, the derivative \eqref{eq:esc:seqFOC} is negative at every $\theta_t \in [0,1]$ at which every retiree's consumption is positive, and the design chosen together with a positive tax is the Beveridgean corner, $\theta_t = 0$. This holds for all values of $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\xi$, $\nu_t$, $\gamma_i$, $\eta_i$ and $X_i$, and whatever tax and design current retirees expected when they saved.
\end{proposition}

\smalltitle{Proof of proposition \ref{prop:esc:corner}.} Since $\sum_i\gamma_i(y_i-1) = 0$, the sum in \eqref{eq:esc:seqFOC} equals $\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\gamma_i\gamma_j(y_i-y_j)\big(\mu_i/D_{t,i}-\mu_j/D_{t,j}\big)$. By \eqref{eq:si_s} at $t-1$, the savings share of a current retiree rises with $y_i$ with a slope of at least one, whatever tax and design generation $t-1$ anticipated for $t$, so $D_{t,i}$ rises with $y_i$. Where every retiree's consumption is positive, $\mu_i/D_{t,i}$ therefore falls with $y_i$, every term with $y_i \neq y_j$ is negative, and so is the derivative. Finally, $D_{t,i}$ falls with $\theta_t$ only for types below mean income, so the designs at which every retiree's consumption is positive form an interval that starts at $\theta_t = 0$, on which $\mathcal{W}_t$ is strictly decreasing. $\square$

\bigskip
The proof uses the propensities to vote only through $\mu_i/D_{t,i}$, which orders the types as $\mu_i/c_{2,t}^i$ does. The derivative is therefore negative at every design at which propensities to vote rise with relative income, if at all, by proportionally less than consumption in retirement, $\mu_i/\mu_j < c_{2,t}^i/c_{2,t}^j$ whenever $y_i > y_j$. In the political objective $\mu_i$ is the weight of type $i$ relative to its share of the population, of which turnout is the observable part. A responsiveness to policy that differed across types, the density of swing voters in probabilistic voting \parencite{LindbeckW87a1}, would multiply $\mu_i$, so the threshold bounds the product of turnout and responsiveness, and the calibration measures only the first. Under CRRA-GHH preferences the design still enters only the consumption of current retirees, and relative savings keep the form of \eqref{eq:si_s}, so $\mu_iD_{t,i}^{-1/\rho}$ takes the place of $\mu_i/D_{t,i}$ and the threshold becomes $\mu_i/\mu_j < (c_{2,t}^i/c_{2,t}^j)^{1/\rho}$.

Propensities to vote that rise with income can overturn the corner, and they do rise in the U.S.\ calibration of section \ref{sec:oecd}, so there the sign is a quantitative matter. With the middle income group at the mean, the sign of \eqref{eq:esc:seqFOC} is set by the threshold for the high- and the low-income group, which binds at $\theta_t = 0$, where the ratio of their consumption in retirement is lowest, $3.50$ in 2020. The corner therefore survives until the weight of the high-income group is $3.5$ times that of the low-income one at $\rho = 1$, twice the observed turnout ratio of $1.61$. This critical ratio is $12.2$ at $\rho = 0.5$ and $1.87$ at $\rho = 2$. Along the projection a larger system compresses consumption in retirement, and at $\rho = 2$ the critical ratio falls to $1.80$ from 2080 on, still above the observed ratio, so at every date and at each of the three elasticities the electorate that sets the design with the tax picks the Beveridgean corner.

If instead $\theta_{t+1}$ is chosen at $t$, the design no longer touches the benefits of current retirees but moves current labor supply and savings, directly and through the anticipated response of $\tau_{t+1}$, and
\begin{align}\label{eq:esc:leadFOC}
   \dfrac{\partial \mathcal{W}_t}{\partial \theta_{t+1}} = \sum_i\gamma_i\mu_i\left[\omega\,(1-\alpha)\dfrac{d\ln\Theta_{h,t}}{d\theta_{t+1}} + \nu_t\left((1+\beta)\dfrac{d\ln\tilde{c}_{1,t}^i}{d\theta_{t+1}} + \beta\dfrac{d\ln R_{t+1}}{d\theta_{t+1}}\right)\right],
\end{align}
where each total derivative includes the response of $\tau_{t+1}$, and of $\theta_{t+2}$, to the design chosen. Retirees are unanimous, since their term does not depend on $i$, and at a given anticipated tax they favor a more earnings-related system, which raises the return to an hour worked at $t$ and with it the labor supply on which their benefits are levied. The young are divided between this stake and the redistribution of their own future benefits, the force of \eqref{eq:esc:seqFOC} one period ahead. At observed U.S.\ inequality and under log preferences the redistributive force dominates, and the choice is again the corner $\theta = 0$. Section \ref{sec:esc} adopts this timing: it lets the design move labor supply and savings before it binds, without the commitment that a design chosen once and for all would require.

\subsection{The deadweight cost of redistribution}\label{app:esc:cost}
This subsection motivates the form of the cost in section \ref{sec:esc:cost}. It does not derive it from a model of participation and formality, and it leaves the size of the loss to the calibration.

\smalltitle{The implicit tax.} Against a purely earnings-related system that pays out the same revenue, $\Pi_t\tau_t$ per unit of past earnings to every type, the flat component levies the implicit tax $t_i$ of \eqref{eq:esc:implicitTax}: a tax on types above mean income and a subsidy to types below it. It is the redistributive part of the tax component of the contribution in the sense of \textcite{Summers89a1}, the part of the payroll charge that buys the contributor no benefit.

\smalltitle{Which margin.} The wedge \eqref{eq:esc:implicitTax} is an average implicit tax: it compares what type $i$ receives per unit of its earnings with the benchmark. The marginal wedge is a different object. The flat component depends on aggregate hours and not on own hours, so an extra hour raises the benefit of every type by $\Pi_t\tau_t\theta_t$ per unit of earnings, and the marginal wedge of the flat component, $\Pi_t\tau_t(1-\theta_t)$, is common to all types. That wedge is already in the model: it is the group $\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\theta\tau_{t+1}\Gamma_{s,t}$ in the labor supply function \eqref{eq:h}, through which hours respond to the design with the Frisch elasticity $\xi$, and GHH preferences carry no income effect through which the transfers between types could move hours. A cost built on the hours margin would count that response twice and could not depend on the spread of incomes. The cost therefore stands for margins the model does not contain and on which the average wedge is the relevant one, participation and formality: a worker who decides whether to bring earnings into the system, or into the formal sector, compares the earnings at stake with what they buy, so the wedge that matters is the average tax on those earnings \parencite{Saez02a1,KlevenK06a1}. Participation is the margin on which \textcite{Disney04a1} finds the tax component to matter. The reporting of earnings, on which \textcite{KumlerVF20a1} find it, is a different margin: how much of a given wage to report responds to the marginal wedge $\Pi_t\tau_t(1-\theta_t)$, which does not vanish when incomes are equal, so a loss on that margin would not depend on the dispersion of relative incomes. The cost does not contain it, and we read the evidence on reporting as evidence that the tax component matters rather than as a margin the cost prices.

\smalltitle{The Harberger loss.} Proportional wedges $t_i$ on bases $e_i$ that respond to their net-of-tax rates with a common elasticity $\varepsilon$ cost $\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\sum_i\gamma_i t_i^2 e_i$ to second order \parencite{Harberger64a1}. The wedge \eqref{eq:esc:implicitTax} falls on the benefit paid at $t$ per unit of earnings made at $t-1$, which a worker values at $1/R_t$ as in the labor supply condition \eqref{eq:model:formalFOCs}, so the rate that enters is $t_i/R_t$, and per unit of mean past earnings the bases are $e_i = y_i$. As a share of revenue the loss is then $\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\,(\Pi_t/R_t)\,\tau_t\,(1-\theta_t)^2\,\tilde{V}$, with $\tilde{V}$ the dispersion of \eqref{eq:esc:budget}: quadratic in the flat share of the system, linear in its size and zero when all incomes are equal. The quadratic counts the subsidy to types below mean income as a loss, as it does the tax on types above it; where participation is already taxed, lowering the participation tax of low-income workers can reduce the total loss \parencite{Saez02a1}, so the symmetry is a property of the reduced form rather than of the argument.

\smalltitle{Specification.} The form $f(\theta_t,\tau_t)$ of \eqref{eq:esc:budget} scales both components of the benefit alike, so that $1-f$ is the share of revenue lost and, to first order, the Harberger share with $\lambda$ in the place of $\varepsilon\Pi_t/R_t$.\footnote{The factor $\Pi_t/R_t$ multiplies every implicit tax alike and so scales the loss once. It falls as the population ages, from $0.55$ in 2020 to $0.47$ in 2110 along the U.S.\ path of section \ref{sec:oecd} at $\rho = 1$. The U.S.\ results of section \ref{sec:esc:calibration} absorb it into a constant $\lambda$, and the \oa{esc-countries} gives the UK and France that $\lambda$; to carry the cost across countries, section \ref{sec:esc:crossSection} and appendix \ref{app:oecd26} keep it, $\lambda_{n,t} = \varepsilon\,\Pi_{n,t}/R_{n,t}$.} Since $\ln f$ is quadratic in $1-\theta_t$, both $\partial\ln f/\partial\theta_t$ and $\partial\ln f/\partial\tau_t$ vanish at $\theta_t = 1$, so benefits and the tax, through which alone the design enters the equilibrium of appendix \ref{app:esc:equilibrium}, have there the values and slopes they have without the cost. Under log preferences, where the design chosen does not move the designs chosen later (proposition \ref{prop:esc:separability}), the slope of the political objective at the Bismarckian corner and the tax chosen there therefore do not depend on $\lambda$, and the cost cannot by itself make the corner a local maximum of the political objective. Scaling both components alike is a choice. A cost on the flat component alone delivers the same endpoints and differs in the interior, but it loses the separate identification of $\theta$ and $\lambda$ (appendix \ref{app:esc:equilibrium}). A cost on the design itself rather than on the transfer it performs, $f(\theta) = \phi + (1-\phi)\theta^{p}$, stays in place when a compressed income distribution removes the redistributive stakes; the \oa{esc-costs} reports it.

\smalltitle{Magnitude.} The shape of $f$ follows from the tax component; its size does not. In the U.S.\ calibration for 2020 the return factor is $\Pi_t = 1.35$, close to $\nu_t$ (table \ref{table:US:CalibUSFR}), and the thirty-year return to which $\beta$ is calibrated is $R_t = 2.44$ (appendix \ref{app:US}), so that $\Pi_t/R_t$ is about $0.55$. Participation and taxable-income elasticities of $0.1$ to $0.4$ \parencite{ChettyGMW11a1,SaezSG12a1} then correspond to $\lambda$ between about $0.06$ and $0.22$. The calibrated values of section \ref{sec:esc} are more than an order of magnitude above that range at $\rho = 0.5$ and $1$, $28$ to $113$ times as large at $\rho = 1$, and $6$ to $25$ times as large at $\rho = 2$, where $\lambda$ is smallest, so $\lambda$ also stands in for administrative, evasion and political costs of redistribution that the model does not contain.

\subsection{The equilibrium with the cost}\label{app:esc:equilibrium}
With the cost, a retiree of type $i$ receives $f(\theta_t,\tau_t)\,\nu_tw_th_t\tau_t\left(1+\theta_t\left[y_i-1\right]\right)$, and the model without the cost is $f = 1$.

\smalltitle{Economic equilibrium.} In appendix \ref{app:EE}, $\tau_{t+1}$ enters only through the benefits it pays the current young, and $\tau_t$ through the benefits of current retirees and the net wage. The cost is therefore exactly the substitution of $\tau_{t+1}f(\theta_{t+1},\tau_{t+1})$ for $\tau_{t+1}$ throughout appendix \ref{app:EE}, and of $\tau_tf(\theta_t,\tau_t)$ for $\tau_t$ in the benefits of current retirees, with the net wage $(1-\tau_t)w_t$ unchanged. Here $\tau_{t+1} = \mathcal{T}_{t+1}(\theta_{t+1})$, with $\mathcal{T}_{t+1}$ the equilibrium tax function below, so the young anticipate both the design and the size of the system that will pay them.

\smalltitle{The tax condition.} In the first order condition of appendix \ref{app:PEE:LOG}, the substitution changes only the benefit term of $\mathcal{E}_{2,t}^i$, in which $\tau_tf$ replaces $\tau_t$,
\begin{align}\label{eq:esc:E2}
    \mathcal{E}_{2,t}^i = (1-\alpha)\frac{\partial \ln\left(\Theta_{h,t}\right)}{\partial \tau_t}+\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\left(1+\theta_t\left[y_i-1\right]\right)\frac{f\left(1+\ln f\right)}{D_{t,i}},
\end{align}
with $D_{t,i}$ evaluated at $\tau_tf$. The factor $f(1+\ln f) = \partial(\tau_tf)/\partial\tau_t$, since $\ln f$ is linear in $\tau_t$: a larger system is worth less to a retiree at the margin than the revenue it collects. The young's terms contain no policy but $\tau_t$, so the condition involves $\theta_{t+1}$ nowhere, and the equilibrium tax at $t$ is a function $\mathcal{T}_t(\theta_t)$ of the inherited design and of parameters and demographics alone, as in proposition \ref{prop:LOG:PEE}.

\smalltitle{Identification of $\theta$.} The benefit of type $j$ per unit of mean past earnings, $\Pi_t\tau_tf\left(1+\theta_t\left[y_j-1\right]\right)$, is a line in relative earnings, and its earnings-linked share at mean earnings, the datum that identifies $\theta$ (appendix \ref{app:oecd26:data}), is $\theta_t$, since $f$ scales slope and intercept alike. The design is therefore identified separately from the cost, and $\theta^{\ast} = 0.544$ for the U.S.\ whatever $\lambda$. A cost on the flat component alone would give the share $\theta_t/\left(\theta_t + f(1-\theta_t)\right)$, so that the same datum would imply a different $\theta$ for every value of the cost parameter.

\smalltitle{The design condition.} With the design chosen one period in advance, $\theta_{t+1}$ enters the objective through the split of the benefits of the young and through $\tau_{t+1}f(\theta_{t+1},\tau_{t+1})$, with $\tau_{t+1}$ the anticipated equilibrium tax. Without the cost, a more Bismarckian design at a given anticipated tax shifts next period's benefits one for one from the flat to the earnings-related component, and the redistributive stake of the young dominates as in appendix \ref{app:esc:corner}. With it, the design also raises the total the young will receive, since less of the revenue is lost,
\begin{align}\label{eq:esc:ftheta}
    \frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta_{t+1}} = f\,\lambda\,\tau_{t+1}\,\tilde{V}\,(1-\theta_{t+1}) \geq 0,
\end{align}
which is what lets the design condition cross zero in the interior. The gain is zero at $\theta_{t+1} = 1$. It rises with the size of the system the young anticipate and with the dispersion of relative incomes if and only if $\ln f > -1$, and with the flat share $1-\theta_{t+1}$ if and only if $\ln f > -\frac{1}{2}$. The first condition holds wherever the anticipated tax is interior, since the benefit term of \eqref{eq:esc:E2} carries the factor $1+\ln f$ and every other term of the tax condition is negative. At the 2020 tax rate the second holds at every design and every intertemporal elasticity: the loss is largest at a flat benefit, and there it is at most $26.6\%$ of revenue, at $\rho = 0.5$ (table \ref{table:US_ESC:calibration}), less than the $39\%$ at which $\ln f = -\frac{1}{2}$.

\subsection{The design choice under log preferences}\label{app:esc:log}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:esc:separability}
Assume log-GHH preferences and no informal households, and let the design be chosen one period in advance under the cost \eqref{eq:esc:budget}. Then the political objective at $t$ is additively separable,
\begin{align}\label{eq:esc:separability}
    \mathcal{W}_t = \mathcal{A}_t\left(\tau_t,\theta_t\right) + \mathcal{B}_t\left(\theta_{t+1}\right) + \mathcal{C}_t\left(s_{t-1}\right),
\end{align}
where $\mathcal{C}_t$ depends on no policy. The tax chosen at $t$ depends on the inherited design, $\theta_t$, and on neither $\theta_{t+1}$ nor $s_{t-1}$; the design chosen at $t$ maximizes $\mathcal{B}_t$ alone and depends on neither $\theta_t$ nor $s_{t-1}$.
\end{proposition}

\smalltitle{Proof of proposition \ref{prop:esc:separability}.} By \eqref{eq:h} with the substitution of appendix \ref{app:esc:equilibrium}, $\Theta_{h,t}$ is the product of a factor in $\tau_t$ and a factor in $(\tau_{t+1},\theta_{t+1})$, and $\tau_{t+1} = \mathcal{T}_{t+1}(\theta_{t+1})$, so $\ln h_t$ is the sum of a term in $\tau_t$, a term in $\theta_{t+1}$ and a multiple of $\ln(s_{t-1}/\nu_t)$. By \eqref{eq:s} and appendix \ref{app:EE:CRRA} the same holds for $\ln s_t$ and $\ln\tilde{c}_{1,t}^i$. The return $\ln R_{t+1}$ is affine in $\ln(s_t/\nu_{t+1})$ and $\ln\Theta_{h,t+1}$, which depends on $\tau_{t+1}$ and on the continuation $(\theta_{t+2},\tau_{t+2})$. By backward induction from the terminal period, in which no design is chosen, the design chosen at $t+1$ depends on no state, so the continuation is a function of $\theta_{t+1}$ alone. Finally, $\ln c_{2,t}^i$ is $(1-\alpha)\ln h_t+\alpha\ln(s_{t-1}/\nu_t)$ plus $\ln D_{t,i}$, a function of $(\tau_t,\theta_t)$ since by \eqref{eq:si_s} at $t-1$ the savings shares of current retirees are. Every term of \eqref{eq:LOG:PolObj} is therefore a function of $(\tau_t,\theta_t)$, of $\theta_{t+1}$ or of $s_{t-1}$, which is \eqref{eq:esc:separability}. The tax condition involves $\mathcal{A}_t$ alone, and the design chosen at $t$ maximizes $\mathcal{B}_t$. $\square$

\bigskip
The design chosen at $t$ is thus the same whatever system the electorate inherits and whatever the capital stock, which is what section \ref{sec:numerical} uses: each date's choice is a search over a grid of designs with no state, the tax at each candidate design is found as in proposition \ref{prop:LOG:PEE}, and the condition $\theta_{2020} = \theta^{\ast}$ that calibrates $\lambda$ in section \ref{sec:esc:calibration} is a function of $\lambda$ alone. The property is specific to log preferences. Under CRRA preferences the powers do not separate, both $s_{t-1}$ and $\theta_t$ re-enter the design choice, and the equilibrium is a backward recursion over policy functions of the state $(s_{t-1},\theta_t)$ \parencite{BergGE26doc}.
