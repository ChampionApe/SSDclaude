OA.4 The endogenous pension design

Section 7 of the paper lets the electorate choose the design \(\theta\) as well as the tax rate, under a deadweight cost of redistribution whose parameter \(\lambda\) is calibrated so that the U.S. electorate re-elects its observed design. This chapter holds the tables behind section 7, the same counterfactuals with the UK as host, the design along the baseline path, the timing of the choice and an alternative cost specification.

OA.4.1 The cost and the cross-country test

The paper’s two tables of section 7.2: the calibrated cost at each intertemporal elasticity of substitution (IES), and the designs the UK and France choose under the U.S. parameter, with the UK’s own parameter; France, at the corner, has none.

\(\cdot\)

Table 4 of the paper: one row per IES, each with the cost parameter at which the U.S. electorate re-elects its observed design in 2020. The parameter, and with it the share of revenue lost at the observed design and at flat benefits, falls steeply as the IES rises, since a higher elasticity strengthens the forward-looking channels that hold the choice away from the Beveridgean corner.

Table 5 of the paper: the test of the cost parameter calibrated on the U.S. alone, at an IES of one. Under the U.S. parameter the chosen designs keep the observed ranking of the UK, the U.S. and France, compressed toward the U.S. value; the UK’s own parameter lies below the U.S. one, and regrouping the UK at the U.S. income percentiles moves its chosen design only slightly.

OA.4.2 Counterfactuals on the U.S.

Each counterfactual is a separate equilibrium path read in 2020, with one characteristic changed over the whole horizon, every other parameter at its U.S. value and the political choice binding from the first period. Each is read twice, with the design pinned at the U.S. value and with the design chosen, and both readings carry the cost, so the distance between them is the political response. The figure’s last row, France’s income distribution and voting patterns together, shares its design, tax rate and savings rate with the table’s row that imposes all French characteristics, since France’s level of \(X\) moves hours and nothing else. In the paper’s figure an open marker is the pinned reading and a filled one the chosen; click either for its row.

\(\cdot\)

Figure 7.1 of the paper: each counterfactual with the design pinned and with it chosen, at three values of the IES. Acute ageing moves the chosen design toward earnings-related benefits and France’s voting patterns toward flat ones at every IES, the voting patterns furthest at \(\rho = 2\), where choosing the design also adds to the rise in the tax rate and shortens the workweek; the direction in which France’s income distribution moves the design changes with the IES, and the last row follows the voting patterns.

Table OA.41: acute ageing at each IES, read with the design pinned at the U.S. value and with it chosen, both under the cost. The baseline rows are the economy with the cost in place and the design chosen, so they differ slightly from those of section OA.3.4. The chosen design is more Bismarckian at every IES and nearly the same across them, and choosing it adds a little to the rise in the tax rate and to the fall in the savings rate and leaves the workweek almost unchanged.

France’s income distribution on the U.S. at each IES, read with the design pinned and with it chosen. With the design pinned the tax rate falls at every IES, by less than without the cost (table OA.31), since the compressed distribution also lowers the share of revenue the flat component dissipates. The chosen design is less Bismarckian than the observed one at \(\rho = 0.5\) and more Bismarckian at \(\rho = 1\) and 2, and choosing it keeps the tax rate close to the pinned reading at \(\rho = 0.5\) and 1 and raises it at \(\rho = 2\).

France’s voting patterns on the U.S. at each IES, read with the design pinned and with it chosen. The chosen design moves toward flat benefits at every IES, furthest at \(\rho = 2\), where the cost that holds the observed design in place is thinnest; choosing it keeps the tax rate close to the pinned reading at \(\rho = 0.5\) and 1, and at \(\rho = 2\) raises it and shortens the workweek.

All of France’s household characteristics on the U.S. at each IES, read with the design pinned and with it chosen. The voting patterns prevail: the chosen design moves toward flat benefits at every IES, further than under the voting patterns alone (table OA.43), and choosing it keeps the tax rate close to the pinned reading at \(\rho = 0.5\) and 1 and raises it at \(\rho = 2\).

OA.4.3 The UK as host

The same counterfactuals with the UK as host, at the UK’s own cost parameter, the one at which its electorate re-elects the UK’s observed design; it lies below the U.S. parameter at an IES of one or below and above it at two. The figure sets the two hosts side by side; the tables print the UK’s numbers. On both hosts France’s voting profile moves the design most, toward flat benefits, and by less on the UK, whose own voting profile is already flatter than the U.S. one. France’s income distribution moves the two hosts’ designs in opposite directions, since on the U.S. it compresses relative incomes and on the UK it spreads them. The design response leaves the UK’s tax rate, savings rate and workweek essentially at the reading with the design pinned, so on the UK the response is confined to the design itself.

\(\cdot\)

France’s characteristics on the two hosts with the design chosen, the U.S. in the upper row of panels and the UK in the lower, on shared axes so that the hosts compare by position. The last row is France’s income distribution and voting patterns together, whose design and tax rate are those of the tables’ all-French rows. On the U.S. that row follows the voting patterns at every IES; on the UK it does so at \(\rho = 1\) and 2, while at \(\rho = 0.5\) the chosen design barely moves.

France’s income distribution on the UK at each IES, read with the design pinned at the UK’s value and with it chosen. The chosen design moves toward earnings-related benefits at \(\rho = 0.5\) and toward flat ones at \(\rho = 1\) and 2, each time the reverse of its move on the U.S. (table OA.42).

France’s voting patterns on the UK at each IES, read with the design pinned at the UK’s value and with it chosen. The chosen design moves toward flat benefits at every IES, furthest at \(\rho = 2\), as on the U.S. (table OA.43).

All of France’s household characteristics on the UK at each IES, read with the design pinned at the UK’s value and with it chosen. At \(\rho = 1\) and 2 the chosen design follows the voting patterns toward flat benefits, while at \(\rho = 0.5\) it barely moves; the workweek ends above the UK’s baseline in both readings, where on the U.S. it ends below.

OA.4.4 The design along the baseline path

The calibration pins the design in 2020 only; every other design on the path, before 2020 as after it, is an outcome of the model. Section 7 describes the drift of the design along the baseline path in words; here are the paths of the design and of the tax rate, chosen and pinned, at each IES and at two other values of the Frisch elasticity \(\xi\), which the paper reports in a footnote.

\(\cdot\)

Along the baseline path the chosen design dips below the observed one before 2020 at every specification, returns to it in 2020, where the calibration pins it, and then rises toward earnings-related benefits as ageing proceeds, leveling off with population growth. After 2020 the tax rate with the design chosen lies slightly above the one with it pinned, since a more Bismarckian system dissipates less and is worth more to the electorate at the margin, and the Frisch elasticity barely moves either line.

The design choice at three Frisch elasticities and an IES of one, each recalibrated with its own cost parameter; the design channel runs through hours, which makes this the elasticity to vary. The cost parameter falls a little as the elasticity rises, the designs on the baseline path and under acute ageing are nearly unchanged, and only the level of the tax rate under acute ageing moves, lower at a higher elasticity whether the design is pinned or chosen.

OA.4.5 The timing of the design choice

Appendix C.1 of the paper shows that each timing of the choice puts the design at a corner when redistribution is costless: a design chosen together with the tax rate re-splits benefits current retirees have already earned, a design chosen one period ahead trades the young’s redistributive stake against the forward-looking one, and a design chosen once and for all enters every channel at once. The table reports the numerical checks of these timings.

\(\cdot\)

One block per timing of the choice, all without the cost of redistribution. Chosen with the tax or one period in advance, the design is the Beveridgean corner at every IES checked. Chosen once and for all, it is the Beveridgean corner at the lower elasticities of the grid shown and the Bismarckian corner at the higher ones; near the switch the electorate is close to indifferent between the two, so the switch is not sharply located.

A cost attached to the design rather than to the transfer it performs. The paper’s cost in the upper block and the cost on the design in the lower.
