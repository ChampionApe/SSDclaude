C5. The cross-country test

Section 7.3 of the paper tests the model on the countries of figure 1.1. Every country keeps its own workers per retiree, corrected for its gap to the U.S. statutory retirement age, its income distribution, its observed design and its propensities to vote by income group, from the CSES; Luxembourg, which has no CSES survey and enforces compulsory voting, votes at the same rate in every income group. Every country takes from the U.S. the discount factor \(\beta\) and the cost of redistribution as the elasticity \(\varepsilon\), so that country \(n\) faces \(\lambda_{n,t} = \varepsilon\,\Pi_{n,t}/R_{n,t}\) at its own ratio of the pay-as-you-go return to the market return. In the model every country also takes the same political weight of the old \(\omega\), the mean of the weights that match each country’s pension spending at its observed design; the model with each country’s own political weight, own \(\omega\) for short, calibrates each country’s \(\omega\) to that spending. The U.S. anchor, where \(\omega\) and \(\varepsilon\) are calibrated, is measured as every other country is (appendix G.2 of the paper). This page shows the variants of appendix G in full, and the test with Luxembourg left out and with the model at the U.S. political weight.

The paper’s specification

The paper reports the test in figure 7.2 and table 5, and each country’s own political weight and the design it chooses with own \(\omega\) in table 10. Table 11 gives the slopes under each variant below, and figures G.1 and G.2 the predicted designs.

Other population data

Workers per retiree as the population data give them, and corrected for each country’s gap to the U.S. effective age of labour-market exit in place of the statutory age. The data side of the test does not depend on them. With own \(\omega\) the test hardly moves under either reading, since the political weights are calibrated to spending. In the model the exit age leaves the test close to the paper’s. With workers per retiree uncorrected, Luxembourg, whose population grew fastest, chooses a design close to the Beveridgean corner, and the predicted designs track the observed ones much less closely; without Luxembourg they track them about as closely as under either correction (appendix G.4 of the paper).

Other measures of the design

Each measure of the design is its own cross-section: the data and the model are calibrated to it, on the countries where it is defined, with the U.S. anchor recalibrated under it (appendix G.4 of the paper). The public tier on 23 countries leaves the test as it is. Under all mandatory tiers the relations keep their signs, while the model predicts the design less well, in part because it puts Iceland, which only this measure covers, close to the Beveridgean corner.

The countries outside the test

Figure 1.1, and so the test, leaves out the OECD members before 2000 that the model does not represent. In Korea, Mexico and Turkey a large share of employment is informal, and the model of sections 6 and 7 has no informal households. In Australia, Denmark, Iceland, the Netherlands and Switzerland funded tiers pay a large share of mandatory benefits, or the public pension is tested against them, where the model has a single public pay-as-you-go system. D1 gives the data of the countries the figure covers.
