\subsection{Endogenous system characteristics}\label{\refsec:ESC}
This section documents how the design choices of \cref{\refmodelsec:ESC} are solved. Two solvers are described, one per timing --- the sequential choice needs none, since \eqref{\refmodeleq:esc:seqFOC} is a scalar first order condition of the same form as the tax one, and its sign is established by evaluating the costless expression on the solved baseline path over a grid of $\theta_t$ at every dated period (negative throughout under log preferences and at $\rho = 0.5$ and $2$) --- together with the calibration of the cost parameter and the checks that establish the structural claims rather than assuming them. Numbers quoted in \crefrange{\refsec:ESC:leaded}{\refsec:ESC:crra} that depend on the cost were measured under the previous wedge, $f(\theta) = \phi + (1-\phi)\theta^{p}$, which is kept as a comparison arm; the size-scaled cost of \eqref{\refmodeleq:esc:AB} is calibrated in \cref{\refsec:ESC:calibration}, and its own numbers are the checks of \cref{\refsec:ESC:checks}.

Throughout, $\Theta = \left\lbrace \theta^{(1)},\dots,\theta^{(M_{\theta})}\right\rbrace$ denotes a grid on $[0,1]$ and $\mathcal{T}_t(\theta)$ the ordinary equilibrium tax at date $t$ given a design $\theta$, i.e.\ the solution of $z_t(\tau,\theta)=0$ selected as in \cref{\refsec:RobustRoot}. By the decoupling property this is a \emph{static} scalar problem, one root per node, with no dependence on $s_{t-1}$ and no recursion --- which is what makes the log case cheap.

\smalltitle{Why a grid and not a first order condition.} The question being asked is whether the choice is interior. A solver built on $\mathrm{d}\mathcal{W}/\mathrm{d}\theta = 0$ answers it only by failing to converge, and a corner is then indistinguishable from a badly behaved residual. We therefore evaluate the objective itself on $\Theta$, take the maximiser, and refine it by fitting a parabola through the three nodes around it when the maximum is interior. A corner is returned \emph{as} a corner. The cost is one objective evaluation per node; the benefit is that the central result of the exercise is observed rather than inferred. This inverts the convention used for the tax: there the first order condition locates the candidates and the objective, evaluated at each candidate's own predetermined state, selects among them (\cref{\refsec:RobustRoot}); here the objective is evaluated directly, precisely because its corner behaviour is the substance of the question. The first order condition route remains open once the selection rule carries the corners as candidates, and \cref{\refsec:ESC:crra} states it for the CRRA design (\cref{\refalg:esc:crra2Dfoc}).

\smalltitle{Evaluating the objective does not relax the discipline on predetermined states.} Direct evaluation may look like it surrenders what the first order condition was built to guarantee --- that the electorate takes the inherited distribution of savings as given. It does not, because that guarantee never resided in the representation. The tax residual $z_t$ holds $s_{t-1,i}/s_{t-1}$ fixed because it is \emph{constructed} as the derivative at a frozen distribution, and the selection among its candidates compares them at a frozen distribution for the same reason (\cref{\refsec:RobustRoot}); a grid over designs implements the same definition by evaluating every candidate design at the same predetermined distribution, and the representations enforce one discipline. What \emph{does} move with the candidate --- the current young's labour, savings and their distribution --- is meant to: those choices are being made at $t$, not sunk. Under log preferences the discipline is automatic for the leaded timing: the distribution at vintage $t-1$ is a function of $(\tau_t,\theta_t)$ alone, and neither moves with the candidate $\theta_{t+1}$, since the tax does not see it by decoupling. Under CRRA preferences it is not automatic. The distribution depends on $B_t$, hence on $R_t$ and current hours, and the tax responds to the candidate, so a solver that recomputes the distribution at each candidate's own tax and hours credits the electorate with moving a state it inherits. \Cref{\refsec:ESC:crra} therefore defines the equilibrium at a state with the distribution frozen through both choices and closed afterwards by a scalar consistency condition, and solves it as such; the solver that produced the published CRRA rows of the endogenous-design results valued each candidate at its own distribution, and those rows carry that caveat until the final run replaces them. Under the permanent timing the candidate enters the ratio's formula directly, through $\theta_{t_0}$, and there the ratio is pinned explicitly; \cref{\refsec:ESC:permanent} states the pinning and measures what ignoring it would cost.


\subsubsection{The leaded choice}\label{\refsec:ESC:leaded}
The design chosen at $t$ is a state at $t+1$, so the object to identify is a pair of sequences of policy functions, $\mathcal{T}_t(\theta_t)$ and $\Theta_t(\theta_t)$. \Cref{prop:esc:separability} says the second is constant in its argument, but the algorithm does not use that: it tabulates the policy on $\Theta$ precisely so that the claim can be measured.

\begin{alg}\label{\refalg:esc:leaded}
\emph{Leaded choice, log preferences.} Iterate backwards over $t = T-1,\dots,1$.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Terminal period.} Solve $\mathcal{T}_{T-1}(\theta)$ for every $\theta\in\Theta$ from the terminal first order condition, i.e.\ \eqref{\refmodeleq:PEELOG} evaluated with $\beta_{T,j}=0$. There is no $\theta_T$ to choose.
    \item \emph{Taxes.} At $t<T-1$, evaluate $z_t$ on the flattened mesh $\Theta\times\tilde{\mathcal{T}}$ in a single call --- $z_t$ is elementwise in both arguments, so the mesh costs one evaluation rather than $M_{\theta}$ --- and select at each node as in \cref{\refsec:RobustRoot}. \emph{Polish} each interior selection by a bracketed scalar root find within one grid cell.
    \item \emph{Design.} For each $\theta_t\in\Theta$, evaluate
    \begin{align}\label{\refeq:esc:leadedObjective}
        \mathcal{W}_t\left(\theta';\theta_t\right) = \sum_i \gamma_{t-1,i}\omega p_{t-1}\mu_{t-1,i}\ln\left(c_{2,t}^i\right) + \nu_t\sum_i \gamma_{t,i}\mu_{t,i}\left[(1+\beta_{t,i})\ln\left(\tilde{c}_{1,t}^i\right)+\beta_{t,i}\ln\left(R_{t+1}\right)\right]
    \end{align}
    at every candidate $\theta'\in\Theta$, using $\tau_t = \mathcal{T}_t(\theta_t)$ from step 2 and the continuation $\tau_{t+1} = \mathcal{T}_{t+1}(\theta')$, $\theta_{t+2} = \Theta_{t+1}(\theta')$, $\tau_{t+2} = \mathcal{T}_{t+2}(\theta_{t+2})$ from the periods already solved. Set $\Theta_t(\theta_t)$ to the refined maximiser.
\end{enumerate}
\end{alg}

Three implementation points are worth recording, because each guards against a plausible wrong answer.

\emph{The polish is not optional.} The grid selection alone is $O(\text{spacing}^2)$ accurate, roughly $10^{-4}$ at the resolutions used, while $\tau_{t_0}$ is a calibration target matched at $10^{-8}$. Without the refinement the cost parameter would be calibrated against grid noise. Corners are never polished: $z_t \neq 0$ there is the correct answer, not a residual.

\emph{$s_{t-1}$ is set to one.} By \cref{prop:esc:separability} the maximiser does not depend on it. The normalisation is convenient rather than necessary and is verified numerically, by re-maximising \eqref{\refeq:esc:leadedObjective} at savings levels spanning two orders of magnitude and confirming the argument does not move.

\emph{Interpolation is piecewise linear throughout.} A policy function that is about to be maximised over must not carry interpolation artefacts: a smoother or a cubic can manufacture a spurious interior maximum out of a profile that is genuinely flat at a bound, and an argmax --- unlike a root --- has no residual through which such an artefact would announce itself. The cautions of \cref{\refsec:PEE} about interpolation kind therefore apply with more force here than in their original setting.


\subsubsection{The permanent choice}\label{\refsec:ESC:permanent}
Here the design is chosen once, at $t_0$, and the electorate chooses $\tau_{t_0}$ alongside it. \Cref{prop:esc:concentration} reduces what looks like a two-dimensional grid search over $\left(\tau_{t_0},\theta\right)$ to a one-dimensional one, and the reduction is what makes the exercise cheap:

\begin{alg}\label{\refalg:esc:permanent}
\emph{Permanent choice.} Given a pinned ratio $\bar{\sigma}_i$, for each $\theta\in\Theta$: (i) obtain $\tau_t = \mathcal{T}_t(\theta)$ for every $t\geq t_0$ --- for $t>t_0$ this is the ordinary equilibrium at a constant design, with no recursion in $\theta$ to solve; (ii) evaluate \eqref{\refeq:esc:leadedObjective} at $t_0$ with $\theta_{t_0}=\theta'=\theta$ and $s_{t_0-1,i}/s_{t_0-1}=\bar{\sigma}_i$. Take the refined maximiser over $\Theta$. The vote being anticipated, $\bar{\sigma}_i$ is itself the ratio implied by the winning design, so the design is the fixed point of
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:esc:permFixedPoint}
    \theta^{\ast} = \arg\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\ \mathcal{W}_{t_0}\left(\theta;\ \sigma_i(\theta^{\ast})\right),
\end{align}
reached by iterating the above from the incumbent design. Convergence is reported, not assumed: this is a best response and not a contraction, so a cycle has to be visible.
\end{alg}

\smalltitle{The predetermined ratio must be held fixed, and at which value.} This is the one place where the permanent timing differs from the leaded one in substance rather than in bookkeeping, and it raises two questions that are easily run together.

The first is whether the ratio may move with the candidate at all. It may not. Under the leaded timing $\theta_{t+1}$ does not appear in $s_{t_0-1,i}/s_{t_0-1}$ and the question never arises; under the permanent timing $\theta$ does appear there, through $\theta_{t_0}$. But savings made at $t_0-1$ are sunk when the vote is taken at $t_0$: a voter comparing two candidates does not face a different $s_{t_0-1,i}$ under each. Maximising $\mathcal{W}_{t_0}$ while recomputing the ratio at every candidate would credit the electorate with internalising a state it takes as given --- the same error that \cref{\refsec:PEE} forbids for $\tau_t$, arriving in a new guise --- and it is not a small one: at $p=0.4$ the two readings give $0.775$ and $0.910$. It is also inconsistent with the tax it would be paired with, since $s_{t_0-1,i}/s_{t_0-1}$ is a function of $\tau_{t_0}$ as well as $\theta_{t_0}$ while $\mathcal{T}_{t_0}$ solves $z_{t_0}=0$, which is constructed holding the ratio fixed. It would take \cref{prop:esc:concentration} with it.

The second question is which value the pinned ratio takes, and there the answer is the timing's. The reform is \emph{anticipated}: households arrive at $t_0$ knowing a design will be chosen, and what they choose among is which of the $\left|\Theta\right|$ equilibrium paths the economy follows from $t_0$ on. The savings they made at $t_0-1$ were therefore made against the design that wins, which is \eqref{\refeq:esc:permFixedPoint}. The incumbent's ratio --- the natural pinning if the reform were a surprise --- is the seed of that iteration and not its answer. The two coincide \emph{exactly} wherever the chosen design reproduces the incumbent one, which is precisely the calibration condition \eqref{\refeq:esc:calibration}, so the calibrated cost is common to both readings and only the counterfactuals separate them; at $p=0.4$, away from the permanent timing's own calibration, the anticipated reading gives $0.775$ against the unanticipated $0.773$, and at $p=0.25$ it gives $0.542$ against $0.549$. Both are reported.

That the fixed point is a scalar problem at all is worth one line. The ratio in \eqref{\refmodeleq:esc:auxiliary:si} at vintage $t_0-1$ is a function of date-$t_0$ policy alone, so the kinked path the reform actually is --- the incumbent design before $t_0$, the chosen one from $t_0$ --- delivers the same $\bar{\sigma}_i$ as a design that had always been $\theta$, and no separate solve is needed to evaluate a candidate's savings. The kink is nonetheless carried in the reported equilibrium path, where a design applied to periods before the vote would be a counterfactual past.

\smalltitle{Verification.} \Cref{prop:esc:concentration} is checked rather than trusted: the tax at the chosen design must equal $\mathcal{T}_{t_0}$ evaluated there, and does, to $10^{-6}$. Were the concentration to fail --- because some channel made $\theta$ a function of $\tau$ --- \cref{\refalg:esc:permanent} would be solving the wrong problem, and this is the check that would say so.


\subsubsection{CRRA preferences}\label{\refsec:ESC:crra}
None of the log simplifications survive. The powers in $\left(\tilde{c}\right)^{1-1/\rho}$ do not turn products into sums, so \eqref{\refmodeleq:esc:separability} fails, both $s_{t-1}$ and $\theta_t$ re-enter the design choice, and the Markov object is a policy function over the two-dimensional state $\left(s_{t-1},\theta_t\right)$ --- a second dimension on top of the grid machinery of \cref{\refsec:PEE}. For the permanent timing this costs nothing: a permanent design means a \emph{constant} $\theta$ path, each candidate is one ordinary CRRA equilibrium solve, and \cref{\refalg:esc:permanent} goes through unchanged. For the leaded timing the object is computed by extending the backward recursion of \cref{\refsec:PEE} with the design as a second state (\cref{\refalg:esc:crra2D}); a cheaper path iteration that approximates it is stated after it (\cref{\refalg:esc:crraPath}) and kept as a cross-check; and the same equilibrium is then formulated through first order conditions for both choices (\cref{\refalg:esc:crra2Dfoc}), which was piloted against it and not adopted.

\smalltitle{The equilibrium at a state.} At $t$ the electorate inherits $\left(s_{t-1},\theta_t\right)$ and the distribution of past savings $D_t\equiv\left\lbrace s_{t-1,i}/s_{t-1}\right\rbrace_i$, and chooses $\left(\tau_t,\theta_{t+1}\right)$. $D_t$ is \eqref{\refmodeleq:EE:si_s} at vintage $t-1$: a function of $\left(\tau_t,\theta_t\right)$ and of $\bm{B}_t$, which under CRRA carries $R_t$, hence $s_{t-1}$ and current hours. With a discount factor common to all types --- which every calibration imposes, and which the solver asserts --- it is the one-parameter family
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:esc:aDef}
    \frac{s_{t-1,i}}{s_{t-1}} = 1 + a_t\left(y_{t-1,i}-1\right), \qquad a_t = 1+\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\,\tau_t\,\frac{f\left(\theta_t,\tau_t\right)\left(1-\theta_t\right)}{1+B_t},
\end{align}
with $y_{t-1,i}$ the relative income of \cref{\refmodelsec:ESC}, $B_t = B_t\left(s_{t-1},h_t\right)$ the common value of $B_t^i$, and $f$ the cost of \eqref{\refmodeleq:esc:AB}: inherited savings are more dispersed than income by the flat benefit's crowding-out of low types' saving, and the scalar $a_t$ is all the state the retirees' term carries. Under log preferences $B_t=\beta$ and $a_t$ is a function of $\tau_t$ alone; under CRRA it moves with hours, and hours with the candidate design.

The electorate at $t$ takes the predetermined states $\left(s_{t-1},D_t\right)$ as given. Both are the savings decisions of the previous generation; that those were made in anticipation of the policy chosen at $t$ pins their equilibrium value, through consistency, without making either of them a choice at $t$. For the tax this is enforced by construction: $z_t$ is the derivative at a frozen distribution, the distribution is substituted afterwards, and the candidates are compared at frozen distributions (\cref{\refsec:RobustRoot}). For the design the same discipline can be carried in either of two representations. \Cref{\refalg:esc:crra2D} evaluates the objective directly, as the convention of this section has it, and so imposes the discipline on the comparison itself: every candidate design, and the tax it would be paired with, is valued at one $a_t$, and $a_t$ is closed afterwards by consistency. \Cref{\refalg:esc:crra2Dfoc} carries it in a first order condition for the design, constructed as the tax one is. Both compute the equilibria of the following definition; the first is the production solver. A policy $\left(\hat{\tau},\hat{\theta}^{\prime}\right)$ and a scalar $a$ form an equilibrium at the state if
\begin{subequations}\label{\refeq:esc:stateEq}
\begin{align}
    \hat{\tau} &= \arg\max_{\tau\in[l,u]}\ \mathcal{W}_t\left(\tau,\hat{\theta}^{\prime};\,a\right), \label{\refeq:esc:stateEq:tax}\\
    \hat{\theta}^{\prime} &= \arg\max_{\theta^{\prime}\in[0,1]}\ \mathcal{V}_t\left(\theta^{\prime};a\right), \qquad \mathcal{V}_t\left(\theta^{\prime};a\right)\equiv\max_{\tau\in[l,u]}\ \mathcal{W}_t\left(\tau,\theta^{\prime};\,a\right), \label{\refeq:esc:stateEq:design}\\
    a &= a_t\left(\hat{\tau},\,h_t\left(\hat{\tau},\hat{\theta}^{\prime}\right);\,\theta_t\right), \label{\refeq:esc:stateEq:consistency}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\mathcal{W}_t\left(\tau,\theta^{\prime};a\right)$ is the CRRA form of \eqref{\refeq:esc:leadedObjective}, with $\upsilon_{1,t}^i = \left(\hat{c}_{1,t}^i\right)^{1-1/\rho}/(1-1/\rho)$ of \eqref{\refeq:hatc1i} and $\upsilon_{2,t}^i = \left(c_{2,t}^i\right)^{1-1/\rho}/(1-1/\rho)$, evaluated at the date-$t$ equilibrium at $\left(\tau,\theta^{\prime}\right)$ --- hours, savings and the continuation read off the period-$(t+1)$ policies at $\left(s_t,\theta^{\prime}\right)$, all of which move with the candidate and are meant to --- and with $c_{2,t}^i$ formed from the frozen $a$ rather than from the shares consistent with $\left(\tau,\theta^{\prime}\right)$. In \eqref{\refeq:esc:stateEq:design} the tax is re-optimised at every candidate design at the \emph{same} $a$: that is the payoff of the deviation, since an electorate that chose another design would set the tax for that design against the savings it inherited. Condition \eqref{\refeq:esc:stateEq:tax} at \eqref{\refeq:esc:stateEq:consistency} is the equilibrium test \eqref{\refeq:equilibriumTest} of the tax rule, so the two rules are one definition; what \eqref{\refeq:esc:stateEq} adds is that the design is tested at the same frozen state. Since $a$ is a scalar, \eqref{\refeq:esc:stateEq} is a scalar fixed point at every state: the best responses \eqref{\refeq:esc:stateEq:tax}--\eqref{\refeq:esc:stateEq:design} are well defined at any $a$, and \eqref{\refeq:esc:stateEq:consistency} is one equation in one unknown. Under log preferences neither $a_t$ nor the tax sees the candidate, the fixed point is the tax rule's own, and \eqref{\refeq:esc:stateEq} collapses to \cref{\refalg:esc:leaded}.

\begin{alg}\label{\refalg:esc:crra2D}
\emph{Leaded choice, CRRA preferences.} Let $\mathcal{S}\times\Theta$ be a grid over the state $\left(s_{t-1},\theta_t\right)$ and $\Theta^{\prime}$ a grid of candidate designs. Solve the terminal period as in \cref{\refsec:PEE} once per $\theta_T\in\Theta$; it has no design to choose. Then, backwards over $t$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{States.} For every $\left(\tau_t, s_{t-1}, \theta^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{S}\times\Theta^{\prime}$, resolve the equilibrium state $s_t$ from the state residual \eqref{\refeq:stateResidual}, with the continuation $\left(\tau_{t+1}, h_{t+1}\right)$ read off the period-$(t+1)$ policy interpolants at $\left(s_t, \theta^{\prime}\right)$; then $h_t$, $B_t$, and the young's terms of $z_t$ and of $\mathcal{W}_t$, including the numerical $\tau$-derivatives of \cref{\refalg:CRRA:grid}. None of these involves $\theta_t$ or $a$: the inherited design and distribution enter the retirees' term alone, which is closed form in $\left(\tau_t,h_t;\theta_t,a\right)$ and is the only term rebuilt below.
    \item \emph{The range of the state.} At each $\left(s_{t-1},\theta_t\right)\in\mathcal{S}\times\Theta$, evaluate the consistent $a_t$ of \eqref{\refeq:esc:aDef} at every $\left(\tau_t,\theta^{\prime}\right)$ of the policy grid, and let $\mathcal{A}$ be a grid of $M_a$ nodes ($M_a = 5$) spanning its range, extended by one cell at each end. A fixed point of \eqref{\refeq:esc:stateEq} lies in that range, since the consistent $a_t$ at any policy does.
    \item \emph{Best responses at a frozen state.} For each $a\in\mathcal{A}$ and each candidate $\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta^{\prime}$, assemble $z_t\left(\cdot\,,\theta^{\prime};a\right)$ along $\mathcal{T}$ with the retirees' shares frozen at $a$, and take $\hat{\tau}\left(\theta^{\prime};a\right)$ as the maximiser over $\mathcal{T}$ of its integral --- the profile \eqref{\refeq:objectiveProfile} with $D_c$ replaced by $a$. With $a$ frozen nothing moves along the grid, the integral is the objective up to a constant, and no equilibrium test is needed; an interior maximiser is located at the crossing of $\hat{z}_t$ in the cell adjacent to the maximising node where it changes sign (the nearer one if both), which is the crossing nearest that node. Set $\mathcal{V}_t\left(\theta^{\prime};a\right)$ to $\mathcal{W}_t$ at $\left(\hat{\tau}\left(\theta^{\prime};a\right),\theta^{\prime};a\right)$, read from the grid by the quadratic through the three nodes nearest the located tax, as the equilibrium test reads $\mathcal{V}_c(\tau_c)$. Then $\hat{\theta}^{\prime}(a)$ is the maximiser of $\mathcal{V}_t\left(\cdot\,;a\right)$ over $\Theta^{\prime}$, refined parabolically when interior and reported as a corner when not, and $\hat{\tau}(a)$ is $\hat{\tau}\left(\cdot\,;a\right)$ interpolated linearly along the candidate axis at $\hat{\theta}^{\prime}(a)$.
    \item \emph{Closing the state.} Form the residual
    \begin{align}\label{\refeq:esc:aResidual}
        r(a) \equiv a - a_t\left(\hat{\tau}(a),\,h_t\left(\hat{\tau}(a),\hat{\theta}^{\prime}(a)\right);\,\theta_t\right), \qquad a\in\mathcal{A},
    \end{align}
    with $h_t$ at the off-grid policy interpolated from step 1. Bracket every sign change of $r$ on $\mathcal{A}$ and close it by a bracketed secant (false position with the Illinois modification, bisection where a step would leave the bracket or the bracket stalls), re-running step 3 at each trial $a$, until the bracket is narrower than $10^{-9}$; it is a root if $\left|r\right|$ there is below $10^{-6}$. Since $r$ is close to linear this takes four to six iterations. A bracket that does not close --- a jump of $\hat{\theta}^{\prime}(a)$ across zero, which happens where the maximiser of $\mathcal{V}_t$ switches between two separated local maxima as $a$ moves --- contains no fixed point and is discarded with a flag. Each closed root is an equilibrium \eqref{\refeq:esc:stateEq} at the state. When there are several, select the one with the highest $\mathcal{W}_t$ in its own equilibrium, the rule of \cref{\refsec:RobustRoot}, and report at every state and period the number of brackets and the number closed. Should none close, flag the state and let the earlier criterion --- each candidate design valued at its own consistent state --- stand in for it, so that the recursion is not stopped by one state.
    \item \emph{Hand-back.} At the selected root $a^{\ast}$ set $\Theta_t\left(s_{t-1},\theta_t\right) = \hat{\theta}^{\prime}\left(a^{\ast}\right)$ and $\mathcal{T}_t\left(s_{t-1},\theta_t\right) = \hat{\tau}\left(a^{\ast}\right)$; re-solve the state once at these policies; smooth $\mathcal{T}_t$ along $s_{t-1}$ with pinned knots as in \cref{\refalg:CRRA:grid}; tabulate $\left(\tau_t,\theta_{t+1},s_t,h_t\right)$ and rebuild the interpolants for period $t-1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{alg}

One backward pass --- no iteration across periods and no convergence tolerance beyond the bisection's. Four points about the construction.

\emph{The fixed point is closed by a root, not by iteration.} Iterating $a\mapsto a_t\left(\hat{\tau}(a),h_t(\cdot)\right)$ from a seed, as \cref{\refalg:esc:permanent} does, finds one fixed point and says nothing about others. Tabulating $r$ on $\mathcal{A}$ shows every crossing, and the count is what says whether the selection among equilibria ever bound --- the same reporting the tax rule carries. A coarse $\mathcal{A}$ suffices, since $r$ is close to linear: $a_t$ responds to $a$ only through the response of the chosen policy to the distribution it inherits, which is weak, and the closing supplies the precision. Measured (the pilot of 2026-10-02, $\rho = 0.5$ and $2$, the baseline and the French-voting counterfactual, one period at the published resolution and full recursions at $50$ state nodes): one bracket at every state of every run, every bracket closed, $\left|r\right| \leq 3.5\times10^{-10}$ at the root, and no fallback. Where a multiplicity occurs it is intrinsic, not numerical: savers who expect a more Bismarckian design save in a way that can make that design attractive, and the selection by payoff dominance is the assumption about expectations that \cref{\refsec:RobustRoot} states, applied to the design as it is to the tax.

\emph{Nothing in steps 2--4 calls the state fixed point or the splines.} With $a$ frozen every term of $z_t$ and $\mathcal{W}_t$ is either on the grid from step 1 (the young's, and every numerical derivative) or closed form (the retirees'), so the loop over $\mathcal{A}$ and the bisection are closed-form passes over the grid, and the period's cost is that of step 1 plus $M_a$ and some thirty such passes. The earlier solver re-solved the state at every candidate's tax to evaluate the objective there; that re-solve is now done once, at the selected policy, and the value it returns is the check on the grid reading of $\mathcal{V}_t$ in step 3. Measured on the published grid ($\rho = 2$, $150$ state nodes, $41$ candidates): $126$ frozen passes per period at $0.40$ s each, and the choice layer costs $1.06$ times the earlier layer's; reading $\mathcal{V}_t$ from the grid instead of re-solving moves the design by $2.6\times10^{-6}$ at the state on the equilibrium path and by at most $6.5\times10^{-3}$ at the worst state ($\rho = 2$, French voting, $50$ state nodes).

\emph{Pinned periods.} At a period where the design is history rather than a choice (every $t\leq t_0$ in the baseline timing), $\Theta^{\prime}$ collapses to the inherited design and \eqref{\refeq:esc:stateEq} reduces to the tax rule at that design --- the fixed point in $a$ is the one the equilibrium test of \cref{\refsec:RobustRoot} already closes --- so steps 2--4 are skipped and the period is solved as in \cref{\refalg:CRRA:grid} along the $\theta_t$ axis. Pinning is built into the recursion rather than the simulation because $\tau_t$ responds to $\theta_{t+1}$ under CRRA. Pinned everywhere, the recursion collapses to the exogenous-design solver of \cref{\refsec:PEE} on a redundant grid, and reproduces its tax path to $\max_t\left|\Delta\tau_t\right| = 3.2\times10^{-5}$ --- a check of every ingredient except the choice layer against the production solver, which does not involve the design comparison and stands.

\emph{What the earlier version did, and the status of the numbers.} The solver that produced the CRRA rows of the endogenous-design results valued each candidate design at its own consistent distribution: it maximised $\mathcal{W}_t\left(\hat{\tau}(\theta^{\prime}),\theta^{\prime};\,a_t\left(\hat{\tau}(\theta^{\prime}),h_t(\theta^{\prime})\right)\right)$ over $\theta^{\prime}$, with $\hat{\tau}(\theta^{\prime})$ the consistent tax at that candidate. At an interior tax the derivative of that function is $\partial_{\theta^{\prime}}\mathcal{W}_t\big|_a + \partial_a\mathcal{W}_t\cdot\mathrm{d}a_t/\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime}$, and the second term is a gain from moving last period's savings that the electorate cannot obtain. It runs through $\mathrm{d}\ln B_t/\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime} = (\rho-1)(1-\alpha)\,\mathrm{d}\ln h_t/\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime}$ and through the tax response, both small, and $\partial_a\mathcal{W}_t$ is a transfer of savings between rich and poor retirees valued at their marginal utilities and propensities to vote; it vanishes as $\rho\rightarrow1$, and its sign may differ between $\rho=0.5$ and $\rho=2$. Under log preferences the two constructions coincide, since neither $a_t$ nor the tax sees the candidate. The solver implements \cref{\refalg:esc:crra2D} (\texttt{LeadedCRRA2D}, \texttt{designRule = 'root'}; the earlier construction is kept as \texttt{'legacy'} for comparisons). In the pilot that decided it ($50$ state nodes, $41$ candidates, every period choosing) the design in force at $t_0$ moved from $0.7383$ to $0.7385$ at $\rho = 2$, from $0.3347$ to $0.3366$ at the French-voting counterfactual at $\rho = 2$ and from $0.7364$ to $0.7334$ at $\rho = 0.5$, while across states the two layers differ by up to $0.03$. The CRRA rows of the endogenous-design results are now those of \cref{\refalg:esc:crra2D} (the final run of 2026-10-03; the size of the correction is item 18 of \cref{\refsec:ESC:checks}), and the numbers of this subsection measured with the earlier construction are marked as such.

\smalltitle{Resolution, measured with the earlier comparison.} At the calibrated cost at $\rho=2$, the choice at $t_0$ was $0.759$ against the path iteration's $0.761$, and the tax at $t_0$ landed on its target (drifts of $3.2\times10^{-4}$ in $\tau$ and $3.1\times10^{-4}$ in $R$). Away from the calibrated design the objective, not the method, limits the resolution: at the French-voting counterfactual at $\rho = 2$ it differs by $10^{-5}$ between designs of $0.27$ and $0.29$ while the corners lie $0.02$ and $0.09$ below its maximum, so the reported design is resolved by the candidate grid $\Theta^{\prime}$. With $13$, $21$, $41$ and $81$ candidates the recursion returned $0.285$, $0.273$, $0.263$ and $0.262$ there. The state grid is immaterial (the choice moves by $3\times10^{-5}$ between $50$ and $150$ nodes) while the design-\emph{state} grid is not ($7$ nodes misplace the choice by $0.02$; $13$ suffice). The tables are produced with $41$ candidates, at which the choice had settled to $10^{-3}$, and the cost is calibrated by scanning on the $50$-node state grid and refining the bracketed root on the $150$-node one. Flatness of this order is also why the frozen comparison matters: a tilt of $10^{-5}$ across $0.02$ of design is what the term $\partial_a\mathcal{W}_t\cdot\mathrm{d}a_t/\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime}$ has to be compared with, so a tilt of that order can move the chosen design by hundredths. Under \cref{\refalg:esc:crra2D} the candidate grid moves the design by at most $0.006$ between $41$ and $81$ candidates (the maximum over states; $\rho = 2$, French voting, $50$ state nodes) and the design-state grid by $6\times10^{-4}$ between $13$ and $21$ nodes at the state on the path, so the published settings stand; the final run on them is read in \cref{\refsec:ESC:checks}.

\smalltitle{The path iteration.} A cheaper construction approximates \cref{\refalg:esc:crra2D} by solving the equilibrium \emph{path} rather than the policy functions, re-solving the whole politico-economic equilibrium at each candidate design:

\begin{alg}\label{\refalg:esc:crraPath}
\emph{Leaded choice, CRRA preferences, path iteration approximation.} Given a design path $\bm{\theta}$ with $\theta_t$ fixed at the incumbent value for $t\leq t_0$: sweep forward over $t \geq t_0$; at each $t$, evaluate $\mathcal{W}_t$ at every candidate $\theta_{t+1}\in\Theta^{\prime}$, re-solving the whole politico-economic equilibrium of \cref{\refalg:CRRA:grid} for each candidate while holding $\theta_{t+2},\dots$ at the current iterate; replace $\theta_{t+1}$ by the maximiser. Repeat the sweep until the path stops moving.
\end{alg}

Re-solving at every candidate is what makes the envelope logic right for the tax: $\tau_t$ is re-optimised at each design, so its own response to $\theta_{t+1}$ contributes nothing to first order and the objective is evaluated at the equilibrium tax rather than a stale one. The construction approximates \cref{\refalg:esc:crra2D} on two counts, of different kinds.

\emph{The continuation is held fixed.} Holding $\theta_{t+2}$ fixed while $\theta_{t+1}$ varies is exact if the choice at $t+1$ does not respond to the design it inherits. Under log preferences that is \cref{prop:esc:separability} and no approximation at all. Under CRRA it is an approximation, measured directly: perturbing $\theta_{t+1}$ and re-optimising $\theta_{t+2}$ at each perturbation gives $\mathrm{d}\theta_{t+2}/\mathrm{d}\theta_{t+1}$ of $-0.0085$ at $\rho=2$, two orders of magnitude below the movements being studied. This part vanishes as the grids of \cref{\refalg:esc:crra2D} are refined and the policy function is used in place of the path.

\emph{The predetermined states move with the candidate.} Each candidate is valued on its own re-solved path, so the savers of every date before $t$ anticipate it: both the distribution $a_t$ and the level $s_{t-1}$ differ across candidates, which is the discipline that \eqref{\refeq:esc:stateEq} imposes and the path iteration does not. This part does not vanish with any grid. The earlier exact solver let the distribution move in the same way, so the measured gap between the two methods (\cref{\refsec:ESC:checks}: up to $0.09$ at the French-voting counterfactuals at $\rho=2$ against $0.003$ at the baseline) reflects the continuation and the level, not the distribution; the gap to \cref{\refalg:esc:crra2D} as stated is re-measured in the final run and carries all three.

\smalltitle{Validation against the log limit.} The path iteration has no closed form to check against, so it is held to continuity: as $\rho\rightarrow1$ its answer must converge on the log solver's, and at a first-order rate, since nothing in the problem is kinked at $\rho=1$. Holding the cost parameter and the calibration fixed, the gap is $0.0903$, $0.0448$ and $0.0188$ at $\rho = 1.10$, $1.05$ and $1.02$, so the ratio $\text{gap}/(\rho-1)$ is $0.90$, $0.90$, $0.94$. The two solvers share the weights of \eqref{\refeq:esc:leadedObjective} and nothing else --- one maximises a closed form on a state grid by backward recursion, the other iterates on a path of full equilibrium solves --- so their agreement in the limit is informative. The same test applies to \cref{\refalg:esc:crra2D}, whose correction to the earlier comparison vanishes at $\rho=1$ by construction, and is one of the CRRA items of \cref{\refsec:ESC:checks}.


\smalltitle{The design by its first order condition.} \Cref{\refalg:esc:crra2D} locates the design by evaluating $\mathcal{V}_t$ directly and closes the state by a root in $a$. The same equilibria can be located as the tax is: from a first order condition for the design, constructed at a frozen state and evaluated at the consistent one, with the corners and the crossings as candidates and each candidate tested at its own frozen state. The reason this section evaluated the design objective directly --- that a corner is invisible to a root finder --- does not apply to the selection rule of \cref{\refsec:RobustRoot}, which carries the corners as candidates; what direct evaluation saves is one numerical derivative.

\emph{The condition.} Write $\hat{\tau}\left(\theta^{\prime}\right)$ for the tax that the rule of \cref{\refsec:RobustRoot} selects at the design $\theta^{\prime}$ --- the candidates of the consistent $z_t$, tested at their own shares --- and $a\left(\theta^{\prime}\right) \equiv a_t\left(\hat{\tau}(\theta^{\prime}),h_t\left(\hat{\tau}(\theta^{\prime}),\theta^{\prime}\right);\theta_t\right)$ for the consistent state there. At the tax best response $\hat{\tau}\left(\theta^{\prime};a\right)$, interior or at a corner that stays binding, the envelope theorem gives
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:esc:designEnvelope}
    \partial_{\theta^{\prime}}\mathcal{V}_t\left(\theta^{\prime};a\right) = \partial_{\theta^{\prime}}\mathcal{W}_t\left(\tau,\theta^{\prime};a\right)\Big|_{\tau = \hat{\tau}(\theta^{\prime};a)},
\end{align}
the partial derivative at a fixed tax and a fixed state, so the design's consistent first order condition is
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:esc:designFOC}
    z_t^{\theta}\left(\theta^{\prime}\right) \equiv \partial_{\theta^{\prime}}\mathcal{W}_t\left(\tau,\theta^{\prime};a\right)\Big|_{\tau = \hat{\tau}(\theta^{\prime}),\ a = a(\theta^{\prime})},
\end{align}
the exact analogue of \eqref{\refeq:consistentFOC}: a derivative at a frozen state with the consistent state substituted afterwards, which by the same argument is the derivative of no single function of $\theta^{\prime}$, so that its crossings locate candidates and nothing more. In $\partial_{\theta^{\prime}}\mathcal{W}_t$ the young's terms see the candidate through $h_t$, $s_t$, $\Gamma_{s,t}$ and the continuation read at $\left(s_t,\theta^{\prime}\right)$, and are differentiated numerically along $\Theta^{\prime}$ as they are along $\mathcal{T}$ in \cref{\refalg:CRRA:grid}; the retirees' term sees it through hours alone, $\partial_{\theta^{\prime}}\ln c_{2,t}^i = (1-\alpha)\,\partial_{\theta^{\prime}}\ln h_t$ at fixed $\left(\tau_t,a\right)$, a closed form given the numerical $\partial_{\theta^{\prime}}\ln h_t$.

\begin{alg}\label{\refalg:esc:crra2Dfoc}
\emph{Leaded choice, CRRA preferences, by first order conditions.} Grids and the terminal period as in \cref{\refalg:esc:crra2D}. Backwards over $t$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{States and derivatives.} Step 1 of \cref{\refalg:esc:crra2D}, and in addition the numerical derivatives along $\Theta^{\prime}$ of $\ln h_t$ and $\ln\hat{c}_{1,t}^i$, smoothed with pinned knots as the $\tau$-derivatives are.
    \item \emph{The tax at each design.} For every $\left(s_{t-1},\theta_t,\theta^{\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{S}\times\Theta\times\Theta^{\prime}$, select $\hat{\tau}\left(\theta^{\prime}\right)$ by the rule of \cref{\refsec:RobustRoot} along $\mathcal{T}$, and form $a\left(\theta^{\prime}\right)$ with $h_t$ interpolated along $\tau$.
    \item \emph{The design condition.} Assemble $z_t^{\theta}$ of \eqref{\refeq:esc:designFOC} on $\Theta^{\prime}$: the young's derivative of step 1 interpolated along $\tau$ at $\hat{\tau}\left(\theta^{\prime}\right)$, and the retirees' term at $a\left(\theta^{\prime}\right)$.
    \item \emph{Candidates and the test.} The candidates are $\left\lbrace 0,1\right\rbrace$ and the crossings of the piecewise-linear interpolant of $z_t^{\theta}$ through the nodes of $\Theta^{\prime}$, each with its own state $a_c = a\left(\theta^{\prime}_c\right)$ and tax $\hat{\tau}_c = \hat{\tau}\left(\theta^{\prime}_c\right)$, both interpolated linearly along $\Theta^{\prime}$. For each candidate freeze $a_c$: for every $\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta^{\prime}$ take the tax best response $\hat{\tau}\left(\theta^{\prime};a_c\right)$ as in step 3 of \cref{\refalg:esc:crra2D}, and integrate the frozen derivative \eqref{\refeq:esc:designEnvelope} at $a_c$ along $\Theta^{\prime}$ by the trapezoidal rule,
    \begin{align}\label{\refeq:esc:designProfile}
        \mathcal{V}^{c}\left(\theta^{\prime}\right) \equiv \mathcal{V}_t\left(0;a_c\right) + \int_0^{\theta^{\prime}}\partial_{x}\mathcal{W}_t\left(\hat{\tau}\left(x;a_c\right),x;a_c\right)dx, \qquad \theta^{\prime}\in\Theta^{\prime},
    \end{align}
    which is $\mathcal{V}_t\left(\cdot\,;a_c\right)$ up to the smoothing of the derivatives, the same object the tax test integrates. The candidate is an equilibrium if $\mathcal{V}^{c}\left(\theta^{\prime}_c\right) \geq \max_{\Theta^{\prime}}\mathcal{V}^{c}$, read as \eqref{\refeq:equilibriumTest} is. Among several, select the highest $\mathcal{W}_t$ in its own equilibrium; report the number of candidates and of equilibria at every state and period; flag a state with none and let the earlier criterion stand in for it.
    \item \emph{Hand-back.} Step 5 of \cref{\refalg:esc:crra2D}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{alg}

The two algorithms compute the same set. An equilibrium of \eqref{\refeq:esc:stateEq} with an interior design is a zero of $z_t^{\theta}$, by \eqref{\refeq:esc:designEnvelope} at $a = a^{\ast}$; with a corner design it is a corner candidate; and a candidate that passes the test of step 4 satisfies \eqref{\refeq:esc:stateEq} at $a_c$ by construction. With the same selection the two return the same policy, up to the resolution of a crossing against a parabola and of the integrated profile against the direct reading, and that agreement is a check on both. What separates them is cost and exposure. \Cref{\refalg:esc:crra2Dfoc} needs one further numerical derivative, through the continuation interpolants along the candidate axis, whose kinks at the nodes of $\Theta$ the smoothing has to remove; in return it runs one frozen pass per candidate, typically three or four, instead of the $M_a$ and the bisection's of \cref{\refalg:esc:crra2D}, and it locates the design from a condition rather than from a parabola through grid values, which on an objective as flat as the one measured below is the more honest instrument. Under log preferences both reduce to \cref{\refalg:esc:leaded}, and the same first order condition route is open there. The comparison was run (2026-10-02; both layers on one shared step 1, $\rho = 0.5$ and $2$, the baseline and the French-voting counterfactual). \Cref{\refalg:esc:crra2Dfoc} costs $0.8$ to $1.5$ times the earlier layer and is the least sensitive to the candidate and design-state grids, but its fixed-knot derivative along $\Theta^{\prime}$ misses a central finite difference of the directly evaluated young's term by $18$ to $23$ percent --- the continuation interpolants are piecewise linear over the $13$ nodes of $\Theta$, a kink every three or four candidate cells, which the smoother averages through with a bias --- the design it selects fails the one-shot deviation test at its own frozen state by up to $1.6\times10^{-5}$ relative where \cref{\refalg:esc:crra2D}'s passes at $10^{-8}$, and its design in force at $t_0$ sits $0.023$ below \cref{\refalg:esc:crra2D}'s at the $\rho = 2$ baseline. \Cref{\refalg:esc:crra2D} is the production solver; \cref{\refalg:esc:crra2Dfoc} is kept in the sandbox module (\texttt{policyESCpilot.py}) as the documented alternative, and its lesson is recorded (\texttt{notes/crossCuttingFindings.md}, item 19).


\subsubsection{Calibrating the cost}\label{\refsec:ESC:calibration}
Three targets and three parameters. $\left(\beta,\omega\right)$ are calibrated to $\left(R_{t_0},\tau_{t_0}\right)$ exactly as in \cref{\refsec:calibration}, with the cost installed and the design held at $\theta^{\ast}$; and $\lambda$ in \eqref{\refmodeleq:esc:AB} is chosen so that the design \emph{in force} at the baseline year, on a path where the political choice binds from the first period of the horizon, is the observed one,
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:esc:calibration}
    \theta_{t_0} = \Theta_{t_0-1}\left(\theta_{t_0-1}\right) = \theta^{\ast}.
\end{align}
Read in words: the model's own political history delivers the observed system by the baseline year. Everything after $t_0$ is then a prediction, and the drift of the design as $\nu_t$ falls is the object of interest. In the code $\lambda$ occupies the slot that the exponent of the previous wedge used (\texttt{wedgeP}; the column \texttt{p} of the result files), so that no schema or merge key changed with the specification; the paper prints it as $\lambda$.

\smalltitle{Why the design in force and not the choice made.} The design is a state chosen one period earlier, so $\theta_{t_0}$ and $\Theta_{t_0}(\theta^{\ast})$ are different numbers: the first was picked under $\nu_{t_0-1}$, the second binds only at $t_0+1$. They would coincide if the policy function were time invariant, and $\nu_t$ is not. The distinction is forced by what the counterfactuals report. Those are equilibrium paths read at $t_0$ (\cref{\refsec:ESC:counterfactuals}), so the baseline path must sit on the observed design \emph{at} $t_0$ or every comparison in the tables is drawn against a number the model missed. Under log preferences \cref{prop:esc:separability} makes $\Theta_{t_0-1}$ constant in its argument, so \eqref{\refeq:esc:calibration} does not depend on the inherited $\theta_{t_0-1}$ and no fixed point in the design's own history arises; under CRRA the residual is evaluated with the rest of the path held at $\theta^{\ast}$, exactly as elsewhere in this section, which is again exact at the root. The two targets are close --- under the previous wedge, calibrating on the choice at $t_0$ instead gives $p = 0.402$ against $0.408$ at $\rho=1$, and a design at $t_0$ of $0.727$ against the observed $0.738$ --- so what the choice of target buys is not a different economics but a baseline row that reproduces its own datum.

\smalltitle{The nesting is an approximation, and is reported as one.} $\left(\beta,\omega\right)$ are calibrated along the path with the design held fixed, not along the endogenous-design path. At the calibrated cost the two agree at $t_0$ and $t_0+1$ by construction and drift only later, so the error is second order --- but it is an error, and the solved endogenous path is therefore checked back against the $\left(R,\tau\right)$ targets it was calibrated to. The residuals were $9\times10^{-15}$ and $2.6\times10^{-9}$ under the previous wedge, which is what licenses treating the two blocks as separable; they are re-measured under \eqref{\refmodeleq:esc:AB} in \cref{\refsec:ESC:checks}.

This caveat belongs to the leaded timing alone. Under the permanent timing the chosen design is constant from $t_0$ on and, at the calibrated cost, equal to the incumbent one --- so the exogenous path $\left(\beta,\omega\right)$ are calibrated along \emph{is} the endogenous path, period for period, and the nesting is an identity rather than an approximation.

\smalltitle{The permanent residual is the fixed-point condition.} Equation \eqref{\refeq:esc:calibration} asks for the cost at which the electorate re-elects the system it has, and under the permanent timing that is exactly \eqref{\refeq:esc:permFixedPoint}: a cost at which the choice taken against the incumbent's savings ratio reproduces the incumbent design is a cost at which the incumbent design is the fixed point. The two therefore share a root, and the residual is evaluated in the cheaper single-pass form; only away from the root do they differ, so a scan must not mix them.

\smalltitle{Why \eqref{\refeq:esc:calibration} is scanned and not bracketed.} The residual is \emph{flat at a corner}: wherever the choice sits at $\theta=0$ or $\theta=1$ it does not move with $\lambda$ at all. A root finder handed such a bracket reports spurious convergence at whichever endpoint it happened to evaluate. We therefore scan $\lambda$ on a logarithmic grid, locate a genuine sign change, and only then bracket. A run that finds no interior crossing reports that fact rather than returning a number --- which is the correct outcome for France, whose observed design is itself at the boundary, and under \eqref{\refmodeleq:esc:AB} the expected one, since the marginal cost of the first unit of redistribution is zero and no finite $\lambda$ places the choice at $\theta=1$. It is also what happens if the scanned interval is simply placed wrong: the required cost spans an order of magnitude across $\rho$, so an interval chosen at $\rho=2$ lies entirely below the root at $\rho=0.5$, and the scan says so instead of converging on its own endpoint. The interval scanned is of order $[0.5,\,200]$ at $\rho=1$ and an order of magnitude lower at $\rho=2$; that the scan has exactly one sign change is itself one of the checks of \cref{\refsec:ESC:checks}.

\smalltitle{Two consequences of the identification.} Under \eqref{\refmodeleq:esc:AB} the target $\theta^{\ast}$ in \eqref{\refeq:esc:calibration} does not move with $\lambda$, so the residual is a function of the cost alone. Under the flat-only variant of the previous wedge it did: \eqref{\refmodeleq:esc:RR} made $\theta^{\ast}$ a function of the cost, the target was re-derived from $RR_0$ at every trial value --- the datum is the replacement-rate ratio, not the design --- and the inversion had to be bracketed by a scan, since $(1-\theta)/\theta$ is decreasing while $f$ is increasing and their product need not be monotone. Second, the required cost turns out to be nearly the same under all three timings, which is itself a useful check that the two solvers are solving the same economic problem.

\smalltitle{The calibrated cost across $\rho$.} Under CRRA the residual in \eqref{\refeq:esc:calibration} is evaluated with the future design held at $\theta^{\ast}$ while the single choice at $t_0$ varies --- exact at the root, where the held path \emph{is} the equilibrium path, and only an approximation away from it, where it merely has to locate the sign change. The calibrated cost falls steeply in the intertemporal elasticity: $\lambda = 18.24,\ 8.64,\ 1.73$ at $\rho = 0.5,\ 1,\ 2$ (the $\rho\neq1$ values from the earlier design comparison of \cref{\refsec:ESC:crra}, re-calibrated in the final run), at which the observed design loses a share $1-f(\theta^{\ast}) =$ $3.9\%,\ 1.8\%,\ 0.4\%$ of revenue and a flat system would lose $1-f(0) =$ $44\%,\ 24\%,\ 5\%$, with $\tilde{V} =$ $0.436$ in the US common-$X$ calibration. Under the previous wedge $p = 0.965,\ 0.408,\ 0.090$ (and $1.461,\ 0.709,\ 0.178$ under its flat-only variant, where $\theta^{\ast}$ moves with it: $0.690,\ 0.716,\ 0.733$). A higher elasticity strengthens the young's forward-looking stake in a Bismarckian design --- the same ordering the stake decomposition found --- so less friction is needed to keep the choice off the Beveridgean corner. The magnitude is the caveat of \cref{\refmodelsec:ESC}: $\lambda$ at $\rho=1$ is an order of magnitude above the $\varepsilon\Pi_t$ that participation and taxable-income elasticities of $0.1$ to $0.4$ would give, and is read as a calibrated stand-in for costs the model does not contain, not as an elasticity. All points are in the repository's \texttt{results/esc/escCalibration\{,CRRA\}.csv}, keyed on the specification so that the rows of the two costs coexist, produced by \texttt{python/US/runESC.py} and \texttt{runESCcrra.py} and consumed by the paper pipeline's \texttt{US\_ESC\_Calibration} table.


\subsubsection{The counterfactuals}\label{\refsec:ESC:counterfactuals}
Every counterfactual in this appendix --- and every one in the main text's US tables --- is a \emph{separate equilibrium path}, not a shock applied to the calibrated economy at the baseline year. The changed characteristics hold over the whole horizon, the economy begins at its own steady state rather than at the United States', the political choice binds from the first period, and the path is read at $t_0$. What a row describes is a country that has always had this mix of characteristics --- mostly American, partly French --- and the question the tables put is how far the observable characteristics carry the United States towards France.

\smalltitle{Why not an unanticipated reform at $t_0$.} An alternative construction dates the change at $t_0$, seeds the economy with the savings the baseline generation actually made, and so describes an economy surprised in 2020. That object is coherent, but it is not commensurable with France's own calibrated path, which is an equilibrium and not a surprise. Since the point of the French experiments is a comparison across countries, the paths have to be built the same way on both sides. The new-path construction has a second advantage: with the design pinned as history through $t_0$, a change dated $t_0$ could not move $\theta$ before $t_0+1$, so a table read at $t_0$ would be identical across the exogenous- and endogenous-design readings by construction. Here $\theta_{t_0}$ is itself an outcome, and 2020 carries the design response.

\smalltitle{What the convention costs, measured.} At $\rho=1$ the choice between the two constructions moves the average workweek and nothing else: the tax rate and the savings rate come out identical to every printed digit under both readings. Both are rate objects, and under log preferences with a Cobb--Douglas technology neither depends on the inherited capital stock, while the \emph{level} of hours responds to the wage and hence to $k_{t_0}$. This is a property of the log case and does not survive it --- under CRRA the tax responds to the state, and the $\rho\neq1$ rows move.

\smalltitle{Two readings.} Each counterfactual is reported both with the design held at the value the replacement-rate data imply under the changed characteristics, and with the design chosen. The two differ already at $t_0$, which is what makes a table read there informative. Alongside them the French tables carry France's own calibrated path. That row is not a counterfactual on the American model: France brings its own political weight $\omega$ as well as its own characteristics, so the distance between it and the all-characteristics row measures what the observables leave unexplained. Its workweek is a calibration target rather than a prediction, since the French calibration of \cref{\refsec:calibration} targets French hours relative to American ones, and is marked as such wherever it is printed.
\subsubsection{Checks}\label{\refsec:ESC:checks}
The claims of this section and of \cref{\refmodelsec:ESC} are measured, not assumed. Each item names the check and the pinned or expected value; the measured one is filled from the regression suite (\texttt{python/US/test\_esc.py}, which pins the first seven) or from the result files in \texttt{results/esc/}. Every value below at $\rho\neq1$ that involves the chosen design is from the final run under \cref{\refalg:esc:crra2D} (2026-10-03, \texttt{logs/finalRun1002/}) unless it is marked as measured with the earlier design comparison (the last implementation point of \cref{\refsec:ESC:crra}); the $\rho=1$ values do not involve the design layer.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Nesting.} At $\lambda=0$ the size-scaled cost reproduces the exogenous-design model bitwise: every tax, savings and hours path identical to the last digit. Measured: the largest deviation in the tax and in retirees' consumption is exactly zero.
    \item \emph{The comparison arm is untouched.} The calibrated $p = 0.4076119851$ of the previous wedge at $\rho=1$, and every number the regression suite pins for it, still reproduce. Measured: it does, and the slow suites that pin the previous wedge's CRRA and permanent-timing numbers pass.
    \item \emph{Decoupling.} $z_t$ does not see $\left(\tau_{t+1},\theta_{t+1}\right)$: on the unit square of $\left(\theta_t,\theta_{t+1}\right)$ the tax policy is constant along $\theta_{t+1}$. Measured: the variation is exactly zero.
    \item \emph{Separability.} The leaded choice is invariant to $s_{t-1}$ over two orders of magnitude (\cref{prop:esc:separability}). Measured: the choice moves by $2.6\times10^{-14}$, at an interior choice of $0.797$.
    \item \emph{The derivative of the retired.} The analytic $\mathrm{d}\ln c_{2,t}^i/\mathrm{d}\tau_t$ of \eqref{\refmodeleq:esc:dlnc2i} equals a central finite difference of $\ln c_{2,t}^i$ at fixed $s_{t-1,i}/s_{t-1}$ to $10^{-8}$ relative. Measured: $2.6\times10^{-10}$ at three points of $(\theta,\tau)$; dropping the factor $1+\tau\,\partial_{\tau}\ln f$ moves the derivative by at least $1.5\times10^{-3}$, so the factor is not negligible.
    \item \emph{$\tilde{V}$ invariances.} $\tilde{V}$ of \eqref{\refmodeleq:esc:Vtilde} is invariant to a common rescaling of $X_i$ and to one of $\eta_i$, is zero when every $y_i=1$, and $f\equiv1$ there. Measured: $-1.7\times10^{-16}$ under $X_i\times2.5$ and $0$ under $\eta_i\times1.7$; at $y_i=1$, $\tilde{V}=0$ and $\max|f-1|=0$ over $33$ points of $(\theta,\tau)$. In the US common-$X$ calibration $\tilde{V}=0.436$.
    \item \emph{Identification.} $\theta^{\ast}$ from \eqref{\refmodeleq:esc:RR} under the size-scaled cost is $0.7382263650$, unchanged from the exogenous-design model. Measured: $0.7382263650$.

Measured: one sign change at every $\rho$, between $7.6$ and $13.1$ under log preferences, $14.6$ and $25.1$ at $\rho=0.5$, and $0.98$ and $2.22$ at $\rho=2$.
