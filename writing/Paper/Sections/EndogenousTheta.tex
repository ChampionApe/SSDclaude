\section{Endogenous Pension Design}\label{sec:esc}
So far the Bismarckian index $\theta$ is calibrated while the tax rate is chosen politically. This section lets the electorate choose the design with the same objective $\mathcal{W}_t$, in the model without informal households, so one political process delivers the size of the system and its shape. The derivations are in appendix \ref{app:esc}, further results in appendix \ref{app:escDetails}, and the algorithms in the technical documentation \parencite{BergGE26doc}.

\subsection{The deadweight cost of redistribution}\label{sec:esc:cost}
Without a deadweight cost of redistribution the choice is a corner. An electorate that weighs retirees by their marginal utility wants all of the transfer the flat component performs: if propensities to vote do not rise with income, the design chosen with the tax is the Beveridgean corner, $\theta_t = 0$ (proposition \ref{prop:esc:corner} in appendix \ref{app:esc:corner}). Propensities to vote that rise with income, as in the U.S., could overturn it, but at the U.S.\ calibration every timing of the choice, with the tax, one period in advance or once and for all, gives a corner (appendix \ref{app:esc:corner}). The model lacks a mechanism that makes flat benefits costlier than earnings-related ones, and an interior design requires one.

The flat component redistributes within the retired cohort, and the transfer it performs is an implicit tax. A retiree of type $i$ receives $\Pi_t\tau_t\left[\theta_t + (1-\theta_t)/y_i\right]$ per unit of past earnings, where $y_i$ is the labor income of type $i$ relative to the mean and $\Pi_t \equiv \nu_t w_t h_t/(w_{t-1}h_{t-1})$ the pay-as-you-go return factor, against $\Pi_t\tau_t$ from an earnings-related system of the same revenue. The flat component therefore levies $t_i = \Pi_t\tau_t(1-\theta_t)\left(1-1/y_i\right)$ per unit of past earnings, equation \eqref{eq:esc:implicitTax} in appendix \ref{app:esc:cost}: a tax on retirees whose past earnings were above the mean, a subsidy to those below it, and a pure transfer in the aggregate. It is the tax component of the contribution in the sense of \textcite{Summers89a1}, the part of the payroll charge that buys the contributor no benefit. The model already contains one response to it. The flat benefit depends on aggregate hours and not on own hours, so the marginal wedge of the flat component is the same for every type, and hours respond to it with the Frisch elasticity through the labor supply function \eqref{eq:h}. What the model lacks is an extensive margin, whether to participate, to work formally or to report earnings, on which a worker compares the earnings at stake with what they buy, so that the average wedge $t_i$ is what matters, and it differs across types.

What such a wedge costs is the subject of a large literature. A tax costs resources beyond the revenue it raises, by a loss that is quadratic in the wedge \parencite{Harberger64a1}, and redistribution carries the same loss in Okun's image of the leaky bucket, in which part of what is carried from the rich to the poor does not arrive \parencite{Okun75a1}. The tax component of pension contributions acts as a tax on the extensive margin: \textcite{Disney04a1} splits contributions in OECD countries into a tax and a saving component and finds that the tax component lowers the participation of women while the saving component raises it, and \textcite{KumlerVF20a1} find that the underreporting of wages to Mexico's social security agency fell for the workers whose benefits a reform tied more closely to reported wages. On that margin the relevant wedge is the average tax on the earnings at stake rather than the marginal rate, and the cost of taxation is larger than its hours response alone would suggest \parencite{Saez02a1,KlevenK06a1}; the participation and taxable-income elasticities that measure it are of the order of $0.1$ to $0.4$ \parencite{ChettyGMW11a1,SaezSG12a1}. Finally, the size of a system is related to its design: \textcite{CondeRuizP07a1} document that more redistributive systems are smaller, and \textcite{KoethenbuergerPP08a1} account for it by an efficiency--redistribution trade-off in which flat benefits carry a larger efficiency cost per unit of size.

We take the ingredients of the cost from this literature and let it scale the revenue that reaches retirees, in the sense of Okun's bucket: part of what the flat component carries does not arrive. Per unit of mean past earnings the flat component carries $\tfrac{1}{2}\Pi_t\tau_t(1-\theta_t)M$, with $M \equiv \sum_i\gamma_i|y_i-1|$ the mean absolute deviation of relative income, since what is taken above mean income is what arrives below it (appendix \ref{app:esc:cost}). The leak has two parts. A constant share of every unit carried is Okun's leak proper, the administrative and political cost of the transfer rather than a behavioral response; it costs a share of revenue proportional to $(1-\theta_t)M$ and prices the first unit of redistribution. A share that rises with the load is the Harberger loss of the implicit taxes on the margins the model lacks: quadratic in each type's wedge and summed over types, it is a share proportional to $\tau_t(1-\theta_t)^2\tilde{V}$, with $\tilde{V} \equiv \sum_i\gamma_i(y_i-1)^2/y_i$ the dispersion of relative incomes, in which a given transfer counts for more against a smaller income, and it leaves the first unit of redistribution free at the margin. We scale both parts by the size of the system, so that a larger system is costlier to keep redistributive, the trade-off by which \textcite{KoethenbuergerPP08a1} account for the finding of \textcite{CondeRuizP07a1}, read from the other side. Taken out of revenue, the budget is
\begin{align}\label{eq:esc:budget}
    \nu_t w_t\tau_th_t\, f(\theta_t,\tau_t) &= \sum_i \gamma_i b_t^i, \\
    f(\theta_t,\tau_t) &= \exp\!\Big\{-\tau_t(1-\theta_t)\Big[\lambda_1 M + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda_2\tilde{V}(1-\theta_t)\Big]\Big\}, \nonumber
\end{align}
where $1-f$ is the share of revenue lost, to first order $\lambda_1\tau_t M(1-\theta_t) + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda_2\tau_t\tilde{V}(1-\theta_t)^2$, and $\lambda_1, \lambda_2 \geq 0$ are the cost parameters (appendix \ref{app:esc:cost} derives the form from the implicit tax).\footnote{The exponential form keeps $f$ positive at every size of the system and nests the model without the cost at $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = 0$. The factor $\tau_t$ on the linear term is a choice: a constant share of the amount carried is, as a share of revenue, independent of the size of the system, and we let it rise with the size as the quadratic share does, so that one factor serves every variant in the tax condition and a larger system lowers the cost parameter at which an electorate chooses the Bismarckian corner (appendix \ref{app:esc:cost}).} We compare three variants. The quadratic cost, $\lambda_1 = 0$, is the Harberger loss alone; the linear cost, $\lambda_2 = 0$, is Okun's leak alone; the linear-quadratic cost has both, written as a scale and a shape, $\lambda_1 = \lambda\kappa$ and $\lambda_2 = \lambda$, and is our preferred specification.

Three properties hold for every variant. Nothing is lost when nothing is redistributed: $f = 1$ at $\theta_t = 1$ and, for every design, when all incomes are equal, where $M$ and $\tilde{V}$ vanish together. The share lost rises with the size of the system, through the factor $\tau_t$. And the marginal cost of the first unit of redistribution is $\lambda_1\tau_t M$: zero under the quadratic cost, under which no electorate chooses the Bismarckian corner, and positive under the two with a linear term, under which an electorate chooses the corner once $\lambda_1$ exceeds a threshold that falls with the size of the system and rises with a flatter voting profile and a more compressed income distribution (proposition \ref{prop:esc:bismarckCorner}). The solution of sections \ref{sec:model} to \ref{sec:numerical} carries over with $f\theta_t$ and $f(1-\theta_t)$ in place of $\theta_t$ and $1-\theta_t$ (appendix \ref{app:esc:equilibrium}); the cost cancels from the replacement-rate ratio, so $\theta^{\ast} = 0.738$ is unchanged under every variant, and adds one factor to the tax condition, $1+\ln f$, as a larger system is worth less to a retiree at the margin than the revenue it collects. The design is chosen one period in advance, the Markov timing in which it moves labor supply and savings before it binds and the way benefit formulas are legislated; under log preferences the choice depends on neither the inherited design nor savings (proposition \ref{prop:esc:separability}). With the cost, a more Bismarckian design also raises the total the young will receive, which makes an interior choice possible; the variants differ in how fast the marginal cost falls as the design becomes more earnings-related, and so in how far a characteristic moves the chosen design.

The cost stands for the margins the model lacks, participation, formality and the reporting of earnings, and for the administrative and political costs of redistribution; its parameters are calibrated rather than derived. The quadratic one is larger than estimated elasticities would give, and the linear one has no elasticity benchmark (appendix \ref{app:esc:cost}); their discipline is the cross-country test of section \ref{sec:esc:calibration}.

\subsection{Calibration and a cross-country test}\label{sec:esc:calibration}
Table \ref{table:US_ESC:variants} reports the three calibrations. Under the quadratic and the linear cost one parameter is calibrated so that $\theta_{2020}$, the design chosen in 1990 on the equilibrium path with the choice binding from the first period, equals $\theta^{\ast}$, with $(\beta,\omega)$ recalibrated at every trial value. Under the linear-quadratic cost the scale and the shape are pinned jointly by the U.S.\ and the UK designs, the UK at its own income cuts as in section \ref{sec:oecd}, so that both electorates re-elect their observed designs under one pair $(\lambda,\kappa)$. The calibration pins 2020 only; every later design is a prediction. The preferred cost has $\lambda = 5.237$ and $\kappa = 0.129$, so a linear parameter of $0.674$; the linear cost has $\lambda_1 = 1.710$ and the quadratic $\lambda_2 = 8.643$. The share of revenue the observed U.S.\ design loses in 2020 rises with the linear term, $1.8\%$, $2.6\%$ and $3.6\%$ under the quadratic, the preferred and the linear cost, while the share a flat benefit of the same size would lose falls, $23.8\%$, $19.8\%$ and $13.2\%$ (\oa{esc-variants}). One datum pins each parameter, so the test of a specification rests on the economies not used: the UK and France under the one-parameter costs, France under the preferred one.

Under the U.S.\ parameters the UK (observed design $0.560$, tax rate $11.9\%$) chooses $0.625$ under the quadratic cost and the Beveridgean corner under the linear one, while France ($1.000$, $21.3\%$) chooses $0.749$ and the Bismarckian corner. Under the preferred cost, with the UK on its design by construction, France is predicted at $0.806$: interior, further above the U.S.\ design than under the quadratic cost, but short of its corner. The ordering of the three designs is the observed one under every variant.

The own parameters say what each design asks of the cost. The UK's is within $16\%$ of the U.S.\ value under the quadratic cost, $7.264$ against $8.643$, and $41\%$ above it under the linear one, $2.414$ against $1.710$. France's is a threshold, the smallest value at which its electorate chooses the corner: no finite value under the quadratic cost, since the first unit of redistribution is free at the margin; $1.312$ under the linear cost, below the U.S.\ value; and $\lambda \geq 9.793$ under the preferred cost at the U.S.\ shape, nearly twice the U.S.\ value. The UK's design thus pins a small linear term, under which France is predicted interior, and France's corner takes a linear term about twice as large: with the shape pinned by France's corner instead, $\kappa = 0.629$, the UK is predicted at $0.397$, far below its design (table \ref{table:US_ESC:variantsCountry} in appendix \ref{app:escDetails:calibration}). No pair of parameters that keeps the U.S.\ on its design reconciles the UK and France. The linear cost reaches France's corner at the U.S.\ parameter, but it is knife-edge: with no curvature in the leak, the chosen design responds to a characteristic as a switch, and the UK's electorate falls to the Beveridgean corner. What separates the UK from France is the size of the system, $11.9\%$ against $21.3\%$, as in figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}: the two have nearly the same dispersion of relative incomes, $0.223$ and $0.218$, about half the U.S.\ $0.436$, and a larger system lowers the threshold at which an electorate chooses the corner in proportion (proposition \ref{prop:esc:bismarckCorner}); under the preferred cost the amount France's flat component carries, $M = 0.422$ against the UK's $0.292$, adds to it. The UK regrouped at the U.S.\ income percentiles and the calibrations at $\rho \neq 1$ are in appendices \ref{app:escDetails:calibration} and \ref{app:escDetails:rho}.

\subsection{Ageing and inequality}\label{sec:esc:results}
We read three of the counterfactuals of section \ref{sec:oecd} twice (table \ref{table:US_ESC:results}), with the design pinned at the U.S.\ value and chosen, both under the cost, so their distance is the political response, one block per variant. On the baseline path (figure \ref{fig:US_ESC:path}) the chosen design rises with ageing under every variant. Under the preferred cost it rises from $0.738$ in 2020 to $0.824$ in 2050 and $0.850$ from 2110 on, tracking the fall in workers per retiree from $1.34$ to $1.07$, and the tax rate rises from $14.4\%$ to $20.8\%$, $0.3$ p.p.\ above the path with the design pinned, since a more Bismarckian system dissipates less and is worth more to the electorate at the margin. Under the quadratic cost the design reaches $0.809$, and under the linear cost the Bismarckian corner by 2050, after which the tax path carries no further design response.\footnote{Recalibrated at Frisch elasticities $\xi = 0.2$ and $0.4$ under the quadratic cost, $\lambda$ moves by about one percent and the tax in 2110 by about $0.3$ p.p.\ either way, while the design in 2110 is unchanged to the third decimal and the acute-ageing design within one unit of it (\oa{esc-path}).}

\smalltitle{Ageing.} Acute ageing, $\nu_t = 1$ throughout, raises the chosen design from $0.738$ to $0.874$ under the preferred cost. A scarcer young generation lifts the political weight of retirees, who are unanimous for a more earnings-related system since it increases the labor supply on which their benefits are levied (appendix \ref{app:esc:corner}). And the tax that ageing calls for, $22.3\%$ against $14.4\%$, raises the loss per unit of redistribution by more than half, since the loss is proportional to $\tau_t$. This channel supplies about half the response under the quadratic cost, where it can be switched off: under a cost on the design itself, which does not scale with the size of the system, the design rises only to $0.778$ against $0.825$ (\oa{esc-scale}). The chosen design adds $0.4$ p.p.\ to the pinned tax response of $7.9$ p.p., which is below the $8.7$ p.p.\ of section \ref{sec:oecd} because the cost makes a larger system worth less to retirees at the margin.

\smalltitle{Income distribution.} The model's inequality is the dispersion of relative incomes, not the Gini coefficient of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}: France's flatter distribution halves $\tilde{V}$, from $0.436$ to $0.218$, and lowers the amount the flat component carries, $M$, by a quarter, from $0.575$ to $0.422$. With the design pinned it lowers the tax from $14.4\%$ to $13.8\%$, less than the $1.2$ p.p.\ of section \ref{sec:oecd}, because a lower dispersion also lowers the share of revenue lost, and the tax moves as little under every variant, chosen or pinned. The design is another matter. Under the quadratic cost it moves to $0.772$, close to the observed one, since the cost is proportional to the same dispersion as the redistribution a flat benefit performs: compressing the distribution lowers the redistributive stakes and their cost together, and only the young's forward-looking stake, which does not scale with the dispersion, gains a little ground. Under the preferred cost it moves to $0.850$, nearly as far as under acute ageing. The linear term is weighted by the amount carried, which falls by a quarter where $\tilde{V}$ halves, so the cost of redistribution falls by less than the redistributive stake and the forward-looking stake gains more ground; and the linear term has no curvature, so the marginal cost of redistribution falls less as the design becomes more earnings-related than under the quadratic cost, whose curvature damps the response. The tax, $13.9\%$, is within $0.1$ p.p.\ of the pinned reading.

\smalltitle{Voting patterns.} France's much flatter voting profile moves the design the other way, to $0.622$, by about as much as its income distribution moves it up: when the poor carry more voting weight, the redistributive force of proposition \ref{prop:esc:corner} regains ground. The tax rises by $0.8$ p.p.\ in both readings. Combined with France's income distribution, the voting patterns prevail: the two together put the design at $0.641$, below the U.S.\ one, as under the quadratic cost, $0.627$ (table \ref{table:US_ESC:variantsShocks} in appendix \ref{app:escDetails:counterfactuals}).

As in the cross-section of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}, ageing moves the chosen design a long way toward earnings-related benefits, and the size of the system with it, under every variant. Inequality moves the size of the system little under every variant, but it moves the design: under the preferred cost a compressed income distribution moves it about as far as ageing, toward earnings-related benefits, and France's voting patterns as far the other way. The weak relation between inequality and the design in the cross-section is matched by the quadratic cost, under which the design moves little, and under the preferred cost by the composite of France's income distribution and voting patterns, the pair the cross-section confronts, rather than by the income distribution alone.

The other two variants show what each ingredient contributes. The Harberger curvature alone, the quadratic cost, damps every response, since its marginal cost falls as the design becomes more earnings-related: acute ageing moves the design to $0.825$, France's income distribution to $0.772$ and its voting patterns to $0.653$, and France's corner is out of reach. Okun's leak alone, the linear cost, prices the first unit of redistribution but has no curvature, so the chosen design responds to a characteristic as a switch: acute ageing and France's income distribution send it to the Bismarckian corner, France's voting patterns to $0.047$, next to the Beveridgean one, and the UK's electorate under the U.S.\ parameter to the Beveridgean corner itself (section \ref{sec:esc:calibration}). Together, the leak lets a characteristic move the design further and makes France's corner reachable at a finite parameter, while the curvature keeps the choice interior. The scaling by the size of the system, common to the three, is what carries the ageing response through the tax (\oa{esc-scale}). Mild ageing, the composites, the savings rate and the workweek under each variant are in appendix \ref{app:escDetails:counterfactuals}.

\smalltitle{The effect of the intertemporal elasticity of substitution.} The linear and the linear-quadratic costs are computed under log preferences only; the quadratic cost is calibrated at each elasticity (appendix \ref{app:escDetails:rho}). Its parameter falls from $18.267$ at $\rho = 0.5$ to $1.724$ at $\rho = 2$, as a higher elasticity strengthens the forward-looking channels against the Beveridgean corner; at $\rho = 2$ it is closest to what estimated elasticities imply (appendix \ref{app:esc:cost}), adding to the case of section \ref{sec:argentina} for $\rho \in [1,2]$. The ageing response is stable, $0.816$ and $0.826$ at $\rho = 0.5$ and $2$ against $0.825$ at $\rho = 1$. The responses to inequality and to voting patterns are not: at $\rho = 2$ the observed design loses only $0.4\%$ of revenue, and on this thin cushion France's income distribution moves the design to $0.918$ and its voting patterns to $0.335$, against $0.707$ and $0.687$ at $\rho = 0.5$ (table \ref{table:US_ESC:summary}; \oa{esc-us}).
