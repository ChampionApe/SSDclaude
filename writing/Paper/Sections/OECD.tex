\section{Social Security in Rich OECD Countries}\label{sec:oecd}\label{sec:quant:OECD}
In order to study what shapes pensions, savings rates and labor supply in rich economies, we now calibrate the model to the U.S.\ and use it as a laboratory, in which counterfactual economies change one characteristic of the U.S.\ calibration at a time. In the U.S., as in France and the UK, nearly every worker qualifies for a contributive pension, so we set informality aside and the design reduces to one dimension, the extent to which benefits are earnings-related.\footnote{\textcite{WhitmanRa1} estimate that about 4\% of individuals aged 62--84 in the U.S.\ in 2010 would never collect Social Security, most of them for lack of the 40 quarters of covered earnings needed to qualify.} France and the UK supply the characteristics: France its income distribution, voting profile and taste for leisure, and both countries the demography that two ageing scenarios bracket. The design is given throughout the section, which closes with pension spending across 26 OECD countries at their observed designs. Section \ref{sec:esc} lets the electorate choose the design and tests the choice on the UK and France (section \ref{sec:esc:deepTest}) and on the 26 countries (section \ref{sec:esc:crossSection}).

Appendix \ref{app:US} and the \oa{oecd} contain more details on the calibration and supplementary evidence.\footnote{The online appendix holds the calibrations across the intertemporal elasticity of substitution, under the alternative treatment of the taste for leisure and with Medicare among U.S.\ transfers to the old, and the counterfactuals of this section across the Frisch elasticity, each figure with the data behind it.}


\smalltitle{Calibration.}
We calibrate the U.S.\ and France to 2020 in the same way as Argentina in section \ref{sec:argentina}, with a few exceptions; the UK's calibration, which section \ref{sec:esc:deepTest} uses, is in appendix \ref{app:US}. First, with informality set aside, the population is split into three formal income groups. The first two groups are cut, as for Argentina, to have mean incomes of half the mean and the mean, the incomes at which the OECD's replacement rates identify $\theta$. France's system is fully Bismarckian, $\theta = 1$ at any cuts, so we group French households at the U.S.\ income percentiles instead, which puts the two countries' household types in one-to-one correspondence for the counterfactuals below. Second, the discount factor $\beta$ matches the U.S.\ thirty-year interest rate rather than a capital--output ratio and is imposed on France. Third, where the Argentine calibration gives every household the same propensity to vote, the political objective here weights each income group by its observed propensity. In each country the tax rate is calibrated to public cash spending on pensions, the measure of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}, so U.S.\ Medicare enters neither the tax rate nor $\theta$, just as public health spending does not in France and the UK (appendix \ref{app:US}).

Table \ref{table:US:CalibUSFR} reports the parameters. Population growth is higher in the U.S.\ than in France, $\nu_{2020} = 1.34$ against $1.04$, and the ratio of high to low productivity is about a third higher, $3.08$ against $2.29$. France's average taste for leisure is $1.6$ times the U.S.\ level. In the U.S.\ the propensity to vote rises with income, from $0.47$ to $0.77$, while in France it is nearly flat, between $0.81$ and $0.86$ (tables \ref{table:a_US:CalibUS} and \ref{table:a_US:CalibFR}). The political weight of the old is calibrated to each country's pension spending, $\omega = 1.23$ in the U.S.\ and $1.45$ in France, and the counterfactuals hold it at its U.S.\ value.

The tax panel identifies the pension design and ageing as the main determinants of pension spending. Going from a fully Bismarckian system to a fully Beveridgean one raises the equilibrium tax rate by $6.6$ p.p.\ at $\rho = 1$ and lowers the savings rate by $1.9$ p.p.\ of GDP. The reason for this is that of proposition \ref{prop:LOG:PEE}: flatter benefits direct more of the revenue to poorer retirees, whose higher marginal utility gives them more weight in the political objective, so retirees support a larger system. Holding taxes fixed, an increase in $\theta$ would lower savings, but the endogenous fall in taxes more than offsets this and the net effect is positive. For labor supply, the economic-equilibrium and political effects of an increase in $\theta$ are aligned, and at $\rho = 1$ and below the tax response contributes more than the direct effect.

Population ageing is the determinant that previous studies of the politico-economic equilibrium of pension spending have highlighted: \textcite{mkvss08} predict an increase in U.S.\ spending of around 3.6 p.p.\ towards the middle of this century because of the slowdown in population growth. In the data, including projections, ageing is more acute in France than in the UK, and much more than in the U.S. We consider two scenarios, which roughly correspond to the data for the two European countries. In the first, {\it mild ageing}, we halve current and projected U.S.\ population growth; in the second, {\it acute ageing}, the population is constant, $\nu_t = 1$ for all $t$. Acute ageing raises the tax rate by $8.7$ p.p.\ and mild ageing by $4.0$ p.p. The fall in population growth in the mild scenario is close to the one projected by \textcite{mkvss08},\footnote{In \textcite{mkvss08}, $\nu_{2000} = 1.38$ and the projected $\nu_{2080} = 1.23$, a fall of about $0.15$, similar to the fall of $0.17$ in 2020 in the mild ageing scenario.} although their $3.6$ p.p.\ is a change over time within one economy and our $4.0$ p.p.\ a difference between two economies in 2020. When acute ageing drives up pension contributions significantly, this crowds out private savings by $2.0$ p.p.\ of GDP at $\rho = 1$. Working hours fall by roughly $0.7$ hours a week: the higher tax rate lowers the effective wage rate net of taxes, but this is partly offset by a capital deepening effect from ageing on wages. Thus, the net result is a modest drop in hours worked.



The characteristics that set France's households apart move the tax rate much less. At $\rho = 1$, France's flatter income distribution, imposed with $\theta$ held at its U.S.\ value, lowers it by $1.3$ p.p.,\footnote{We read $\theta$ off the ratio of replacement rates at mean and half-mean income, so under France's flatter income distribution the same observed ratio would imply a considerably less Bismarckian system. Re-estimating $\theta$ would bundle a change in the design into a counterfactual about inequality.} and its flatter voting profile, under which the poor vote about as often as the rich, raises it by $0.9$ p.p. The voting profile moves the tax rate towards France's, but once the electorate chooses the design as well (section \ref{sec:esc}) it moves the design away from France's fully Bismarckian system, towards flat benefits. Rescaling the taste for leisure to France's level shortens the workweek by nearly the whole gap to France's and leaves every rate at the baseline, since a proportional change in $X_i$ scales hours, output and savings together.

The workweek panel ranks the determinants differently. France's leisure preferences move hours most and leave every rate unchanged. Among the characteristics that move the tax rate, the pension design moves hours most, by about $1.7$ hours a week at the Beveridgean corner at $\rho = 1$, while ageing, the largest effect on taxes, moves them less than France's income distribution does at every $\rho$. The determinants of pension spending are thus not the determinants of hours worked.

\smalltitle{The effect of the intertemporal elasticity of substitution.}
In general, our results above are robust across different $\rho$ and the overall conclusion about the determinants of pension spending, savings, and labor supply holds. The effects on the tax rate and the savings rate are amplified for lower values of $\rho$ and dampened for higher values, modestly for ageing and much more for the income distribution; the one exception where this pattern is reversed is the French voting profile. The effect of ageing on the workweek is about the same at every $\rho$. The IES plays a key role in the effect of the pension design: going from $\theta = 1$ to $\theta = 0$ raises the tax rate by $9.1$ p.p.\ at $\rho = 0.5$ and by only $2.0$ p.p.\ at $\rho = 2$. The reason for this is that $\theta$ moves the tax rate through the difference in marginal utility between poor and rich retirees (proposition \ref{prop:LOG:PEE}), and a higher IES makes marginal utility fall less steeply with consumption, so that this difference counts for less; the savings rate and the workweek follow.


The calibrated parameters absorb institutions that the model does not contain. The political weight of the old, $\omega$, stands for the way legislation is introduced, debated and passed, and for constitutional arrangements such as whether the political system is presidential or parliamentary. The taste for leisure, $X$, stands for preferences and also for institutions that shape labor supply, such as unionization, collective bargaining, labor-market regulation and statutory holidays, so the large difference in $X$ between France and the U.S.\ includes these institutional differences.




\smalltitle{The OECD cross-section: spending.}
We now return to figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}, in which pension spending rises as population growth falls and is not related to income inequality. We take the model to the 26 countries of the figure in which at most a quarter of employment is informal, which leaves out Mexico, Turkey and Korea. Each country enters at its observed design, with its own number of workers per retiree and income distribution, and with the U.S.\ discount factor and the political weight of the old that reproduces U.S.\ spending at these data.\footnote{At these data the U.S.\ design and income distribution are measured as for every other country (appendix \ref{app:oecd26}), so this weight differs slightly from that of table \ref{table:US:CalibUSFR}.} Everything else, the propensities to vote included, is held at its U.S.\ value, under log preferences. Section \ref{sec:esc:crossSection} sets out in full what the cross-country test varies and what it holds; this is its first layer, here with the design given and without the cost of redistribution of section \ref{sec:esc:cost}.

In figure \ref{fig:OECD26:spending}, spending falls with population growth in the model as in the data, at a slope of $-14.90$ against $-12.84$, within one standard error of the data's. The model reproduces the slope but not the dispersion around it, since only demography, the income distribution and the design differ across its countries. Neither the model nor the data relate spending to the Gini: the two slopes have opposite signs, $-15.65$ in the model and $17.80$ in the data, but each is smaller than its standard error. With the design given, the model thus reproduces the two relations, as the counterfactuals above suggest: demography is the main determinant of the tax rate, and the income distribution moves it little.\footnote{On the disposable-income Gini of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}, the U.S., at 0.42, is the most unequal of the 26 countries, and France, at 0.32, lies close to their median.}
