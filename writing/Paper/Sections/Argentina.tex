\section{Pension System Reform in Argentina}\label{sec:argentina}\label{Argentina}

Argentina is a middle-income country with high labor informality. In 1994 the social security system was reformed tying full benefits to 30 years of contributions, which left about a third of the elderly without a pension by 2004.\footnote{The reform also let workers opt for a fully funded system, whose funds were nationalized in December 2008.} From 2005 a series of tax amnesties let workers with shorter contribution records draw a pension. They added 2.7 million beneficiaries by 2011 \parencite{RofmanA15a1}, raising the number of pensioners from about 3 to 5 million (figure \ref{fig:Arg}) and the coverage of the elderly from 68\% to over 90\% \parencite{Roffman}. Thus, in a few years, Argentina experienced a significant increase in non-contributive pensions: Between 2005 and 2010 social security spending increased by 1.9\% of GDP with most of this increase, 1.8\% of GDP, due to the tax amnesties \parencite{CetrangoloG}.

\smalltitle{Calibration.}
We calibrate the model with informal savers to Argentina before the reforms and read the tax amnesties as an increase in $\epsilon$. Informal households work when young and save for retirement, facing the same rate of return on savings as formal households. A model period is thirty years, the calibration year is 2010, and every household has the same propensity to vote. Table \ref{table:Arg:Calib} reports the result and appendix \ref{app:EPH} the household survey, the identifying formulas and the sources. The pre-reform $\epsilon = 0.29$ is the basic pension of retirees without a full contribution record, relative to the benefit of the least productive formal workers and discounted because it starts five to ten years after the formal retirement age, and $\gamma_0 = 0.32$ is set to the share of the population over 65 without a pension before the reform \parencite{Roffman}. The Bismarckian index $\theta = 0.84$ matches the observed ratio of replacement rates at mean and half-mean income. The reform raises $\epsilon$ to the universal level $\epsilon^U = 1-\theta+\theta\eta_1h_{t-1,1}/h_{t-1}$ of section \ref{sec:model}, at which informal retirees receive the benefit of the least productive formal ones. We impose a formal-sector capital share of $\alpha = 0.35$, between the values implied by the survey's informal earnings and by shadow-economy estimates of informal output, a Frisch elasticity of $\xi = 0.30$, and a ratio of workers to retirees $\nu_t$ from thirty-year gross population growth in census data and projections, as in \textcite{mkvss08}.

The remaining parameters are identified jointly with the equilibrium path. The formal productivities $\eta_i$ match the income distribution across the four formal quartiles of the survey, with a taste for leisure $X$ common to them whose level is pinned by the observed formal workweek of 42.5 hours.\footnote{Identifying one $X_i$ per quartile from the survey's relative hours instead makes hours data and leaves their average level unidentified (appendix \ref{app:EPH:vectorX}). Since $X$ enters no aggregate, only $\eta_i$ and $X_i$ differ, and every result of this section is the same under both.} Relative hours across quartiles are then a prediction, which reproduces the survey's ranking and overstates the hours of the top quartile, 1.18 against 1.10 times the formal average. Four parameters are solved against four targets in 2010: $\beta$ against a capital-output ratio of $3.23$, $\omega$ against an equivalent social security tax rate of 10.9\%, which is pension spending of 7.1\% of GDP before the 2001 crisis divided by the labor share of formal output, and $\eta_0$ and $X_0$ against the informal households' income and hours relative to the formal average.

\smalltitle{The reform.}
Table \ref{table:Argentina:Universal} reports the effect in 2010 of an unanticipated and permanent rise of $\epsilon$ to $\epsilon^U$ under log-GHH preferences, first with the tax rate held at its pre-reform path and then chosen politically. We find that taxes increase, which drives down savings and labor supply; with taxes held fixed, formal savings rise, since informal households now hold a claim on the pension system and save less, and hours fall only marginally. 

In 2010 the model predicts that the social security tax rate rises by 1.2 p.p., which at the labor share of formal output is pension spending of 0.8\% of GDP, almost half of the 1.8\% of GDP that the amnesties added. The reason for this is that the reform moves benefits toward informal retirees, who are poorer than formal ones and value benefits more at the margin.\footnote{The tax also rises when informal households are hand-to-mouth, consistent with condition \eqref{eq:LOG:epsCondition} in appendix \ref{app:PEE:LOG}.} The savings rate falls by 0.3 p.p.\ of GDP and the average workweek by 0.1 hours, the fall in savings in line with the estimate of \textcite{Ruffo} for the amnesty. The tax response builds over time, to 2.1 p.p.\ above the pre-reform path by 2040, because informal households, now covered, save less for retirement and as retirees demand more taxation. The tables, figures and data behind this section, and a map of the equilibrium in 2010 over the whole range of designs $(\epsilon, \theta)$, are in \oa{arg}.

\smalltitle{The effect of the intertemporal elasticity of substitution.}
Since the environment has no uncertainty, we read the parameter $\rho$ of CRRA preferences as the intertemporal elasticity of substitution (IES). The evidence on its size is inconclusive,\footnote{Estimates from aggregate consumption data are low, which \textcite{Guvenena1} reconciles with higher values from output and wealth data through limited asset market participation, and asset-pricing models with Epstein--Zin preferences and long-run or disaster risk require an IES above one \parencite{BansalYa1,Barroa1}.} and we keep logarithmic utility as the benchmark. Figure \ref{fig:ARG:EffectOfCRRA} repeats the reform with the pre-reform economy calibrated at each $\rho$ and traces the effects in 2010 and 2040 for taxes, savings rates, average workweek, and informal savings.

The effects of the pension reform are nonlinear in $\rho$, in particular for values lower than one. When $\rho<1$ the effects of the pension reform are dampened across all metrics. The reason for this is that with a low IES the young resist taxation more strongly, so the recalibrated economy needs a larger political weight of the old to reproduce the observed tax rate and responds less to a change in benefits. For an IES between 1 and 2 the results stay close to the log benchmark: the tax rate rises by 1.2 to 1.3 p.p.\ in 2010 and by 2.0 to 2.1 p.p.\ by 2040, the workweek shortens by 0.1 hours in 2010 and by 0.3 to 0.4 hours by 2040, and the fall in the savings rate shrinks from 0.3 to 0.2 p.p.\ of GDP, in 2010 as by 2040. The ratio of informal to formal savings falls by 4.5 to 4.9 p.p.\ from 0.31 throughout this range. Since \textcite{Ruffo} find that the amnesty reduced savings, we regard $\rho \in [1,2]$ as the more plausible range.
