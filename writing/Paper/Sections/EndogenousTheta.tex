\section{Endogenous Pension Design}\label{sec:esc}
So far the Bismarckian index $\theta$ is calibrated while the tax rate is chosen politically. This section lets the electorate choose the design with the same objective $\mathcal{W}_t$, in the model without informal households, so one political process delivers the size of the system and its shape. The derivations are in appendix \ref{app:esc}, the algorithms in the technical documentation \parencite{BergGE26doc}.

\subsection{The deadweight cost of redistribution}\label{sec:esc:cost}
Without a deadweight cost of redistribution the choice is a corner. An electorate that weighs retirees by their marginal utility wants all of the transfer the flat component performs: if propensities to vote do not rise with income, the design chosen with the tax is the Beveridgean corner, $\theta_t = 0$ (proposition \ref{prop:esc:corner} in appendix \ref{app:esc:corner}). Propensities to vote that rise with income, as in the U.S., could overturn it, but at the U.S.\ calibration every timing of the choice, with the tax, one period in advance or once and for all, gives a corner (appendix \ref{app:esc:corner}). An interior design requires flat benefits to be costlier than earnings-related ones.

The flat component redistributes within the retired cohort. Relative to an earnings-related benefit paying the same revenue, it takes from retirees whose past earnings were above the mean and gives to those below it, a pure transfer in the aggregate. We assume that the transfer is costly, in the sense of Okun's leaky bucket \parencite{Okun75a1}: part of what the flat component carries does not arrive, and the part lost rises with the amount carried. The amount carried to or from a retiree of type $i$, per unit of past earnings, is proportional to $\tau_t(1-\theta_t)(1-1/y_i)$, where $y_i$ is the labor income of type $i$ relative to the mean: it rises with the size of the system, with the flat share of benefits and with the distance of the type's income from the mean. A loss on each type's earnings that is quadratic in this amount, as the deadweight loss of a tax is quadratic in its rate \parencite{Harberger64a1}, summed over types and taken out of revenue, gives the budget
\begin{align}\label{eq:esc:budget}
    \nu_t w_t\tau_th_t\, f(\theta_t,\tau_t) &= \sum_i \gamma_i b_t^i, \\
    f(\theta_t,\tau_t) &= \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\lambda\,\tau_t\,\tilde{V}\,(1-\theta_t)^2\right), \qquad \tilde{V} \equiv \sum_i\gamma_i\frac{(y_i-1)^2}{y_i}, \nonumber
\end{align}
where $1-f$ is the share of revenue lost, $\lambda \geq 0$ is the cost parameter and $\tilde{V}$ is the dispersion of relative incomes, in which a given transfer counts for more against a smaller income (appendix \ref{app:esc:cost} derives the form from the implicit tax of the flat component).\footnote{The exponential form keeps $f$ positive at every size of the system and nests the model without the cost at $\lambda = 0$; to first order $1-f$ is the quadratic loss.} Nothing is lost when nothing is redistributed: $f = 1$ at $\theta_t = 1$ and, for every design, when all incomes are equal, where a flat and an earnings-related benefit pay the same to everyone. And because the loss is quadratic in the amount carried while revenue is linear in it, the share lost rises with the size of the system, so a larger system is costlier to keep redistributive, the efficiency--redistribution trade-off by which \textcite{KoethenbuergerPP08a1} account for the finding of \textcite{CondeRuizP07a1} that more redistributive systems are smaller, read from the other side.

The model contains the hours response to the flat component; the cost stands for what it does not contain, the participation, formality and reporting margins on which the evidence finds the unlinked part of contributions to matter \parencite{Summers89a1,Disney04a1,KumlerVF20a1}, and the administrative and political costs of redistribution. Its parameter $\lambda$ is calibrated rather than derived; it is larger than estimated elasticities would give (appendix \ref{app:esc:cost}), and its discipline is the cross-country test of section \ref{sec:esc:calibration}. The solution of sections \ref{sec:model} to \ref{sec:numerical} carries over with $f\theta_t$ and $f(1-\theta_t)$ in place of $\theta_t$ and $1-\theta_t$ (appendix \ref{app:esc:equilibrium}); the cost cancels from the replacement-rate ratio, so $\theta^{\ast} = 0.738$ is unchanged, and adds one factor to the tax condition, as a larger system is worth less to a retiree at the margin than the revenue it collects. The design is chosen one period in advance, the Markov timing in which it moves labor supply and savings before it binds and the way benefit formulas are legislated; under log preferences the choice depends on neither the inherited design nor savings (proposition \ref{prop:esc:separability}). With the cost, a more Bismarckian design also raises the total the young will receive, which makes an interior choice possible.

\subsection{Calibration and a cross-country test}\label{sec:esc:calibration}
The cost parameter is calibrated so that $\theta_{2020}$, the design chosen in 1990 on the equilibrium path with the choice binding from the first period, equals $\theta^{\ast}$, with $(\beta,\omega)$ recalibrated at every trial value. The calibration pins 2020 only; every later design is a prediction. At $\rho = 1$, $\lambda = 8.643$: the observed design loses $1.8\%$ of revenue in 2020, and a flat benefit of the same size would lose $24\%$ (\oa{esc-calibration}). One datum calibrates the cost, so the UK and France test the specification, not its fit. Under the U.S.\ parameter the UK (observed design $0.560$, tax rate $11.9\%$) chooses $0.625$ and France ($1.000$, $21.3\%$) chooses $0.749$, while the U.S.\ re-elects $0.738$ by construction (table \ref{table:US_ESC:country}). The ordering is the observed one, but France's choice is only $0.011$ above the U.S.\ one, so the test rests on the UK. Its own cost, $\lambda = 7.264$, is within $16\%$ of the U.S.\ value, and regrouping its households at the U.S.\ income percentiles, which re-reads its observed design as $0.543$ and $\tilde{V}$ as $0.065$, moves its choice only to $0.644$. What separates the UK from France, at nearly the same dispersion of relative incomes, $0.223$ and $0.218$, about half the U.S.\ $0.436$, is the size of the system, as in figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}. France's corner is out of reach, since under a quadratic cost the first unit of redistribution is free at the margin.\footnote{A linear term in $1-\theta$ would allow it, at the price of a second parameter that the UK's design could pin with France as the prediction; we leave it for future work.}

\subsection{Ageing and inequality}\label{sec:esc:results}
We read three of the counterfactuals of section \ref{sec:oecd} twice (table \ref{table:US_ESC:results}), with the design pinned at the U.S.\ value and chosen, both under the cost, so their distance is the political response. On the baseline path (figure \ref{fig:US_ESC:path}) the chosen design rises from $0.738$ in 2020 to $0.797$ in 2050 and $0.809$ from 2110 on, tracking the fall in workers per retiree from $1.34$ to $1.07$ and flattening a period after it does. The tax rate rises from $14.4\%$ to $20.9\%$, $0.2$ p.p.\ above the path with the design pinned, since a more Bismarckian system dissipates less and is worth more to the electorate at the margin.\footnote{Recalibrated at Frisch elasticities $\xi = 0.2$ and $0.4$, $\lambda$ moves by about one percent and the tax in 2110 by about $0.3$ p.p.\ either way, while the design in 2110 is unchanged to the third decimal and the acute-ageing design within one unit of it (\oa{esc-path}).}

\smalltitle{Ageing.} Acute ageing, $\nu_t = 1$ throughout, raises the chosen design from $0.738$ to $0.825$. A scarcer young generation lifts the political weight of retirees, who are unanimous for a more earnings-related system since it increases the labor supply on which their benefits are levied (appendix \ref{app:esc:corner}). And the tax that ageing calls for, $22.8\%$ against $14.4\%$, raises the loss per unit of redistribution by more than half, since the loss is proportional to $\tau_t$. This channel supplies about half the response: under a cost on the design itself, which does not scale with the size of the system, the design rises only to $0.778$ (\oa{esc-scale}). The chosen design adds $0.3$ p.p.\ to the pinned tax response of $8.1$ p.p., which is below the $8.7$ p.p.\ of section \ref{sec:oecd} because the cost makes a larger system worth less to retirees at the margin.

\smalltitle{Income distribution.} The model's inequality is $\tilde{V}$, the dispersion of relative incomes, not the Gini coefficient of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}. With the design pinned, France's flatter distribution lowers the tax from $14.4\%$ to $13.8\%$, less than the $1.2$ p.p.\ of section \ref{sec:oecd}, because a lower $\tilde{V}$ also lowers the share of revenue lost. When chosen, the design moves to $0.772$, interior and close to the observed one: since the cost is proportional to the same dispersion as the redistribution a flat benefit performs, compressing the distribution lowers the redistributive stakes and their cost together, and the young's forward-looking stake, which does not scale with the dispersion, gains a little ground. The tax is that of the pinned reading.

\smalltitle{Voting patterns.} France's much flatter voting profile moves the design the other way, to $0.653$, by about as much as ageing: when the poor carry more voting weight, the redistributive force of proposition \ref{prop:esc:corner} regains ground. The tax is $15.3\%$ in both readings. Combined with France's income distribution, the voting patterns prevail, at $0.627$, as they do on the UK as host (\oa{esc-uk}).

As in the cross-section of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}, ageing moves the chosen design a long way toward earnings-related benefits, and the size of the system with it, while inequality moves the design little.

\smalltitle{The effect of the intertemporal elasticity of substitution.} The cost parameter falls from $18.267$ at $\rho = 0.5$ to $1.724$ at $\rho = 2$, as a higher elasticity strengthens the forward-looking channels against the Beveridgean corner; at $\rho = 2$ it is closest to what estimated elasticities imply (appendix \ref{app:esc:cost}), adding to the case of section \ref{sec:argentina} for $\rho \in [1,2]$. The ageing response is stable, $0.816$ and $0.826$ at $\rho = 0.5$ and $2$. The responses to inequality and to voting patterns are not: at $\rho = 2$ the observed design loses only $0.4\%$ of revenue, and on this thin cushion France's income distribution moves the design to $0.918$ and its voting patterns to $0.335$, against $0.707$ and $0.687$ at $\rho = 0.5$ (\oa{esc-us}). In this sense the model ties the design to ageing, not to inequality. The UK's own cost is 16 to 17\% below the U.S.\ one at $\rho \leq 1$ and well above it at $\rho = 2$ (\oa{esc-uk}).
