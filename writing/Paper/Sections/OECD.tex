\section{Social Security in Rich OECD Countries}\label{sec:oecd}\label{sec:quant:OECD}
We now calibrate the model to the U.S.\ and use it as a laboratory, in which counterfactual economies change one characteristic of the U.S.\ calibration at a time. We take the characteristics from France, an older and more equal economy than the U.S., in which the poor vote about as often as the rich and households work shorter hours. In the U.S., as in France, nearly every worker qualifies for a contributive pension, so we set informality aside and the design reduces to one dimension, the extent to which benefits are earnings-related.\footnote{\textcite{WhitmanRa1} estimate that about 4\% of individuals aged 62--84 in the U.S.\ in 2010 would never collect Social Security, most of them for lack of the 40 quarters of covered earnings needed to qualify.} The design is given throughout the section, which closes by testing the model's predictions for pension spending against the OECD data of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}. Appendix \ref{app:US} and the \oa{oecd} give the details of the calibration and further results.\footnote{The online appendix holds the calibrations across the intertemporal elasticity of substitution and with Medicare among U.S.\ transfers to the old, and the counterfactuals of this section across the Frisch elasticity, each figure with the data behind it.}


\smalltitle{Calibration.}
We calibrate the U.S.\ to 2020 in the same way as Argentina in section \ref{sec:argentina}, with a few differences. The discount factor $\beta$ matches the U.S.\ thirty-year interest rate rather than a capital--output ratio, and where the Argentine calibration gives every household the same propensity to vote, the political objective here weights each income group by its observed propensity. The tax rate is calibrated to public cash spending on pensions, the measure of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}. France is calibrated in the same way, with the U.S.\ $\beta$ and with its households grouped at the U.S.\ income percentiles, which puts the two countries' household types in one-to-one correspondence for the counterfactuals.\footnote{France's system is fully Bismarckian, $\theta = 1$ at any cuts, so its groups need not be cut at half-mean and mean income.}

Table \ref{table:US:CalibUSFR} reports the parameters. France has fewer workers per retiree than the U.S., a flatter productivity profile and a taste for leisure $1.6$ times the U.S.\ level, and its propensity to vote is nearly flat across income groups, where in the U.S.\ it rises from $0.47$ to $0.77$ (tables \ref{table:a_US:CalibUS} and \ref{table:a_US:CalibFR}). The political weight of the old is calibrated to each country's pension spending, and the counterfactuals hold it at its U.S.\ value.\footnote{The calibrated parameters absorb institutions that the model does not contain. The political weight of the old, $\omega$, stands for the way legislation is introduced, debated and passed, and for constitutional arrangements such as whether the political system is presidential or parliamentary. The taste for leisure, $X$, stands for preferences and also for institutions that shape labor supply, such as unionization, collective bargaining, labor-market regulation and statutory holidays, so the large difference in $X$ between France and the U.S.\ includes these institutional differences.}

The tax panel identifies the pension design and ageing as the main determinants of pension spending, with the effect of the design measured from corner to corner. Going from a fully Bismarckian system to a fully Beveridgean one raises the equilibrium tax rate by $6.6$ p.p.\ at $\rho = 1$, lowers the savings rate by $1.9$ p.p.\ of GDP and shortens the workweek by $2.4$ hours, $1.7$ of them between the observed design and the Beveridgean corner. The reason for this is that of proposition \ref{prop:LOG:PEE}: flatter benefits direct more of the revenue to poorer retirees, whose higher marginal utility gives them more weight in the political objective, so retirees support a larger system.

Ageing is the determinant that previous studies of the politico-economic equilibrium of pension spending have highlighted. We consider two scenarios. In {\it mild ageing} we halve current and projected U.S.\ population growth, and in {\it acute ageing} the population is constant, $\nu_t = 1$, close to France's demography. Acute ageing raises the tax rate by $8.7$ p.p.\ and mild ageing by $4.0$ p.p., the latter close to the rise of about $3.6$ p.p.\ in U.S.\ spending that \textcite{mkvss08} find for a similar fall in population growth.\footnote{\textcite{mkvss08} look at the U.S.\ economy with population growth falling from $\nu_{2000} = 1.38$ to the projected $\nu_{2080} = 1.23$, a fall of about $0.15$, similar to the fall of $0.17$ in 2020 in the mild ageing scenario.} The higher contributions crowd out private savings, by $2.0$ p.p.\ of GDP under acute ageing at $\rho = 1$, while the workweek falls by less than an hour, since capital deepening raises the wage and partly offsets the higher tax.

The other characteristics that set France apart move the tax rate much less. At $\rho = 1$, France's more equal income distribution, imposed with $\theta$ held at its U.S.\ value, lowers it by $1.3$ p.p.,\footnote{We read $\theta$ off the ratio of replacement rates at mean and half-mean income, so under France's more equal income distribution the same observed ratio would imply a considerably less Bismarckian system. Re-estimating $\theta$ would bundle a change in the design into a counterfactual about inequality.} and its flatter voting profile raises it by $0.9$ p.p. France's taste for leisure shortens the workweek by nearly the whole gap to France's and leaves every rate at the baseline, since a proportional change in $X_i$ scales hours, output and savings together. The determinants of pension spending are thus not the determinants of hours worked: leisure preferences move hours most, and ageing, the largest effect on taxes, moves them less than the income distribution does.

\smalltitle{The effect of the intertemporal elasticity of substitution.}
These conclusions hold at every IES we consider. The effects on the tax rate and the savings rate are larger at lower values of $\rho$, modestly for ageing and much more for the income distribution, with the voting profile the one exception. The IES matters most for the pension design: going from $\theta = 1$ to $\theta = 0$ raises the tax rate by $9.1$ p.p.\ at $\rho = 0.5$ and by only $2.0$ p.p.\ at $\rho = 2$. The reason for this is that $\theta$ moves the tax rate through the difference in marginal utility between poor and rich retirees (proposition \ref{prop:LOG:PEE}), and a higher IES makes marginal utility fall less steeply with consumption, so that this difference counts for less.


\smalltitle{The OECD cross-section.}
To test these predictions against the data of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}, we construct 26 synthetic countries. Each is the U.S.\ model under log preferences, with the demography, income distribution and pension design of one of 26 countries from the figure.\footnote{We leave out the three countries in which more than a quarter of employment is informal, Mexico, Turkey and Korea (appendix \ref{app:oecd26}).} The synthetic countries thus differ only in demography, the income distribution and the design, the three dimensions of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}.\footnote{Every synthetic country takes the political weight that reproduces U.S.\ spending with the U.S.\ design and income distribution measured as for every other country, $1.26$, which differs slightly from the $1.23$ of table \ref{table:US:CalibUSFR} (appendix \ref{app:oecd26:anchor}).}

In figure \ref{fig:OECD26:spending}, spending falls with population growth in the model as in the data, at a slope within one standard error of the data's. The model reproduces the slope but not the dispersion around it, since only demography, the income distribution and the design differ across its countries. Neither the model nor the data relate spending to the Gini. With the design given, the model thus reproduces the two relations, as the counterfactuals above suggest: demography is the main determinant of the tax rate, and the income distribution moves it little.

The design, however, enters this test as data. In figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata} the Bismarckian index also rises as population growth falls, which suggests that countries respond to ageing by tying benefits more closely to earnings, a response that a model holding $\theta$ fixed cannot address. Section \ref{sec:esc} takes it up by letting the electorate choose $\theta$ as well as $\tau$.
