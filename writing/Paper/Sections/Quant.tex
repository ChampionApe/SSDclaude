\section{Quantitative Analysis}\label{sec:quant}
To evaluate the model's quantitative performance we carry out two evaluations. First, as a proof of concept we analyze the impact of a set of reforms in Argentina from 2005 to 2010 that introduced near-universal pensions benefiting a large share of informal households. We calculate the effect these reforms had on the tax rate, labor participation, and savings rate. Second, we calibrate the model to three different developed economies for which almost all households are covered by contributive pensions and have different pension characteristics, aggregate spending, demographics, and income (and voting) inequality: the United States, France, and the United Kingdom.


The solution method differs depending on the specific model. For the Argentina model in general and the three developed economies with CRRA preferences, the PEE is identified numerically. We solve for the PEE policy function on a grid of states and iterate backwards. We then simulate forward solving for a path of economic equilibria with taxes determined by policy functions evaluated at the path of economic equilibrium states. To identify the sequence of PEE policy functions on the grid of states, we initialize the backwards iteration using two different terminal state assumptions. First, we consider the finite horizon economy that terminates after $T$ periods. In this case, the terminal state PEE policy can be identified analytically. Second, we consider the infinite horizon economy that converges to a steady state after $T$ periods. In this case, the steady state policy function is identified by a fixed point problem where the policy and continuation policy functions are time-autonomous functions of the state. 

Appendix \ref{app:numeric} describes the numerical strategies in more detail, including how identification of the policy functions depend on the terminal state assumption (finite vs. infinite horizon) and the specific model (logarithmic vs.\ CRRA preferences). The following sections show results from the finite horizon solution approach; with a sufficiently large $T$ the two solution approaches yield virtually identical results out of steady state.\footnote{In our model with $t$ measuring 30-year time steps, we use projected population growth rates until 2070-2080 and then default to 5 time steps with steady state rates.} 

\subsection{Pension System Reform in Argentina}\label{Argentina}

Argentina is a middle-income country that faces high levels of labor informality. In 1994 the social security system was reformed introducing strict contribution requirements, which resulted in about a third of individuals approaching retirement age in 2005 not being eligible for full benefits.\footnote{A pension reform in 1994 allowed workers the option between a fully-funded or a pay-as-you-go system. Regardless of this option, workers were required to have 30 years of contributions to receive benefits. In 2007 workers under the fully-funded system were given the option to return to a pay-as-you go system. Then in December 2008 the fully-funded pension funds were nationalized, and its beneficiaries were transferred to the pay-as-you-go system.} Since 2005 a number of reforms were introduced to increase pension coverage. In particular, tax amnesties added 2.7 million additional beneficiaries raising the coverage rate of the elderly from 68\% to 91\%, see figure \ref{fig:Arg}. This was achieved by allowing workers with less than the required 30 years of contributions to receive pensions.\footnote{See \textcite{CetrangoloG} and \textcite{Roffman} for details on recent pension reforms in Argentina, and \textcite{Ruffo} for an estimate of the effect of the tax amnesty on savings.} 
Thus, in a few years, Argentina experienced a significant increase in non-contributive pensions: 
Between 2005 and 2010 social security spending increased by 1.9\% of GDP with most of this increase, 1.8\% of GDP, due to the tax amnesty \cite{CetrangoloG}.

We now describe the calibration of the model with informal households to Argentina before the pension reforms and estimate its predictions for the observed increase in $\epsilon$ due to the tax amnesties on equilibrium tax rates, labor supply and savings. We assume informal households work when young and save for retirement, facing the same rate of return on savings as formal households. 

To calibrate the income distribution and initial labor supply, we resort to the Argentine household survey Encuesta Permanente de Hogares (EPH). We use data from the last two quarters of 2006 and split formal workers, a status that we infer from social security contributions, into four quartiles (see appendix \ref{app:EPH} for details). For formal and informal workers we estimate average income and average hours worked. 
Before the tax amnesties, retirees over the age of 70 who had made contributions for a minimum of 10 years were eligible for basic pension coverage. This coverage consisted of 70\% of the minimum pension and a proportional amount from other contributions.\footnote{The basic pensions was set by law 24347 of year 1995 at 70\% of the first pillar, denoted ``prestaci\'on b\'asica universal'', plus the corresponding fully-funded or pay-as-you-go benefit. For simplicity we assume the latter on average was also 70\% of the benefits received by workers that had at least 30 years of contributions. Thus, basic pensions were 70\% of the benefits received by the least productive formal workers.} We calculated the pre-reform $\epsilon$ \begin{align*}
        \epsilon = 0.7 \left(1-\theta+\theta\frac{\eta^1h_t^1}{h_t}\right)\dfrac{\beta^{5/30} \frac{\mathrm{LE_{men}-5}}{\mathrm{LE_{men}}}+\beta^{10/30} \frac{\mathrm{LE_{women}-10}}{\mathrm{LE_{women}}}}{2},
    \end{align*}
where $\mathrm{LE_{men}}=14.45$ and $\mathrm{LE_{women}}=22.55$ are the expected lifetime for men and women, respectively, from their retirement ages (women retire at age 60, men at 65), such that the formula above corrects for the basic pension being received 5 or 10 years after the formal retirement age.\footnote{To estimate life expectancy at retirement age for Argentina we conduct a regression analysis using data for men and women's life expectancies at birth and at age 65 for 40 OECD countries for the year 2000. We then use the fitted regression and data from the World Bank on male and female life expectancy at birth in Argentina in 2000.} We set $\gamma_0$ to match the share of retirees that only received this pension prior to the reform, 32\% \cite{CetrangoloG}. To identify the Bismarckian incentives, we use OECD data on the ratio of replacement rates, $RR$, at half mean and mean wages for males and females.\footnote{In a pure Bismarckian system the replacement rate would be the same at all wage levels, such that $RR=1$, while in a pure Beveridgean it would double if we halve wages, implying $RR=0.5$. Note that we combine men and women since we do not have data separately on the increase in coverage or government spending by gender.} Specifically, we adjust $\theta$ to ensure that
    \begin{align*}
        RR \equiv \dfrac{\theta+(1-\theta)\frac{h_t}{\eta_t^2h_t^2}}{\theta+(1-\theta)\frac{h_t}{\eta_t^1h_t^1}}= \dfrac{0.678}{0.803},
    \end{align*}
where the superscripts refer to the income quartiles, chosen such that $2 \eta_t^1h_t^1=\eta_t^2h_t^2=h_t$.


After the reforms, we set $\epsilon=1-\theta+\theta \eta^1h_t^1/h_t$ implying that, at their retirement age, all informal workers receive the same benefit as formal workers with the lowest productivity. To gauge the impact transitioning to more universal pension benefits, we calibrate the model to Argentina in 2005-2010 and take a period to be 30 years. The capital income share, $\alpha=0.43$, comes from \textcite{Frankema}. In the baseline, we take the elasticity of labor supply, $\xi=0.30$, and explore values between $0.20$ and $0.40$ as robustness.\footnote{Changes in this parameter affect the calibration but have minor effects on the implications of pension reform. This is consistent with \textcite{Imrohoroglu} that finds aggregate labor supply to be insensitive to this elasticity.} 
To calibrate $\beta$, we target the private savings rate defined as savings relative to GDP per capita. We use the average savings rate between 1994 and 2007 of $18.4\%$ from World Bank national accounts data. Calibration of $\beta$ and $\epsilon$ are done numerically as $\beta$ depends on the politico-economic equilibrium path for savings and labor supply and $\epsilon$ depends on $\beta$.



We calibrate the income distribution and relative working hours across the income distribution using $\eta_j$ and $X_j$. Specifically, let $z_j$ denote income relative to the average formal household; with labor supply following $h_t^i/h_t = \eta_iX_i^{\xi}$ for formal households (cf. proposition \ref{prop:LOG:EE}), we solve the system
\begin{align}\label{eq:calibration_eta}
        z_i^{\eta} &= \dfrac{w_t(1-\tau_t)h_t^i \eta_i}{w_t(1-\tau_t)h_t} = \dfrac{\eta_i^{1+\xi}/X_i^{\xi}}{\sum_{j>0} \left(\gamma_ j\eta_j^{1+\xi}/X_j^{\xi}\right)}, \ \ \ \ \ i>0\\
         z_0^{\eta} &= \dfrac{w_t h_t^0 \eta_0}{w_t(1-\tau_t)h_t} = \dfrac{\eta_0^{1+\xi}/X_0^{\xi}}{(1-\tau_t)\sum_{j>0} \left(\gamma_ j\eta_j^{1+\xi}/X_j^{\xi}\right)}. \nonumber
    \end{align}
We normalize $\sum_i \gamma_i\eta_i^{1+\xi}/X_i^{\xi} = 1$ and solve equations above for a vector of productivities $\eta_j$.

    
To construct the time series for $\nu_t$ we follow \textcite{mkvss08} and use 30-year gross population growth rate and projections from census data. Finally, we use social security spending before the 2001 crisis of 7.1\% of GDP,\footnote{Data from ANSES. We use pre-crisis spending as it was relatively stable during that time, and after 2010 contributive pensions returned to their pre-crisis levels \cite{CetrangoloG}. Following the 2001 crisis, pension spending was reduced (reaching 5.8\% of GDP in 2005) by only allowing minimum pensions to adjust for inflation.} which gives an equivalent social security tax rate of $\tau_{2010}=0.125$, to calibrate $\omega$.

We next consider the effect of general CRRA preferences and how this would affect the effects of system reform. Given that our framework features no uncertainty, the more appropriate interpretation for $\rho$ is that it is the intertemporal elasticity of substitution (IES), i.e.\ it reflects households attitudes towards allocation of consumption over time, not across states of nature. The empirical evidence on the magnitude of the IES remains inconclusive.\footnote{\textcite{Guvenena1}, for instance, argues that taking into account limited asset market participation and heterogeneity in wealth holdings can help reconcile the relatively low estimates obtained from aggregate consumption data with the much higher estimates derived from output or wealth measures. Asset pricing models with Epstein-Zin preferences and either long-run risk or disaster risk show that to explain empirical facts both risk aversion and IES have to be larger than one (or lower than one but risk aversion is known to be larger than one). See \textcite{BansalYa1} and \textcite{Barroa1}.} In view of these considerations, we rely on the estimates obtained under logarithmic utility as our benchmark. Table \ref{fig:Argentina_functionOfParams} shows that the model predictions for the effect of making pensions universal, after recalibration to the pre-reform economy, on the post-reform taxes, savings rate and labor supply are sensitive to the IES. In contrast, keeping system characteristics fixed the effect of ageing is largely insensitive to the IES. This last result is in line with findings in \textcite{mkvss05} for a simpler model with no heterogeneity and thus pensions summarized by their size. 



\subsection{Social Security in Rich OECD Countries}

The model developed in this paper is well suited to rich OECD economies. First, given current labor-market conditions and institutional design, we can abstract from informality and focus on a single policy dimension that shapes the politico-economic equilibrium: the extent to which benefits are earnings-related.\footnote{\textcite{WhitmanRa1} estimated that only around 4\% of individuals aged 62–84 in 2010 in the U.S.\ would never collect Social Security. And for the vast majority of these cases the reason would be lacking the 40 quarters/10 years of covered earnings needed to qualify.} Second, the framework can be used to study the implications of emerging work arrangements with low job security—such as platform work, zero-hour contracts, and certain forms of self-employment. Although these jobs currently represent a small share of total employment, their incidence may rise, particularly given current challenges from automation and artificial intelligence. A central concern is that fragmented and insecure careers could leave a non-negligible fraction of workers unable to meet contributory eligibility requirements by retirement. Our model can be used to quantify the consequences of such a shift and to evaluate how introducing some form of universal coverage would alter the equilibrium.

We take the first of these applications and compare three rich economies that differ markedly on their level of pension spending, demographics, income inequality, and pension characteristics: the U.S., France, and the UK. We show that to a large extent our model can fit all three countries. More interestingly, we then explore the main determinants of the politico-economic equilibrium taxes and find that aging is the most significant with pension characteristics being the second most important factor. Inequality in income or in voting patters and preferences over leisure are of minor importance.\footnote{In the US, the poor tend to vote much less than the rich, in contrast with the other two countries.} Naturally, leisure preferences are by construction important for differences in labor supply across countries. However, they play little role in the aggregation of preferences for inter-generational transfers. 

To calibrate the model with no informal households, we use data for men between ages 25 and 60. The only parameter for pensions is $\theta$, and to calibrate it, we proceed as for Argentina and use OECD's replacement rates at mean income and half-mean income. For all three countries, we use three income groups. The first two are chosen such that their income corresponds to half-mean and mean, respectively, and the remainder of the population grouped in a third high income group. Given that France has a fully Bismarckian system with $\theta=1$, this distinction is not necessary, and we can group individuals using income percentiles from other countries.\footnote{This feature is particularly useful when we want to explore the counterfactual politico-economic equilibrium in the U.S.\ if it had the level of income inequality of France.} 

To calibrate income, productivity, and hours worked we use the CPS March Supplement 2019 for the U.S., the Luxembourg Income Study for France, and the Harmonised BHPS Wave 17 for the UK. For the first two countries the data corresponds to the year before the pandemic for which data collection was not compromised by lockdown measures. For the UK, the data is for year 2006-7.\footnote{There were two more waves of these survey before being discontinued but we use Wave 17 as later surveys were collected, at least partially, during the Great Recession.} 

To calibrate $\beta$ for the U.S., we use the \textcite{mkvss08} estimate of the gross interest rate of net of the average growth of multifactor productivity. For France and UK we impose the same discount factor as calibrated for the U.S. Data on spending as a share of GDP corresponds to year 2020 and comes from \textcite{OECDpen}. Since our model is one of intergenerational transfers, not of overall government spending, we add Medicare spending to pensions for the U.S.\footnote{We do the same for the calibration of $\theta$ described above. We assume Medicare benefits are not correlated with income, thus they tend to make overall transfers more Beveridgean than pure pensions.} While it is true that France and UK's governments are in charge of health expenditures for their retired populations, in these two countries they also cover health spending {\it before} retirement. We impose the same techonology on all three countries and take $\alpha=0.30$.\footnote{Averaging the adjustment 2 and adjustment 3 estimates in \textcite{Gollina1} gives capital income shares of close to 0.3 for France and the U.S., with the UK having a slightly lower value.}

Population growth rates come from census data and population projections. For France we correct for their lower retirement age.\footnote{Since in 2020 the retirement age in France was 63.5 years instead of 66 as in the U.S.\ or UK we take the rate of growth of population in 2020 to be \[
\frac{\mathrm{Population} \ 2020 *(30-2.5)/30}{\mathrm{Population} \ 1990 + \mathrm{Population} \ 2020 *2.5/30}.
\]
We do a similar adjustments for later years taking into account that the gap between retirement ages is expected to shrink from 2.5 to 2 years.} Finally, since it is well known that in the U.S.\ the poor have much lower voting participation rates than the well off, we allow for different weights in the political objective functions based on this. We use data from the U.S.\ Census  Voting and Registration in the Election of November
2020. For France and UK we use data from the Comparative Study of Electoral Systems.\footnote{Given that for these two countries voting patterns are more equal across income groups, we could have abstracted from this feature.}

Table \ref{table:US:Calib} presents the main calibration parameters for the three countries. It shows large differences in population structure with the U.S.\ having the highest rate of population growth and France the lowest. The highest income inequality observed in the U.S.\ is reflected in a ratio of high to low productivity that is more than 50\% higher than in France. Conversely, the average preference for leisure is about 50\% higher in France and the UK than in the U.S. Despite these differences, and the fact that pensions systems differ across countries, the calibrated political weight of the old is basically the same in the U.S.\ and France, being a bit lower in the UK.

We now turn to a number of counterfactual analyses to study how different characteristics affect pension spending, savings, and labor supply. Specifically, we consider the U.S. model and change its pensions characteristics, population growth, income inequality, preferences for leisure, and voting patterns. In all of these exercises we will be guided by what we observe in France and UK, particularly the former as we can construct the same groupings of workers. We start with the degree with which pension benefits are linked to earnings and consider the two polar cases of $\theta=0,1$ as counterfactuals. 


The results reported in table \ref{table:US:pensChars} show that going from a fully Bismarckian system to a fully Beveridgean one would increase the politico-economic tax rate by about 6.3 p.p. The same change would reduce the savings rate by 2.2 p.p. The effect of increasing $\theta$ given taxes results in lower savings, but due to the endogenous response in taxes the net effect is positive. For labor supply, however, the economic equilibrium and political effects of increasing $\theta$ are aligned and both result in increases of roughly  the same magnitude.

In a second counterfactual, we study the effects of population ageing. Previous studies of the politico-economic equilibrium of pension spending have highlighted the effect of ageing. For example, \textcite{mkvss08} predicts an increase in spending in the United States towards the middle of this century of around 3.6 p.p.\ because of the slowdown in population growth.\footnote{\textcite{ssvsde15} extends predictions on the effect of ageing on social security transfers to other rich OECD countries and shows that economic growth would be compromised if these countries could not manage to increase the retirement age when facing increases in longevity.} 
In our data, including population projections not reported here, ageing is more acute in France than in UK, and significantly more than the U.S. For simplicity we consider two scenarios. In the first one, {\it mild ageing}, we simply halve current and future projected population growth rates for the U.S. In the second, {\it acute ageing}, we impose a constant population, i.e.\ $\nu_t=1$ for all $t$. 
 
Table \ref{table:US:ageing} shows the effects of imposing these demographic scenarios that roughly correspond to data for our two European countries. Acute ageing would increase the tax rate by 8.7 p.p.\ and mild ageing would increase it by 4 p.p. Interestingly, this mild ageing scenario almost corresponds to the mentioned projections in \textcite{mkvss08}.\footnote{In \textcite{mkvss08} $\nu_{2000}=1.38$ and projected $\nu_{2080}=1.23$. Thus, a reduction of about 15 p.p., similar to the reduction of 17 p.p.\ in the mild ageing scenario analyzed in this paper.} With log-GHH preferences, the only effect of ageing on savings are through the endogenous response to the tax rate; in the acute ageing scenarios this results in a drop of 3 p.p.\ in savings. The effects on labor supply are mild with hours worked decreasing by at most 0.4 hours. In this case, the direct effect of ageing in economic equilibrium is a capital deepening effect that increases wages and labor supply; as taxes increase, however, the full effect is a modest drop.

Taxes increase by a bit less than one p.p.\ if the poor voted as often as they do in France, and would decrease by a bit more than one p.p.\ if income inequality was as low as in France. It is important to emphasize that the model should be understood as a stylized representation that synthesizes, rather than reproducing real-world features in detail through the calibration of certain key parameters. For instance, the parameter capturing the relative political weight of older generations is meant to absorb a wide set of institutional realities, including the mechanisms by which legislation is introduced, debated, and passed, as well as broader constitutional arrangements such as whether the political system is presidential or parliamentary. In a similar way, the parameter reflecting the preference for leisure is not only an abstract measure of individual utility from non-work time, but also serves as a proxy for institutional factors that affect labor supply decisions. These include, for example, the degree of unionization, the prevalence of collective bargaining, the scope of labor market regulations, or statutory holidays. Thus, we should interpret the large differences in calibrated leisure preferences as including these other institutional differences across our three countries.

We leverage this quantitative analysis to address the difference in hours worked between the U.S. and Europe since the 1970s. \textcite{Prescotta1} attributes the divergence to taxes and pensions using a dynamic model of investment and labor supply. \textcite{Alesinaa1} or \textcite{Blancharda1} instead argue that differences in preferences for leisure, due to a combination of cultural and institutional factors, rationalize the differences in labor supply across the Atlantic. Our study suggests the underlying drivers of taxes and intergenerational transfers must first be examined. Ageing could then explain the increase in taxes in Europe but, according to our model, this would fail to explain the decrease in hours worked unless the labor supply elasticity is much larger than we calibrate it to be.\footnote{This is the key assumption underlying the results in \textcite{Prescotta1}. Note that with GHH preferences, the effect of changes in after tax labor income on labor supply is generally higher than with separable preferences.} 

We now return to figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata} that shows the tight relation between pension spending and how Bismarckian systems are with population ageing, while showing no clear relation between these two variables and income inequality. Our model shows that, consistent with previous related work, ageing is a prime determinant of social security taxes. It also shows that the link between income inequality and taxes is about an order of magnitude smaller.\footnote{We note that the U.S.\ has the highest Gini coefficient in our sample, 0.59, with France, at around 0.43, being below the mean.} \textcolor{red}{While we do not endogenize pension characteristics, the relation between $\theta$ and population growth suggests that, as countries age and face pressures to increase taxes, they might respond by making their pension benefits more tightly linked to earnings (according to our model an increase in $\theta$ dampens the effects of ageing). We leave the study of pension characteristics choice to future work.}


We next consider the effect of general CRRA preferences and how this would affect the studied counterfactuals. Given that our framework features no uncertainty, the more appropriate interpretation for $\rho$ is that it is the intertemporal elasticity of substitution (IES), i.e.\ it reflects households attitudes towards allocation of consumption over time, not across states of nature. The empirical evidence on the magnitude of the IES remains inconclusive. \textcite{Guvenena1}, for instance, argues that taking into account limited asset market participation and heterogeneity in wealth holdings can help reconcile the relatively low estimates obtained from aggregate consumption data with the much higher estimates derived from output or wealth measures.\footnote{Asset pricing models with Epstein-Zin preferences and either long-run risk or disaster risk show that to explain empirical facts both risk aversion and IES have to be larger than one (or lower than one but risk aversion is known to be larger than one). See \textcite{BansalYa1} and \textcite{Barroa1}.} In view of these considerations, we continue to rely on the estimates obtained under logarithmic utility as our benchmark. This choice is further supported by the fact that most of our counterfactual exercises exhibit only limited sensitivity to variations in the IES. The exception is that we find significant differences regarding the relation between earnings and benefits, see table \ref{table:US:CRRA:pensChars}. If $\rho>1$ the effect of changes in this parameter are dampened, while the opposite happens if $\rho<1$.

Recall that the mechanism through which a reduction in $\theta$ raises equilibrium taxes operates by providing greater benefits to the poorer segment of the elderly. This analysis, however, takes the level of resistance to taxation by the current young as given. Importantly, an increase in the elasticity of intertemporal substitution {\it reduces} the young’s resistance to taxation. Thus, to explain the initial level of taxes the model requires a {\it lower} relative power of the old, $\omega$. Consequently, when evaluating different values of $\theta$, the difference in benefits between rich and poor elderly receives less weight. This explains the dampened response observed in table \ref{table:US:CRRA:pensChars} when $\rho>1$.

As expected, the response of the savings rate and labor supply follow that of taxation, i.e.\ we get less sensitivity to $\theta$ for $\rho>1$ and more sensitivity for lower $\rho<1$. We report the result for the other counterfactual in tables \ref{table:US:CRRA:ageing} and \ref{table:US:CRRA:otherShocks} in the appendix. The effects of ageing are largely unaffected by $\rho$, consistent with findings in \textcite{mkvss08}. For income distribution the changes remain an order of magnitude smaller than for ageing. The main change is when the poor are more likely to vote in which case, when $\rho>1$, the tax rate increases twice as much as in the baseline. The reason for this is that, while low income households have a higher weight in policy, the increase in $\rho$ increases more the push for higher taxes among the retirees of this group than the preference for lower taxes among their workers. The opposite happens when $\rho<1$.
