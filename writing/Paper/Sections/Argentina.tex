\section{Pension System Reform in Argentina}\label{sec:argentina}\label{Argentina}

Argentina is a middle-income country that faces high levels of labor informality. In 1994 the social security system was reformed introducing strict contribution requirements, which resulted in about a third of individuals approaching retirement age in 2005 not being eligible for full benefits.\footnote{A pension reform in 1994 allowed workers the option between a fully-funded or a pay-as-you-go system. Regardless of this option, workers were required to have 30 years of contributions to receive benefits. In 2007 workers under the fully-funded system were given the option to return to a pay-as-you go system. Then in December 2008 the fully-funded pension funds were nationalized, and its beneficiaries were transferred to the pay-as-you-go system.} Since 2005 a number of reforms were introduced to increase pension coverage. In particular, tax amnesties added 2.7 million additional beneficiaries raising the coverage rate of the elderly from 68\% to 91\%, see figure \ref{fig:Arg}. This was achieved by allowing workers with less than the required 30 years of contributions to receive pensions.\footnote{See \textcite{CetrangoloG} and \textcite{Roffman} for details on recent pension reforms in Argentina, and \textcite{Ruffo} for an estimate of the effect of the tax amnesty on savings.} 
Thus, in a few years, Argentina experienced a significant increase in non-contributive pensions: 
Between 2005 and 2010 social security spending increased by 1.9\% of GDP with most of this increase, 1.8\% of GDP, due to the tax amnesty \parencite{CetrangoloG}.

\smalltitle{Calibration.}
We calibrate the model with informal savers to Argentina before the reforms and read the tax amnesties as an increase in $\epsilon$. Informal households work when young and save for retirement, facing the same rate of return on savings as formal households. A model period is thirty years and the calibration year is 2010. Table \ref{table:Arg:Calib} reports the result; appendix \ref{app:EPH} gives the household survey, the identifying formulas and the sources behind every entry.

The pension system is read off the rules in force and the OECD's replacement rates. The pre-reform $\epsilon = 0.29$ is the basic pension that the non-contributing elderly received, valued against the benefit of the least productive formal workers and discounted for the five to ten years by which it was drawn later. The share of households receiving it, $\gamma_0 = 0.32$, is the share of retirees on that pension before the reform \parencite{CetrangoloG}. The Bismarckian index $\theta = 0.84$ matches the observed ratio of replacement rates at mean and half-mean income. After the reforms we set $\epsilon=1-\theta+\theta \eta^1h_t^1/h_t$, so that at their retirement age all informal workers receive the same benefit as formal workers with the lowest productivity; that change in $\epsilon$ is the experiment of this section.

Technology, preferences and demographics are imposed. We take a formal-sector capital income share $\alpha = 0.35$, between the shares implied by the survey's informal earnings and by shadow-economy estimates of informal output, and one that puts Argentina on the same footing as the Gollin-adjusted shares we use for the rich economies in section \ref{sec:oecd}. The Frisch elasticity is $\xi = 0.30$, with values between $0.20$ and $0.40$ as robustness, and $\nu_t$ follows thirty-year gross population growth from census data and projections, as in \textcite{mkvss08}.

The remaining parameters are identified jointly with the equilibrium path, since each of them moves it. The formal productivities $\eta_i$ match the income distribution across the four formal quartiles of the survey, with one taste for leisure $X$ common to them whose level is pinned by the observed formal workweek of 42.5 hours. Relative hours across quartiles are then a prediction rather than a target, and table \ref{table:Arg:Calib} reports that prediction against the survey.\footnote{The alternative is to identify one $X_i$ per quartile from the observed relative hours, in which case hours are data at every point of the formal distribution and the level of average hours is not identified. Neither choice is innocuous, and we report the whole calibration under the second in appendix \ref{app:EPH:vectorX}. Nothing else in this section turns on it: $\beta$, $\omega$, the equilibrium tax rate, the savings rate and every counterfactual below are the same under the two variants to solver precision, because $X$ enters no aggregate.} Four parameters are then solved against four targets at the calibration year: $\beta$ against a capital-output ratio of $3.23$, $\omega$ against a social security tax rate of $10.9\%$ --- pension spending of 7.1\% of GDP before the 2001 crisis, converted at the share of formal output that accrues to labor --- and $\eta_0$ and $X_0$ against the informal households' income and hours relative to the formal average.

\smalltitle{Pension reform shocks.}
For our baseline we take the specification with log-GHH preferences and informal savings. Table \ref{table:Argentina:Universal} shows the effect of the pension reform on tax rates, savings, and labor supply. The ``Economic Equilibrium'' scenario refers to the case where we resolve for an economic equilibrium with universal pension benefits, but keep taxes constant. In general, we find higher tax rates, lower savings, and lower labor supply, with the primary driver of both savings and labor supply coming from endogenous policies: with taxes held fixed, formal savings rise, since informal households now hold a claim on the pension system and save less, and the fall in hours is under a third of its full-effect size.

In 2010 the model predicts that a shift to a more universal pension system increases the equivalent social security tax rate by about 1.25 p.p., which is an increase in pension spending of about 0.8\% of GDP. The model thus accounts for almost half of the observed increase in pension spending in this period due to the tax amnesty of around 1.8\% of GDP. The model predicts a drop of 0.3 p.p.\ of GDP in the savings rate, while average weekly labor supply drops by about 0.15 hours.\footnote{Findings consistent with \textcite{Ruffo}, that estimate that this reform had a negative impact on savings.} Informal households benefit from higher benefits and from a higher return to savings due to lower formal savings rates. The model predicts their savings drop by about 16\%. The tax response builds over time such that by 2040 the tax rate is 2.1 p.p.\ above its pre-reform path. The reason for the stronger long-run effect is that informal households, now covered, retire with less private savings, and retirees with less private income support higher taxes.

\smalltitle{The effect of the intertemporal elasticity of substitution.}
We next consider the effect of general CRRA preferences and the model predictions from the pension reform. Given that our framework features no uncertainty, the more appropriate interpretation for $\rho$ is that it is the intertemporal elasticity of substitution (IES), i.e. it reflects households attitudes towards allocation of consumption over time, not across states of nature. The empirical evidence on the magnitude of the IES remains inconclusive.\footnote{\textcite{Guvenena1}, for instance, argues that taking into account limited asset market participation and heterogeneity in wealth holdings can help reconcile the relatively low estimates obtained from aggregate consumption data with the much higher estimates derived from output or wealth measures. Asset pricing models with Epstein-Zin preferences and either long-run risk or disaster risk show that to explain empirical facts both risk aversion and IES have to be larger than one (or lower than one but risk aversion is known to be larger than one). See \textcite{BansalYa1} and \textcite{Barroa1}.} In view of these considerations, we rely on the estimates obtained under logarithmic utility as our benchmark. Table \ref{table:Argentina:funcOfRho} repeats the analysis from making pensions universal after recalibration to the pre-reform economy and figure \ref{fig:ARG:EffectOfCRRA} collects the impact in 2010 and 2040 across $\rho$.

The table and figure show that the effects of the pension reform are nonlinear in $\rho$, in particular for values lower than one. When $\rho<1$ the effects of the pension reform are dampened across all metrics: at $\rho = 0.5$ the tax rate increases by only 0.2 p.p.\ in 2010, against 1.25 p.p.\ under logarithmic utility, and the savings rate barely moves. The reason for this is that a low intertemporal elasticity makes the young resist taxation more strongly, so the recalibrated economy needs a larger political weight of the old to reproduce the observed tax rate and responds less to a change in benefits. 

For IES in the range $[1, 2]$ results are more robust to the choice of $\rho$. For example, the effect on taxes is 1.2 to 1.3 p.p.\ in 2010 and about 2.0 p.p.\ in 2040 throughout, the effect on labor supply is flat at about 0.15 hours, and the savings effect shrinks gradually, from 0.3 p.p.\ of GDP at $\rho = 1$ to 0.2 p.p.\ at $\rho = 2$. Informal savings fall by 16 to 17\% throughout this range. Since \textcite{Ruffo} find that the tax amnesty reduced savings, we regard calibrations with $\rho \in [1,2]$ as more empirically plausible. On this range the predictions are not drastically different from the logarithmic benchmark.
