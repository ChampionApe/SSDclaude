\section{Logarithmic-GHH Preferences}\label{sec:LOG}
This section derives the model's analytical results. We follow \textcite{GreenwoodHH88a1} and assume
\begin{align}
    u(c_{1,t}^i,h_{t,i})= \ln\left(\tilde{c}_{1,t}^i\right), \qquad \tilde{c}_{1,t}^i \equiv c_{1,t}^i - \dfrac{X_i\xi}{1+\xi}\left(h_{t,i}\right)^{\frac{1+\xi}{\xi}}
\end{align}
where $\xi$ is the Frisch elasticity of labor supply and $X_i$ is a type-specific taste for leisure. Proposition \ref{prop:LOG:EE} characterizes the economic equilibrium under these log-GHH preferences. If informal households are in addition hand-to-mouth, the politico-economic equilibrium carries no state (proposition \ref{prop:LOG:PEE}) and the tax rate is a function of parameters and demographics alone. The quantitative sections keep log-GHH preferences as the benchmark and let informal households save for retirement.


\subsection{Economic Equilibrium}

With log-GHH preferences, formal households $i$ supply labor and save according to
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:LOG:formalSol_i}
\begin{align}
    h_{t,i} &= \left(\dfrac{\eta_iw_t(1-\tau_t)+\frac{\theta \overline{b}_{t+1} \eta_i}{R_{t+1}}}{X_i}\right)^{\xi} \\
    s_{t,i} &= \dfrac{\beta}{1+\beta}\left[w_t\left(1-\tau_t\right)h_{t,i}\eta_{i}-\dfrac{X_{i}\xi}{1+\xi}\left(h_{t,i}\right)^{\frac{1+\xi}{\xi}}\right]-\dfrac{b_{t+1}^i/R_{t+1}}{1+\beta}.
\end{align}
\end{subequations}


Appendix \ref{app:EE} shows that in economic equilibrium every relevant variable $x$ (savings, labor supply and consumption) is a power of capital per worker times a coefficient,
\begin{align*}
    x_t = \Theta_{x,t} \left(\dfrac{s_{t-1}}{\nu_t}\right)^{\sigma_x},
\end{align*}
where the coefficient $\Theta_{x,t}$ depends on parameters and taxes and the exponent $\sigma_x$ on parameters only.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:LOG:EE}
In economic equilibrium we have the following:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Aggregate labor is decreasing in $\tau_t$, $\epsilon$ and $\gamma_0$ and increasing in $\tau_{t+1}, \theta$. Aggregate savings are decreasing in $\tau_t$, $\tau_{t+1}$, and $\theta$ and are increasing in $\epsilon$ and $\gamma_0$.
    \item Labor income inequality and relative labor supply are independent of pension policy and given by
    \begin{align}
        \dfrac{\eta_ih_{t,i}}{h_t} = \frac{\eta_i^{1+\xi}}{X_i^{\xi}\Gamma_h}, \qquad \Gamma_h \equiv \sum_i \frac{\gamma_i\eta_i^{1+\xi}}{X_i^{\xi}}.
    \end{align}
    \item Savings of formal households relative to average savings are independent of $\tau_t$ and linear in $\tau_{t+1}$:
    \begin{align}
        \frac{s_{t,i}}{s_t} = \frac{\eta_i^{1+\xi}}{X_i^{\xi}} \frac{1}{\Gamma_h} + \frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha} \frac{\tau_{t+1}}{1+\gamma_0\epsilon}\frac{1-\theta}{1+\beta}\left(\frac{\eta_i^{1+\xi}}{X_i^{\xi}\Gamma_h}-1\right).
    \end{align}
    \item When $\tau_{t+1}=0$ or with fully Bismarckian pensions ($\theta = 1$), the heterogeneity in savings exactly matches labor income inequality. An increase in $\tau_{t+1}$ or a decrease in $\theta$ amplifies the spread in private savings.
    \item Informal savings increase in $\tau_t$ and $\theta$ and decrease in $\tau_{t+1}$ and $\epsilon$; current taxes also raise their ratio to formal savings.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}


Part i of proposition \ref{prop:LOG:EE} states that current taxes reduce labor supply and savings, while future taxes increase labor supply, since higher expected benefits make work more attractive, and reduce the need to save. A higher $\theta$ increases labor supply for the same reason; with GHH preferences this raises the marginal utility of $\tilde{c}_{1,t}$ and so, through the Euler equation, lowers aggregate savings. A higher $\epsilon$ shifts future benefits toward informal households and lowers the benefit formal households expect, so that they save more and work less, and a larger share of informal households has the same effects.

Parts ii and iii reduce the state space of the politico-economic equilibrium, as the next subsection shows. Part iv implies that higher future taxes or a lower $\theta$, when pensions are redistributive, widen the dispersion of savings and thus of private wealth. Part v works through the universal pension, since informal earnings do not respond to pension policy. The pension's present value to an informal household is proportional to formal savings, with a factor that rises with future taxes and $\epsilon$: current taxes and a higher $\theta$, which lower formal savings, therefore raise informal savings, while future taxes and a higher $\epsilon$ raise the present value of the pension and lower them.


\subsection{Politico-Economic Equilibrium}

In a Markov equilibrium the distributions of past savings and past labor supply are in principle state variables of the political choice of the tax rate. Part ii of proposition \ref{prop:LOG:EE} removes the distribution of labor supply, since taxes do not move relative labor supply.\footnote{This property of GHH preferences also holds under CRRA preferences (appendix \ref{app:EE:CRRA}).} If informal households save, their past savings are a further state, and one that both current and future taxes move. For the analytical results we therefore take informal households to be hand-to-mouth: they do not save, so that their Euler condition \eqref{6d} does not apply, and when old they receive an endowment tied to the informal wage, which can be read as work in old age at a lower productivity.\footnote{We model old-age income as an endowment of goods to keep the model as symmetric with the general case as possible.}

With log-GHH preferences and hand-to-mouth informal households, the policymaker maximizes
\begin{align}\label{eq:LOG:PolObj}
    \mathcal{W}_t(z_t,\tau_t; \tau_t^{+1}(z_{t+1})) = \sum_i \gamma_i \mu_i&\left[\omega \ln\left(c_{2,t}^i\right)+\nu_t\left(\ln\left(\tilde{c}_{1,t}^i\right)+\beta\ln\left(c_{2,t+1}^i\right)\right)\right] \notag \\
    +\gamma_0\mu_0&\left[\omega \ln\left(\tilde{c}_{2,t}^0\right)+\nu_t\left(\ln\left(\tilde{c}_{1,t}^0\right)+\beta \ln\left(c_{2,t+1}^0\right)\right)\right]
\end{align}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:LOG:PEE}
In politico-economic equilibrium with hand-to-mouth informal households, all consumption functions are log-separable in the endogenous state variables, making policy only a function of parameters, including system characteristics and demographics. Politico-economic equilibrium taxes are increasing in $\omega$, decreasing in $\nu_t$, and increasing in income inequality. Furthermore they are decreasing in $\theta$. At an interior equilibrium, an increase in $\epsilon$ raises taxes if and only if
\begin{align}\label{eq:LOG:epsCondition}
    \frac{\phi_t}{\epsilon}\,\sigma_t^0\big(1-\sigma_t^0\big) > \sum_{i>0}\gamma_i\,\sigma_t^i\big(1-\sigma_t^i\big), \qquad \sigma_t^j \equiv \frac{b_t^j}{c_{2,t}^j}, \qquad \phi_t \equiv 1-\frac{(1-\alpha)\xi}{1+\alpha\xi}\,\frac{\tau_t}{1-\tau_t},
\end{align}
where $\sigma_t^j$ is the pension's share of the consumption of a retiree of type $j$, with $\tilde{c}_{2,t}^0$ in the place of $c_{2,t}^0$ for informal retirees, and $\phi_t$ is the marginal revenue of the tax relative to its base. A retiree's support for the tax rises with the pension's share of their consumption, and a share responds to a change in the benefit in proportion to $\sigma(1-\sigma)$: not at all for a retiree without a pension, and not at all for one who lives on the pension alone. The share of informal households $\gamma_0$ scales both sides and does not determine the sign.
\end{proposition}


The economic equilibrium is log-separable in the aggregate states (appendix \ref{app:EE:LOG}), and the one remaining vector of states, the distribution of formal households' savings $s_{t-1,i}/s_{t-1}$, is a function of parameters and the current tax alone; by the same token, the distribution carried into the next period does not depend on the current choice (appendix \ref{app:PEE:LOG}). Despite two dimensions of pension design and heterogeneous households, the politico-economic equilibrium therefore shares the property of \textcite{mkvss08,ssvsde15,Song12a1,UchidaOa1}, among others, that policy is a function of parameters and demographics only, although the equilibrium conditions are more complex and in most cases can be solved only numerically. 

Notwithstanding the orthogonality of current policy with state variables, the trade-offs underlying the equilibrium tax rates are dynamic in nature as they relate contemporaneous policy decisions to capital accumulation and thus future wages and tax revenue. The tractability of the model comes from specifying functional forms that render the factor price elasticities and the derivatives of the indirect utility functions orthogonal to the capital stock and relative labor supply and relative savings decisions independent of current taxes.

The comparative statics in $\omega$ and $\nu_t$ mirror those in \textcite{mkvss08} and \textcite{Song12a1}, and the latter also shows that the equilibrium tax rate increases with income inequality. A distinct contribution to the politico-economic literature on social security is the prediction that a more Bismarckian benefit formula weakens political support for taxation. The reason for this is that a higher $\theta$ shifts benefits from low-income to high-income retirees, and the former, with their higher marginal utility, weigh more in the political objective, so that retirees' support for taxes declines in the aggregate. A higher $\epsilon$ shifts benefits from formal to informal retirees, and only retirees' support moves, since the young's loss from the tax does not depend on $\epsilon$. A unit of tax raises every pension in the same proportion, so with log utility a retiree's support for the tax is the pension's share of their consumption, and a higher $\epsilon$ moves the shares, up for informal retirees and down for formal ones. A share responds to a change in the benefit in proportion to $\sigma(1-\sigma)$: a retiree who lives on the pension alone does not respond, since a higher $\epsilon$ raises their consumption and their marginal benefit in the same proportion. The gain of informal retirees and the loss of formal ones are both proportional to the number of informal households, so $\gamma_0$ does not decide the sign; what does is condition \eqref{eq:LOG:epsCondition}, in which the factor $1/\epsilon$ says that a unit of $\epsilon$ is a large proportional change of a small universal pension. The condition holds when informal retirees are poorer than formal ones and the pension is a comparable share of their consumption, as in the calibration to Argentina of section \ref{sec:argentina}, and fails when they live on the pension alone or are much richer than formal retirees.


When informal households save or preferences are CRRA, the tax rate depends on the state and the equilibrium has to be solved numerically; section \ref{sec:numerical} describes how.
