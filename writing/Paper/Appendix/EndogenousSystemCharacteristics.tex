\section{Endogenous system characteristics}\label{app:ESC}\setcounter{equation}{0}
Throughout the paper the Bismarckian index $\theta$ is calibrated and held fixed while taxes are chosen politically. This appendix endogenizes it: the design is chosen by the same probabilistic-voting objective $\mathcal{W}_t$ that sets $\tau_t$, in the log model without informal households. We explore four variants --- three \emph{timings} of the choice (sequential, leaded, permanent) and, orthogonal to the timing, a \emph{deadweight cost} of redistributive funds. The headline result is easy to state: with costless redistribution, all three timings put the design in the fully Beveridgean corner $\theta = 0$ at observed US inequality; an interior design requires redistribution through the pension system to be costly. Our preferred specification is therefore the \emph{leaded choice with a proportional deadweight cost}, calibrated so that the 2020 electorate re-elects the system it has. This appendix states the four variants, the argument for the preferred one, and the results; derivations, algorithms and the full verification battery are in the online technical documentation published alongside the paper.\footnote{\textit{Social Security Design and Its Political Support: Technical Documentation}, section \textit{Endogenous system characteristics}. It contains the model definitions and propositions for each timing, the numerical algorithms (including the exact two-dimensional policy-function recursion under CRRA and the checks behind every structural claim used here), and the calibration protocol.}

\subsection{Three timings, one corner}
\smalltitle{Sequential.} If $\theta_t$ is chosen at $t$ together with $\tau_t$, it only re-splits benefits already granted: current labor supply depends on the \emph{next} design $\theta_{t+1}$, so there is no strategic channel, and the marginal effect on the political objective runs entirely through current retirees,
\begin{align}\label{eq:esc:seqFOC}
   \dfrac{\partial \mathcal{W}_t}{\partial \theta_t} =\omega \sum_i\gamma_i\frac{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\tau_t\left(\frac{\eta_{i}^{1+\xi}}{X_{i}^{\xi}}\frac{1}{\Gamma_{h}}-1\right)}{\frac{s_{t-1}^i}{s_{t-1}}+\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\tau_t\left(1+\theta_t\left[\frac{\eta_{i}^{1+\xi}}{X_{i}^{\xi}}\frac{1}{\Gamma_{h}}-1\right]\right)}\;\leq\;0 \quad\text{for } \theta_t\in[0,1].
\end{align}
The numerator averages to zero across types while the denominator is larger for high-income retirees, so lowering $\theta_t$ redistributes from retirees with low marginal utility to retirees with high marginal utility and the choice corners at $\theta_t = 0$.

\smalltitle{Leaded.} If instead $\theta_{t+1}$ is chosen at $t$, the design becomes a state variable at $t+1$ and acquires genuine forward-looking content: it moves current labor supply and savings both directly (the economic-equilibrium effect) and through the anticipated response of $\tau_{t+1}$ (the politico-economic effect), and it enters the young's indirect utility through $\tilde{c}_{1,t}^i$ and $R_{t+1}$. The equilibrium is a sequence of policy functions over the inherited design, identified by backward induction as in appendix \ref{app:numeric}. The redistributive force of \eqref{eq:esc:seqFOC} nonetheless still dominates: at observed US inequality the choice corners at $\theta = 0$ for reasonable labor supply elasticities.

\smalltitle{Permanent.} If a design chosen at $t_0$ is expected to hold forever, it enters all channels at once --- the sequential re-splitting at $t_0$, the leaded channel through $h_{t_0}$, and the whole continuation path. Two structural results make this cheaper to solve than it looks: the joint $(\tau_{t_0},\theta)$ choice \emph{concentrates} (the tax first-order condition at any candidate $\theta$ is the ordinary one, so the choice is a one-dimensional search), and because the vote is anticipated, the predetermined savings distribution is a fixed point --- households save at $t_0-1$ against the design that wins. Both results are established and verified in the technical documentation. The outcome is again the corner $\theta = 0$; the permanent timing can only be calibrated to the observed 2020 design under counterfactually compressed inequality.

The common lesson is that in this class of models, costless within-cohort redistribution is politically too attractive: some friction must make Beveridgean pensions more expensive than Bismarckian ones for an interior design to survive.

\subsection{Costly redistribution, and the preferred specification}
We introduce a deadweight cost of raising \emph{redistributive} public funds: the government budget becomes
\begin{align}\label{eq:esc:budget}
    \nu_t w_t\tau_th_t\, f(\theta_t) = \sum_i \gamma_i b_t^i, \qquad f(\theta) = \phi + (1-\phi)\,\theta^{p}, \quad f'>0,
\end{align}
so a fraction of revenue is lost, and more of it the more Beveridgean the system ($f(1)=1$, $f(0)=\phi$). We use two placements of the wedge: the \emph{proportional} cost above, where $f$ scales all benefits, and a \emph{redistributive-only} variant where $f$ multiplies just the flat component of the benefit formula. The economic equilibrium carries through with $f$ inserted into the equilibrium functions ($\Gamma_{s}$, $\Theta_{h}$, relative savings); the sequential first-order condition acquires the terms $f'(\theta)$ and $\theta f'(\theta) + f(\theta)$ that let it cross zero in the interior. The explicit expressions are in the technical documentation.

Our preferred specification combines the \emph{leaded timing} with the \emph{proportional cost}, for three reasons. First, the sequential choice is static by construction, and the permanent choice requires commitment expectations that sit awkwardly beside the paper's Markov equilibria; the leaded choice keeps the Markov structure and gives the design the forward-looking channel the data narrative is about. Second, identification is clean under the proportional cost: $f$ cancels from the replacement-rate ratio that identifies $\theta$ from the OECD data, so the observed design remains the data's own $\theta^{\ast} = 0.738$ and the cost parameter is identified separately, by requiring that the design the electorate picks at the calibration year reproduces the observed one,
\begin{align}\label{eq:esc:calibration}
    \Theta_{t_0}\!\left(\theta^{\ast}\right) = \theta^{\ast},
\end{align}
with $(\beta,\omega)$ recalibrated at every trial value of $p$ and $\phi$ imposed.\footnote{Under the redistributive-only variant $f$ does \emph{not} cancel from the replacement-rate ratio, so $\theta^{\ast}$ and $p$ are jointly identified; it is retained as robustness throughout. The imposed $\phi$ matters mainly through the calibrated $p$: at $\rho = 1$, $\phi = 0.25/0.5/0.75$ give $p = 0.26/0.40/0.94$ under the proportional cost.} Third, everything after $t_0$ is then a prediction: as the demographic ratio moves, the fixed point moves with it, which is the object of interest.

Table \ref{table:US_ESC:calibration} reports the calibrated cost across the paper's range of intertemporal elasticities. The wedge required for the electorate to re-elect the observed design falls steeply in $\rho$ --- from $p = 0.95$ at $\rho = 0.5$ to $p = 0.086$ at $\rho = 2$ under the proportional cost --- because a higher elasticity strengthens the forward-looking (savings and labor supply) stake in a Bismarckian design, so less friction is needed to hold the choice away from the Beveridgean corner.

\smalltitle{Solution.} At $\rho = 1$ the leaded equilibrium is solved exactly: the tax choice is static in the design state, and the choice of $\theta_{t+1}$ is provably independent of both $s_{t-1}$ and $\theta_t$, so the backward recursion is one-dimensional. Under CRRA neither property survives and the honest object is a policy-function recursion over the two-dimensional state $(s_{t-1},\theta_t)$. We compute that recursion and use it to certify a cheaper path iteration; the two agree to $\pm 0.01$ in the design and $2\times10^{-4}$ in the tax --- the objective is flat near its maximum, which bounds what any method can resolve --- and the tables below use the certified method. Details and the verification battery are in the technical documentation.

\subsection{Results}
By construction the electorate re-elects $\theta^{\ast}$ in 2020 at every $\rho$. Along the baseline demographic path the chosen design then drifts Bismarckian as ageing proceeds --- at $\rho = 1$ from $0.738$ to $0.764$ by 2110, at $\rho = 2$ to $0.795$. The counterfactuals below are dated 2020 and reported at 2050 ($t_0+1$): under the leaded timing a shock in 2020 cannot move the design before the next period, so 2020 outcomes are identical with and without the endogenous choice, and 2050 is the first date that carries the design response. Each table compares the shocked economy with the design \emph{pinned} at its calibrated value (exogenous $\theta$) and with the design \emph{chosen} (endogenous $\theta$), at $\rho \in \{0.5, 1, 2\}$.

\smalltitle{Ageing.} Acute ageing ($\nu_t = 1$ from 2020) raises the chosen design at every elasticity --- to $0.77$ at $\rho \leq 1$ and $0.81$ at $\rho = 2$ (Table \ref{table:US_ESC:ageing}). The direction is intuitive: a scarcer young generation makes the deadweight cost of the Beveridgean component harder to carry. Quantitatively the design response is a side channel here: taxes and savings move essentially as in the exogenous-$\theta$ reading, with the endogenous design shaving the tax response slightly --- part of the fiscal adjustment happens through the system's shape rather than its size.

\smalltitle{Income distribution.} Imposing France's flatter income distribution re-derives the data-implied design at $\theta = 0.50$; the electorate instead pushes the chosen design to the fully Bismarckian corner $\theta = 1$ at \emph{every} $\rho$ (Table \ref{table:US_ESC:incomeDistr}) --- it un-does the flattening entirely. This overturns the exogenous-$\theta$ reading of the same counterfactual in two ways. The tax is pulled back up toward baseline (at $\rho = 1$, $19.1\%$ against $16.7\%$ pinned): with the deadweight cost in place, equilibrium taxes are no longer decreasing in $\theta$ --- under the French distribution they are increasing in it, so a more Bismarckian design and a larger system come together. And the savings-rate gain that makes the pinned counterfactual look transformative is almost entirely erased ($20.2\%$ against $22.3\%$, at a baseline of $20.3\%$). The model thus ties the design to inequality far more tightly than the cross-country data do --- the same tension the introduction's cross-section poses.

\smalltitle{Leisure preferences.} French leisure preferences are a pure rescaling of $X_i$, which by the model's scale invariance moves hours and nothing else; consistently, the chosen design does not move at any $\rho$ and the endogenous rows reproduce the exogenous ones (Table \ref{table:US_ESC:leisure}). This is a useful placebo: the machinery does not manufacture design responses where the theory says there are none.

\smalltitle{Voting patterns.} France's much flatter voting-weight profile pushes the design Beveridgean, and this is the one experiment whose strength depends sharply on the elasticity: the chosen design falls from $0.738$ to $0.66$ at $\rho = 0.5$, $0.54$ at $\rho = 1$, and $0.28$ at $\rho = 2$ (Table \ref{table:US_ESC:voting}). When the poor carry more relative voting weight, the redistributive force of \eqref{eq:esc:seqFOC} regains ground against the forward-looking stake --- and the latter is what scales with $\rho$. Taxes end somewhat higher than in the pinned reading, again reflecting that taxes rise as the design moves under the wedge.

\smalltitle{Both at once.} Imposing France's income distribution and voting profile together --- the pair the cross-country comparison actually confronts --- the two forces pull the design in opposite directions, and which wins is an elasticity question: the chosen design corners at $\theta = 1$ for $\rho \leq 1$ but comes back interior at $0.69$ for $\rho = 2$ (proportional cost; $0.85/0.83/0.72$ under the redistributive-only variant). Whether ``French characteristics'' raise or lower the Bismarckian index in the model is therefore not identified by the cross-section alone --- it turns on a preference parameter the cross-section cannot pin.
