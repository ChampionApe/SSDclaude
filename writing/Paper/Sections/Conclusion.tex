\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclu}

This paper studies how two features of a pension system, whether benefits require a contribution record and how closely they follow past earnings, shape its political support and through it taxes, savings and labor supply. The setting is an overlapping generations model with capital, heterogeneous households, an informal sector and probabilistic voting, first over the size of the system and then over its design as well.

With log-GHH preferences and hand-to-mouth informal households the equilibrium tax is a function of parameters and demographics alone. It rises with the political weight of the old, with ageing and with income inequality, and it falls when benefits are more closely tied to earnings, since earnings-related benefits move revenue from poor retirees to rich ones; universal pensions raise the tax when informal retirees are poorer than formal ones and the pension is a comparable share of their consumption, whatever the share of informal households. Calibrated to Argentina, the model accounts for almost half of the rise in pension spending that followed the expansion of coverage of 2005 to 2010, with the savings rate lower by 0.3 p.p.\ of GDP and the workweek by 0.1 hours. Calibrated to the U.S., France and the UK, ageing is the main determinant of the tax rate and the earnings link the second; income inequality and voting patterns matter little, and a higher intertemporal elasticity of substitution changes only the size of the earnings-link effect.

When the electorate chooses the earnings link as well, costless redistribution puts the choice at a corner: with propensities to vote that do not rise with income the design chosen with the tax is fully Beveridgean, and at U.S.\ inequality every timing of the choice gives a corner. An interior design needs flat benefits to be costlier than earnings-related ones. With a deadweight cost of redistribution that rises with the size of the system, calibrated on the U.S.\ design alone, ageing makes the chosen design more Bismarckian, inequality moves it little, and the single cost parameter orders the designs of the UK, the U.S.\ and France as observed; the UK's own cost is within 17\% of the U.S.\ value at intertemporal elasticities of one or below, though not at two. This is the pattern of the OECD cross-section: spending and the earnings link rise together as population growth falls, and neither follows inequality.

Two limitations point to future work. The model has no risk; in \textcite{BergGEwp} we study a related environment in which health risk may prevent households from qualifying for a contributive pension, and find that households work and save more in response. And the cost specification cannot deliver France's fully Bismarckian system, because under a cost quadratic in the redistribution performed the first unit of redistribution is free at the margin; a linear term in the cost, or a formality margin, toward which the informal sector of our Argentine model is a first step, would allow it. The same margin bears on the rise of platform work and other arrangements outside contributive coverage in rich economies, which the model can address.
