\section{Pension System Reform in Argentina}\label{sec:argentina}\label{Argentina}

Argentina is a middle-income country with high labor informality. In 1994 the social security system was reformed tying full benefits to 30 years of contributions, which left about a third of the elderly without a pension by 2004.\footnote{The reform also let workers opt for a fully funded system, whose funds were nationalized in December 2008.} From 2005 a series of tax amnesties let workers with shorter contribution records draw a pension. They added 2.7 million beneficiaries by 2011 \parencite{RofmanA15a1}, raising the number of pensioners from about 3 to 5 million (figure \ref{fig:Arg}) and the coverage of the elderly from 68\% to over 90\% \parencite{Roffman}. Thus, in a few years, Argentina experienced a significant increase in non-contributive pensions: Between 2005 and 2010 social security spending increased by 1.9\% of GDP with most of this increase, 1.8\% of GDP, due to the tax amnesties \parencite{CetrangoloG}.

The following outlines the calibration procedure and presents the main results from the pension reform. The reform is the only change in the universal pension $\epsilon$ that the paper considers, and it is taken as given: when the electorate also chooses the design, in section \ref{sec:esc}, it chooses $\theta$ and not $\epsilon$. Appendix \ref{app:EPH} keeps calibration details and the \oa{arg} provides supplementary evidence from the reform.\footnote{Appendix \ref{app:EPH} holds the data, the identifying formulas and the sources of the calibration. The online appendix has additional evidence including how the design $(\epsilon, \theta)$ shapes equilibrium outcomes and how different calibrations affect results.}


\smalltitle{Calibration.}
We calibrate the model with informal savers to Argentina before the reforms and read the tax amnesties as an increase in $\epsilon$. Informal households work when young and save for retirement, facing the same rate of return on savings as formal households. A model period is thirty years, the calibration year is 2010, and every household has the same propensity to vote. Table \ref{table:Arg:Calib} reports the result. The pre-reform $\epsilon = 0.21$ is the basic pension of retirees without a full contribution record, 70\% of the benefit of the least productive formal workers, in units of the average formal benefit and discounted because it starts five to ten years after the formal retirement age, and $\gamma_0 = 0.47$, informal households per formal household, puts informal households at 32\% of all, the share of the population over 65 without a pension before the reform \parencite{Roffman}. Since some of these households would never have drawn the basic pension, which required ten years of contributions, we also report the reform from a pre-reform $\epsilon = 0$ (appendix \ref{app:EPH}). The design $\theta = 0.84$ matches the observed ratio of replacement rates at mean and half-mean income. The reform raises $\epsilon$ to the universal level $\epsilon^U = 1-\theta+\theta\eta_1h_{t-1,1}/h_{t-1}$ of section \ref{sec:model}, at which informal retirees receive the benefit of the least productive formal ones. We impose a formal-sector capital share of $\alpha = 0.35$, between the values implied by the survey's informal earnings and by shadow-economy estimates of informal output, a Frisch elasticity of $\xi = 0.30$, and a ratio of workers to retirees $\nu_t$ from thirty-year gross growth of the total population in the estimates and medium-variant projections of the United Nations' World Population Prospects 2024, as in \textcite{mkvss08}.

The remaining parameters are identified jointly with the equilibrium path. The formal productivities $\eta_i$ match the income distribution across the four formal quartiles of the survey, with a taste for leisure $X$ common to them whose level is pinned by the observed formal workweek of 42.5 hours.\footnote{Identifying one $X_i$ per quartile instead takes the survey's relative hours as data and leaves the level of hours unidentified (appendix \ref{app:EPH:vectorX}). Since the productivity distribution reaches the aggregates only through a sum that both calibrations normalize to one, only $\eta_i$ and $X_i$ differ, and every result of this section is the same under both.} Relative hours across quartiles are then a prediction, which reproduces the survey's ranking and overstates the hours of the top quartile, 1.18 against 1.10 times the formal average. Four parameters are solved against four targets in 2010: $\beta$ against a capital-output ratio of $3.23$, $\omega$ against an equivalent social security tax rate of 11.8\%, which is pension spending of 7.1\% of GDP before the 2001 crisis divided by formal labor income, 60\% of GDP once GDP includes the output of informal households, and $\eta_0$ and $X_0$ against the informal households' income and hours relative to the formal average.

\smalltitle{The reform.}
Table \ref{table:Argentina:Universal} reports the effect in 2010 of an unanticipated and permanent rise of $\epsilon$ to $\epsilon^U$ under log-GHH preferences, first with the tax rate held at its pre-reform path and then chosen politically. Since the rise is unanticipated, the retirees of 2010 enter it with the savings they made under the pre-reform system. We find that taxes increase, which drives down savings and labor supply. With taxes held fixed, the new pensions are paid for by cutting formal benefits by 12.7\%, and formal households save more and work marginally less, since the reform lowers the benefit they expect (proposition \ref{prop:LOG:EE}); informal households, who now hold a claim on the pension system, save less.

In 2010 the model predicts that the social security tax rate rises by 2.0 p.p., enough to pay for the new pensions and to raise formal benefits by 1.5\%, so that none of the expansion is financed by cuts. The reason for this is that the reform moves benefits toward informal retirees, who are poorer than formal ones and value benefits more at the margin.\footnote{The tax also rises when informal households are hand-to-mouth, consistent with condition \eqref{eq:LOG:epsCondition} in appendix \ref{app:PEE:LOG}.} The tax response builds over time, to 3.5 p.p.\ above the pre-reform path by 2040, because informal households, now covered, save less for retirement and as retirees demand more taxation. In 2010 the savings rate falls by 0.4 p.p.\ of GDP and the average workweek by a quarter of an hour.

Two moments of the episode can be set against the model, the rise in pension spending and the response of household savings. Pension spending rises by 1.2\% of GDP, and spending on informal retirees, which corresponds to the cost of the amnesties, by 1.1\% of GDP, in each case about three fifths of its observed counterpart: the 1.9\% of GDP by which spending rose between 2005 and 2010 and the 1.8\% of GDP that the amnesties added.\footnote{The observed rise starts from 2005, when spending had fallen to 5.2\% of GDP because only minimum pensions were adjusted for inflation after the 2001 crisis, while the model's pre-reform economy is calibrated to the pre-crisis 7.1\% (appendix \ref{app:EPH}). The nationalization of the funded system in December 2008 moved its members and their contributions into the public system in the same window; it bears on the comparison only through spending, and the 1.8\% of GDP counts the beneficiaries of the amnesties alone. Finally, a model period is thirty years, so the model's response in 2010 is a shift in spending that lasts a generation, which we compare with the shift reached within the five years of the amnesties.} The comparison depends on how the pre-reform system is read. If no informal retiree drew a pension before the reform, so that the model is recalibrated at $\epsilon = 0$ and the reform raises $\epsilon$ from zero, the tax rises by 4.2 p.p., pension spending by 2.5\% of GDP and spending on informal retirees by 2.0\% of GDP, more than observed: the two readings bracket the data. \textcite{Ruffo} compare the national expenditure surveys of 2004/05 and 2012/13 by a difference-in-differences in which a household's treatment is its predicted probability of drawing a pension through the amnesties. Household savings, income less consumption, fall for households whose head is of retirement age and, in a specification by cohort, for households of every age: a probability of treatment higher by ten points lowers savings by about 2 to 4\%. The model's counterpart of the effect on younger households is the difference between the shares of disposable labor income that informal and formal young households save, which falls by 5.4 p.p., 5.2 p.p.\ of it with the tax held fixed; retired households do not save in the model.

\smalltitle{The effect of the intertemporal elasticity of substitution.}
Since the environment has no uncertainty, we read the parameter $\rho$ of CRRA preferences as the intertemporal elasticity of substitution (IES). The evidence on its size is inconclusive,\footnote{Estimates from aggregate consumption data are low, which \textcite{Guvenena1} reconciles with higher values from output and wealth data through limited asset market participation, and asset-pricing models with Epstein--Zin preferences and long-run or disaster risk require an IES above one \parencite{BansalYa1,Barroa1}.} and we keep logarithmic utility as the benchmark. Figure \ref{fig:ARG:EffectOfCRRA} repeats the reform with the pre-reform economy calibrated at each $\rho$ and traces the effects in 2010 and 2040 for taxes, savings rates, average workweek, and informal savings.

The effects of the pension reform are nonlinear in $\rho$. For an IES between 1 and 2 the results stay close to the log benchmark: the tax rises by 2.0 to 2.2 p.p.\ in 2010, the workweek shortens by roughly 0.25 hours, and the fall in the savings rate shrinks from 0.4 to 0.3 p.p.\ of GDP. Results are similar in 2040, albeit with tax and workweek effects amplified. Below log utility the responses of the tax rate, savings and hours shrink, and the tax response changes sign between $\rho = 0.6$ and $\rho = 0.5$, at about $0.54$: at $\rho = 0.5$ the tax rate falls by 0.4 p.p.\ on impact, the savings rate rises by 0.2 p.p.\ of GDP and the workweek lengthens slightly, although by 2040 the tax is 1.8 p.p.\ above its pre-reform path. Only the fall in the informal savings ratio, 5.8 to 6.1 p.p., is about the same at every $\rho$. The reversal lies where the calibration is least reliable: it needs a thirty-year discount factor above one, 1.44 at $\rho = 0.5$ and 1.12 at $\rho = 0.6$ (\oa{arg-ies-calibration}), and it is less precise there than elsewhere on the grid.

A decomposition of the 2010 tax condition by group shows that the reversal does not come from the formal young, whose resistance to a higher tax is about the same at $\rho = 0.5$ as at log, but from informal households.\footnote{Each group's contribution is the change that the reform makes to its term of the first order condition at the pre-reform tax, divided by the slope of the post-reform condition between the two taxes, so that the contributions sum to the tax response. At $\rho = 1$ and $\rho = 0.5$ the informal retirees raise the tax by 8.2 and 4.3 p.p., while the informal young lower it by 2.9 and 4.0 p.p., the formal retirees by 2.7 and 0.6 p.p.\ and the formal young by 0.6 and 0.2 p.p.; the retirees' terms through hours contribute less than 0.2 p.p. The formal young's share of the slope is 0.37 and 0.39, the informal retirees' 0.33 and 0.51.} With a low IES the informal retirees' marginal utility falls faster as their pension rises, so they ask for a smaller rise in the tax. And the informal young, who will now draw a pension, withdraw more of their support for a higher tax than at log, since a higher tax lowers savings and with them the value of that pension. At $\rho = 0.5$ the second force offsets nearly all of the first, and the formal retirees, whose benefits fall at a given tax, tip the balance.

Since \textcite{Ruffo} compare households, their estimate does not discriminate between values of the IES: the share of disposable labor income that informal young households save falls against that of formal ones by 4.9 to 5.4 p.p.\ at $\rho = 0.5$, 1 and 2 alike. Read as a fall in the aggregate savings rate, their sign excludes only values of $\rho$ near 0.5, below about 0.6.
