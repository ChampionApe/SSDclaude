\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Pensions are the largest transfer programme of most governments. In 2020 public pension spending averaged about 8\% of GDP across the OECD and close to 4\% in emerging and middle-income economies.\footnote{The dispersion is wide even among rich countries: a bit more than 3\% of GDP in Ireland and Chile and more than 17\% in Italy and Greece \parencite{OECDpen}.} Systems differ along two key dimensions. The first concerns eligibility and coverage: some condition benefits on a contribution record, others offer universal entitlements. The second is the link between benefits and past earnings: Bismarckian systems tie the two closely, whereas Beveridgean systems pay flat benefits. Both dimensions shape the labor supply of workers, the redistribution embedded in the system, and the coalition of voters that supports it.

Given their size, it is no surprise that the design of social security systems remains a central concern in policy debates. As populations age, policymakers must navigate difficult trade-offs among intergenerational equity, fiscal sustainability, and labor market efficiency. Moreover, pension arrangements are not determined by actuarial considerations alone: they are the outcome of political processes in which heterogeneous voters and organized interests shape the level and composition of benefits and contributions. A political-economy perspective is therefore critical for understanding why countries differ in the size of their systems and in their design. Figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata} shows how they differ across the 29 countries that were in the OECD before 2000. Pension spending and the Bismarckian--Beveridgean index, the ratio of replacement rates at mean and at half mean earnings, both rise as population growth falls, and they rise together. Neither is related to income inequality. The last fact runs against the leading theory of pension design, that of \textcite{CondeRuizP07a1}, in which high inequality leads a coalition of the rich and the poor to choose a small Beveridgean system and low inequality leads the middle class to choose a larger Bismarckian one, and few other politico-economic models have integrated the design. We address this gap with a dynamic politico-economic model and ask whether one political process can deliver both the size of the system and its design, and their relation to ageing but not to inequality.

We study the question in an overlapping generations model with capital accumulation and heterogeneous households. Households consume, save, and supply labor, and as voters, they choose among policies on labor income taxes and retirement benefits. Policy preferences vary by income and age; we model the resolution of these conflicts through probabilistic voting with elections taking place every period. Policy decisions not only impact economic outcomes, but also affect future policy decisions. Voters take these effects into account. We study Markov equilibria in which only fundamental state variables enter. Households differ in their productivity and propensity to vote. In addition, a share of households work in an informal sector or get income from home production, where labor is not taxed and savings do not enter the capital stock. Preferences are those of \textcite{GreenwoodHH88a1}, so that labor supply depends on the after-tax wage and how current effort raises future benefits. Benefits combine a flat component with an earnings-related one, the weight of the second being the Bismarckian index, and informal households receive a universal pension whose level is the second design parameter.

We show that when utility is logarithmic, politico-economic equilibria retain analytical tractability despite the presence of heterogeneity and institutional richness. With hand-to-mouth informal households, the equilibrium tax is a function of parameters and demographics alone. The analytical frame clarifies how population ageing, retirees' political influence, pension design, and income inequality shape the equilibrium tax rate. A more Bismarckian benefit formula lowers the equilibrium tax, since it shifts benefits from poor retirees, who value them more at the margin, to rich ones, so that retirees as a group support a smaller system. A more universal pension raises the tax when informal retirees are poorer than formal ones and the pension is a comparable share of their consumption, since a retiree's support for the tax moves with that share and a unit of universal pension is a large proportional change of a small one; the share of informal households scales the gains and the losses alike and does not decide the sign. When utility is constant relative risk aversion (CRRA), we show that the state space for equilibrium policies can be reduced to a single aggregate state and provide numerical solutions.\footnote{When informal households can save, the state space includes two states.}

We present two empirical applications of the model. First, we study Argentina as the case in which the design changed: from 2005 a series of tax amnesties raised the coverage of the elderly from 68\% to almost universal, and pension spending by 1.8\% of GDP. Implementing this reform in our calibrated model accounts for almost half of the observed rise in pension spending. The model also predicts modest declines in labor supply and savings, consistent with available evidence \parencite{Ruffo}. Second, initially taking the design as given, we calibrate the model to the U.S., France, and the UK, three advanced economies with distinct pension system characteristics, demographics, and inequality profiles. We find that the size of the pension system increases strongly with ageing and flat benefits and responds only little to income inequality, leisure preferences, and voting profiles.

We then let the electorate choose the earnings link as well as the tax. Without a cost of redistribution the choice is a corner. The flat component of benefits is a pure transfer within the retired cohort, from retirees above mean income to those below, and an electorate that weighs retirees by their marginal utility wants all of it: we prove that unless propensities to vote rise sharply with income, the design chosen with the tax is fully Beveridgean, whatever the tax rate, the demography and the income distribution.\footnote{We also explore different ways of choosing the pension design, i.e. choosing the design one period in advance or choosing a permanent structure. We test the variants numerically varying parameters like the labor supply elasticity and the intertemporal elasticity of substitution, but no plausible combination avoids a corner choice for the observed U.S. level of inequality.}

An interior design requires flat benefits to be more expensive than earnings-related ones. We introduce a deadweight cost of the redistribution the flat component performs, read as the tax component of contributions: it is quadratic in the flat share of benefits, proportional to the dispersion of incomes and, per unit of revenue, rising with the size of the system. A single cost parameter is calibrated so that the 2020 U.S.\ electorate re-elects its own design, and it is tested on the UK and France, whose designs it orders as observed. With this formulation, the model predicts that an ageing population chooses both a more Bismarckian design and higher pension spending, whereas the income distribution has little effect on either; this is in line with the evidence in figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}.

Throughout, we use the logarithmic utility as our baseline, but explore different combinations of labor supply elasticities and intertemporal elasticities of substitution (IES). Results are generally qualitatively consistent across different elasticities, with an exception being when the electorate chooses the earnings link for high values of IES. In this case, voting profiles and income distribution become important drivers of the size of the pension system.

The paper belongs to the literature on politico-economic equilibria of social security \parencite[e.g.][]{CooleyS99a1,Galasso99a1,Forni05a1,mkvss08,Song12a1}, surveyed by \textcite{GalassoP02a1}. As in \textcite{mkvss08} and \textcite{Song12a1}, under log-GHH preferences the equilibrium has no strategic effects and taxes increase with the political power of retirees, income inequality and ageing; as in \textcite{UchidaOa1}, the equilibrium tax is a function of parameters and demographics alone; and as in \textcite{mkvss05}, the effect of ageing under CRRA preferences is close to the logarithmic one after recalibration. What we add is a pension system that differs in two dimensions, in coverage and in the earnings link, and workers who differ in productivity and in their propensity to vote. Neither adds a state when informal households are hand-to-mouth, and the calibrated model quantifies what each feature of the design does to the tax rate, labor supply and savings.

On the design of the system, \textcite{GalassoP02a1} leave open why some systems are Beveridgean and others Bismarckian, and why the Bismarckian ones are larger, two questions that a vote on the size of the system alone cannot answer. \textcite{Casamattaa1} study the political support of Bismarckian against Beveridgean benefits, \textcite{CondeRuizP07a1} the joint choice of size and design, \textcite{KoethenbuergerPP08a1} the trade-off by which more redistributive systems are smaller, and \textcite{CremerDMP07a1} the choice of type and generosity. We embed the choice in a dynamic equilibrium with capital accumulation and labor supply, in which the design is chosen with the tax at every date, and take it to the data.
