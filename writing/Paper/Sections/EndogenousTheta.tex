\section{Endogenous Pension Design}\label{sec:esc}
Throughout the paper so far the Bismarckian index $\theta$ is calibrated and held fixed while the tax rate is chosen politically. This section lets the electorate choose the design as well. The design is set by the same probabilistic-voting objective $\mathcal{W}_t$ that sets $\tau_t$, in the model without informal households, so that one political process delivers both the size of the system and its shape. The section presents the results under different model specifications; the derivations are in appendix \ref{app:esc}, and the algorithms and additional details in the technical documentation \parencite{BergGE26doc}.

\subsection{A deadweight cost of redistribution}\label{sec:esc:cost}
Without a cost of redistribution the choice of the design is a corner. The flat component of benefits is a pure transfer within the retired cohort, from retirees above mean income to retirees below it, and a design chosen together with the tax moves no economic decision, since current labor supply and savings depend on the design that will apply to the benefits they earn. An electorate that weighs retirees by their marginal utility therefore wants all of the transfer. Proposition \ref{prop:esc:corner} in appendix \ref{app:esc:corner} proves that for propensities to vote that do not rise with income the design chosen with the tax is the Beveridgean corner, $\theta_t = 0$, whatever the tax rate, the political weight of the old, the demography, the income distribution and the intertemporal elasticity. Propensities to vote that rise with income, as in the U.S., can overturn the corner but do not: at the U.S.\ calibration the choice is a corner whether the design is chosen with the tax, one period in advance or once and for all (appendix \ref{app:esc:corner}). An interior design requires a friction that makes flat benefits costlier than earnings-related ones.

We introduce a deadweight cost of the redistribution that the flat component performs, so that the government budget becomes
\begin{align}\label{eq:esc:budget}
    \nu_t w_t\tau_th_t\, f(\theta_t,\tau_t) &= \sum_i \gamma_i b_t^i, \\
    f(\theta_t,\tau_t) &= \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\lambda\,\tau_t\,\tilde{V}\,(1-\theta_t)^2\right), \qquad \tilde{V} \equiv \sum_i\gamma_i\frac{(y_i-1)^2}{y_i}, \nonumber
\end{align}
where $\lambda \geq 0$ is the cost parameter, $y_i$ is the labor income of type $i$ relative to the mean and a share $1-f$ of revenue does not reach retirees. The form follows from reading the flat component as a tax, the tax component of contributions of \textcite{Summers89a1}. Relative to a purely earnings-related system paying the same revenue, the flat component taxes the past earnings of types above mean income and subsidizes those of types below it, a pure transfer in the aggregate, and the deadweight loss of these wedges \parencite{Harberger64a1} is, as a share of revenue, quadratic in the flat share $1-\theta_t$, proportional to the dispersion of relative incomes $\tilde{V}$ and linear in the size of the system $\tau_t$ (appendix \ref{app:esc:cost}). Nothing is lost when nothing is redistributed: $f = 1$ at $\theta_t = 1$, and for every design when all incomes are equal, where a flat and an earnings-related benefit pay the same to everyone. The last property makes a larger system costlier to keep redistributive, the efficiency--redistribution trade-off by which \textcite{KoethenbuergerPP08a1} account for the finding of \textcite{CondeRuizP07a1} that more redistributive systems are smaller, read from the other side.

The model contains the hours response to the wedge, through the Frisch elasticity; the cost stands for the margins it does not contain, participation and the reporting of earnings, on which the evidence finds the tax component of contributions to matter \parencite{Disney04a1,KumlerVF20a1}. The parameter $\lambda$ is thus a reduced form for whatever makes redistribution through the pension system expensive, and its discipline is the cross-country test of section \ref{sec:esc:calibration}: one value, calibrated on the U.S.\ design alone, has to place the designs of the UK and France.\footnote{The cost is proportional, scaling both components of the benefit alike. A cost on the flat component only delivers the same endpoints and differs in the interior, at a cost in identification, since $f$ then no longer cancels from the replacement-rate ratio and $\theta^{\ast}$ and $\lambda$ are identified jointly. A cost attached to the design itself rather than to the transfer it performs, $f(\theta) = \phi + (1-\phi)\theta^{p}$, stays in place when a compressed income distribution removes the redistributive stakes; the \oa{esc-scale} reports it as a robustness check.}

The cost leaves the solution of sections \ref{sec:model} to \ref{sec:numerical} intact. The design enters the economic equilibrium only through the two groups that split the benefit, so the cost is exactly the substitution of $A(\theta_t,\tau_t) = f\,\theta_t$ and $B(\theta_t,\tau_t) = f\,(1-\theta_t)$ for $\theta_t$ and $1-\theta_t$ in the equilibrium functions of appendix \ref{app:EE} (appendix \ref{app:esc:equilibrium}). Because it is proportional it cancels from the replacement-rate ratio that identifies $\theta$ from the OECD data, so the data target is unchanged, $\theta^{\ast} = 0.738$. In the tax condition it adds one factor: since $f$ falls with the tax that funds it, a larger system is worth less to a retiree at the margin than the revenue it collects.

We let the design be chosen one period in advance. A design chosen for the current period moves no labor supply or savings decision, so the cost would be the only force against the corner; a design chosen once and for all requires a commitment that the Markov equilibria of the rest of the paper do not have. The choice one period in advance keeps the Markov structure, it is the only timing in which the design affects labor supply and savings before it binds, the channel through which $\theta$ moves taxes and savings in section \ref{sec:oecd}, and it mirrors how reforms of benefit formulas are legislated, applying to future benefits and leaving benefits in payment untouched. The design in force, $\theta_t$, is then a state for the tax, and under log preferences the design chosen at $t$ depends on neither the design inherited nor aggregate savings (proposition \ref{prop:esc:separability}), so each date's choice is a one-dimensional search. What makes an interior choice possible is that with the cost a more Bismarckian design also raises the total the young will receive, since it dissipates less: $\partial_{\theta}A + \partial_{\theta}B = f_{\theta} \geq 0$, zero at $\theta = 1$ and rising with the flat share, the dispersion of incomes and the size of the system the young anticipate, so the redistributive stake of the young no longer dominates at every design (appendix \ref{app:esc:equilibrium}).

\subsection{Calibration and a cross-country test}\label{sec:esc:calibration}
The cost parameter is identified separately from the design, by requiring that the design in force in the calibration year, on a path where the political choice binds from the first period, is the observed one,
\begin{align}\label{eq:esc:calibration}
    \theta_{t_0} = \Theta_{t_0-1}\!\left(\theta^{\ast}\right) = \theta^{\ast},
\end{align}
with $(\beta,\omega)$ recalibrated at every trial value of $\lambda$. The calibration pins the design in the calibration year only; every later design is a prediction of the model. Table \ref{table:US_ESC:calibration} reports the calibrated cost across the paper's range of intertemporal elasticities, at the U.S.\ dispersion of relative incomes $\tilde{V} = 0.436$: $\lambda = 18.267$, $8.643$ and $1.724$ at $\rho = 0.5, 1, 2$, a fall by a factor of about ten.\footnote{The shape of $f$ follows from the tax component, but its size does not: at $\rho \leq 1$ the calibrated $\lambda$ is more than an order of magnitude above what participation and taxable-income elasticities of $0.1$ to $0.4$ would give \parencite{ChettyGMW11a1,SaezSG12a1}, and at $\rho = 2$ three to thirteen times as large (appendix \ref{app:esc:cost}), so $\lambda$ also stands in for administrative, evasion and political costs of redistribution that the model does not contain.} At those values the observed design loses $3.9\%$, $1.8\%$ and $0.4\%$ of revenue at the 2020 size of the system, and a fully flat system of the same size would lose $44\%$, $24\%$ and $5\%$. The reason for this is that a higher elasticity strengthens the forward-looking channels of the design choice, so less friction is needed to hold the choice away from the Beveridgean corner. That the calibrated cost lies closest to estimated elasticities at $\rho = 2$ adds to the case of section \ref{sec:argentina} for $\rho \in [1,2]$ as the more plausible range.

The cost is calibrated on one datum, the U.S.\ design, so the UK and France are a test of the specification rather than of its fit. Table \ref{table:US_ESC:country} lets each electorate choose the design on its own calibrated path under the U.S.\ parameter $\lambda = 8.643$, at $\rho = 1$. The UK, with an observed design of $0.560$ and a tax rate of $11.9\%$, chooses $0.625$; France, with the corner $\theta = 1$ and $21.3\%$, chooses $0.749$; the U.S.\ re-elects $0.738$ by construction. The observed ranking of the three is thus reproduced from one parameter, though compressed. The reason for this is the size of the system: the UK at its own income cuts and France at the U.S.\ percentiles have nearly the same dispersion of relative incomes, $\tilde{V} = 0.223$ and $0.218$, about half the U.S.\ value, and what separates their chosen designs is the tax that funds them, which is the relation between the size of the system and its design that figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata} shows. Calibrating the UK's own cost, so that its electorate re-elects $0.560$, gives $\lambda = 7.264$ at $\rho = 1$, within $16\%$ of the U.S.\ value, and $17\%$ below it at $\rho = 0.5$; the agreement breaks at $\rho = 2$, where the UK's own cost, which falls less steeply in the elasticity than the U.S.\ one, lies well above it (\oa{esc-uk}). Regrouping the UK's households at the U.S.\ income percentiles lowers its $\tilde{V}$ to $0.065$ and its observed design, re-read at those groups, to $0.543$, and moves the choice under the U.S.\ parameter only to $0.644$, so the result does not turn on how the income groups are cut. France has no own cost: its observed design is the corner, and under a cost quadratic in the redistribution performed the first unit of redistribution is free at the margin, so no electorate chooses exactly $\theta = 1$.\footnote{A linear term in $1-\theta$ would restore that possibility at the price of a second parameter, which the UK's design could pin with France as the prediction; we leave it for future work.}

\subsection{Ageing, inequality and voting patterns}\label{sec:esc:results}
Figure \ref{fig:US_ESC:overview} and table \ref{table:US_ESC:summary} report the counterfactuals of section \ref{sec:oecd} with the design chosen: acute ageing, France's income distribution, France's voting patterns, and the last two together. Each is a separate equilibrium path read in 2020, as in section \ref{sec:oecd}, and is read twice, with the design pinned at the U.S.\ value and with the design chosen; both readings carry the cost, so the distance between them is the political response.\footnote{The online appendix holds the table behind each row of the figure, with the savings rate and the workweek, France's own path and all French characteristics imposed together, the same counterfactuals with the UK as the host economy, the design and the tax along the baseline path, the timings of the choice without the cost and the alternative cost specification; appendix \ref{app:robustness} sets the chosen designs beside the other specifications.} By construction the design in force in 2020 is $\theta^{\ast}$ at every $\rho$. Along the projected demographic path the chosen design then drifts toward earnings-related benefits as ageing proceeds, tracking the projected fall in $\nu_t$ and flattening when it does, while the tax rate rises a little above the path with the design held at $\theta^{\ast}$, since a more Bismarckian system dissipates less and is worth more to the electorate at the margin (\oa{esc-path}).\footnote{The design channel runs through hours, so the Frisch elasticity is the parameter to vary. Recalibrating $(\beta,\omega)$ and the cost at $\xi = 0.2$ and $0.4$ moves $\lambda$ by about one percent from its value at $\xi = 0.3$ and leaves the design in force in 2110 unchanged to the third decimal and the design chosen under acute ageing within one unit of it; only the level of the tax moves, by about $0.3$ p.p.\ in either direction by 2110.}

\smalltitle{Ageing.} Acute ageing, $\nu_t = 1$ throughout, raises the chosen design from $0.738$ to $0.825$ at $\rho = 1$, and to $0.816$ and $0.826$ at $\rho = 0.5$ and $2$, so the response varies little with the elasticity, unlike the calibrated cost. Two mechanisms add up. A scarcer young generation raises the political weight of retirees, who are unanimous for a more contributive system since it raises the labor supply on which their benefits are levied (appendix \ref{app:esc:corner}), and the tax that ageing calls for makes the flat component costlier at the margin, since the loss in \eqref{eq:esc:budget} is proportional to $\tau_t$. Under acute ageing the tax rises from $14.4\%$ to $22.8\%$, so the loss per unit of redistribution rises by more than half, and this second channel is what makes the response first order. The tax and savings responses are essentially those of the exogenous-$\theta$ reading: at $\rho = 1$ the chosen design adds $0.3$ p.p.\ to a tax response of $8.1$ p.p., and between $0.1$ and $0.3$ p.p.\ across $\rho$, since a more Bismarckian system dissipates less and is worth a larger tax to the electorate. The pinned response is a little below the $8.7$ p.p.\ of section \ref{sec:oecd}, because with the cost in place a larger system is worth less to retirees at the margin.

\smalltitle{Income distribution.} The model's inequality is the dispersion of relative labor income across the three income groups at the chosen cuts, the $\tilde{V}$ of \eqref{eq:esc:budget}, a different object from the Gini coefficient of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}. Holding $\theta$ at the U.S.\ value, France's flatter income distribution lowers the equilibrium tax at $\rho = 1$ from $14.4\%$ to $13.8\%$, less than the $1.2$ p.p.\ of section \ref{sec:oecd}, because a compressed distribution also lowers $\tilde{V}$ and with it the share of revenue the flat component dissipates. When the electorate chooses the design it moves to $0.772$ at $\rho = 1$, and to $0.707$ and $0.918$ at $\rho = 0.5$ and $2$: interior at every elasticity, and in a direction that depends on it. This happens because the cost is proportional to the same dispersion of relative incomes as the redistribution a flat benefit performs, so compressing the distribution lowers the redistributive stakes and the cost of carrying them together; what remains is a net effect whose sign depends on the elasticity, since the young's forward-looking stake in earnings-related benefits does not scale with the dispersion of incomes and gains ground when the redistributive stakes shrink. The design response leaves the tax where the pinned reading puts it at $\rho \leq 1$ and adds $0.2$ p.p.\ at $\rho = 2$.

\smalltitle{Voting patterns.} France's much flatter voting-weight profile pushes the design toward the Beveridgean corner, and this is the experiment whose strength depends most on the elasticity: the chosen design falls from $0.738$ to $0.653$ at $\rho = 1$, and to $0.687$ and $0.335$ at $\rho = 0.5$ and $2$. When the poor carry more relative voting weight, the redistributive force of proposition \ref{prop:esc:corner} regains ground against the forward-looking stake; at $\rho = 1$ the response is of the same size as that of acute ageing, with the opposite sign. That the design falls furthest at the highest elasticity follows from the calibration: the cost that re-elects the observed design amounts to only $0.4\%$ of revenue at $\rho = 2$ against $3.9\%$ at $\rho = 0.5$ (table \ref{table:US_ESC:calibration}), so at a high elasticity the interior choice rests on a thin cushion and a stronger redistributive pull moves it a long way. In the exogenous reading a flatter voting profile makes the system larger, by $0.8$ p.p.\ at $\rho = 1$; the design response changes that tax by about $0.1$ p.p.\ at $\rho \leq 1$, because the less Bismarckian design chosen also dissipates more revenue, and lifts it from $16.1\%$ to $16.7\%$ at $\rho = 2$, where the design moves a long way and the cost is thin. The design response moves hours by at most a tenth of an hour a week at $\rho \leq 1$, and by about half an hour under French voting patterns at $\rho = 2$. With France's income distribution and voting patterns imposed together the voting patterns prevail at every elasticity: the chosen design is $0.627$ at $\rho = 1$, and $0.667$ and $0.262$ at $\rho = 0.5$ and $2$, with the tax at $14.4\%$ in both readings at $\rho = 1$. The same exercise with the UK as the host economy, at the UK's own cost parameter, moves its design toward flat benefits under France's voting patterns as well, by less, and leaves its tax rate within $0.1$ p.p.\ of the pinned reading (\oa{esc-uk}), so on the UK the design response is confined to the design itself. What the French characteristics do not deliver, on either host, is France's own corner, for the reason section \ref{sec:esc:calibration} gives.

The two forces that figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata} motivates thus come out as the data do, with one qualification. Ageing moves the chosen design a long way in the same direction at every elasticity, and the size of the system with it. Inequality moves the design in a direction that depends on the elasticity, little at $\rho \leq 1$ and far at $\rho = 2$, so the model ties the design to ageing and not, in any stable direction, to inequality, which is the pattern of the cross-section: spending and the Bismarckian index rise together as population growth falls, and neither follows the Gini coefficient.
