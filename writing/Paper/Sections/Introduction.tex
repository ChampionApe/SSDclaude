\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Pensions are the largest transfer programme of most governments. In 2020 public pension spending averaged about 8\% of GDP across the OECD and close to 4\% in emerging and middle-income economies.\footnote{The dispersion is wide even among rich countries: a bit more than 3\% of GDP in Ireland and Chile and more than 17\% in Italy and Greece \parencite{OECDpen}.} Systems differ along two dimensions. The first is coverage: some condition benefits on a contribution record, others pay a pension to everyone. The second is the link between benefits and past earnings: Bismarckian systems tie the two closely, Beveridgean systems pay flat benefits. Both dimensions shape the labor supply of workers, the redistribution the system performs and the coalition of voters that supports it, and neither is fixed by actuarial considerations alone. They are outcomes of a political process in which voters who differ in age and income weigh what they pay against what they receive. This paper asks what that process delivers, in the size of the system and in its design.

Figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata} shows the cross-section for the 29 countries that were in the OECD before 2000.\footnote{The countries are Australia, Austria, Belgium, Canada, Czech Republic, Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Hungary, Iceland, Ireland, Italy, Japan, Korea, Luxembourg, Mexico, Netherlands, New Zealand, Norway, Poland, Portugal, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, Turkey, United Kingdom and United States; all 29 appear in every panel. Pension spending is public cash expenditure on old-age and survivors' pensions in percent of GDP (OECD Social Expenditure Database). Population growth is the ratio of the population in 2020 to that in 1990 (OECD historical population data), with no correction for differences in retirement age, see section \ref{sec:oecd}. The Bismarckian-Beveridgean index is the ratio of the gross replacement rates of mandatory schemes at average and at half average earnings for a man with a full career under the 2020 rules (OECD, \emph{Pensions at a Glance 2021}, table 4.1), see section \ref{sec:oecd}. The Gini coefficient is that of disposable income per capita (equivalised for New Zealand and Turkey), the observation the World Income Inequality Database (UNU-WIDER, version of 8 September 2026) selects for its companion series. All data are for 2020. Across the 29 countries pension spending correlates $-0.58$ with population growth, $0.63$ with the index and $-0.08$ with the Gini; the index correlates $-0.39$ with population growth and $0.18$ with the Gini.} Pension spending and the Bismarckian index, the ratio of replacement rates at mean and at half mean earnings, both rise as population growth falls, and they rise together. Neither is related to income inequality. The last fact runs against the leading theory of pension design, that of \textcite{CondeRuizP07a1}, in which high inequality leads a coalition of the rich and the poor to choose a small Beveridgean system and low inequality leads the middle class to choose a larger Bismarckian one. The question this paper takes up is whether one political process can deliver both the size of the system and its design, and their relation to ageing but not to inequality.

We study the question in an overlapping generations model with capital accumulation and heterogeneous households. Households live for two periods, work when young and consume their savings and a pension when old; they differ in productivity and, as voters, in their propensity to vote. The payroll tax is set each period by probabilistic voting, and voters take into account how the tax moves savings, wages and the policy of the next period; we study Markov equilibria in which only fundamental state variables enter. Preferences are those of \textcite{GreenwoodHH88a1}, so that labor supply depends on the after-tax wage and on how current effort raises future benefits. A share of the young work in an informal sector, where labor is not taxed and savings do not enter the capital stock; these households carry the coverage dimension of pension design and the application to Argentina. Benefits combine a flat component with an earnings-related one, the weight of the second being the Bismarckian index, and informal households receive a universal pension whose level is the second design parameter.

With logarithmic utility and hand-to-mouth informal households the politico-economic equilibrium is in closed form: the equilibrium tax is a function of parameters and demographics alone. Beyond the results of the literature, that taxes rise with the political weight of the old, with ageing and with income inequality, the closed form shows how the design matters. A more Bismarckian benefit formula lowers the equilibrium tax, since it shifts benefits from poor retirees, who value them more at the margin, to rich ones, so that retirees as a group support a smaller system. A more universal pension lowers the tax when few households are informal and raises it once their share exceeds a threshold, since informal households are poorer than formal ones and value the transfer more.

We then take the design as given and calibrate the model. Argentina is the one case in which the design changed: from 2005 a series of tax amnesties raised the coverage of the elderly from 68\% to almost universal, and pension spending by 1.8\% of GDP. Read as an increase in the universal pension, the reform raises the equilibrium tax rate by 1.2 p.p., which is 0.8\% of GDP and almost half of the observed rise, and lowers the savings rate by 0.3 p.p.\ of GDP and the workweek by 0.1 hours, a fall in savings consistent with the evidence of \textcite{Ruffo}. For the U.S., France and the UK we change one characteristic of the U.S.\ calibration at a time, guided by France, and rank the characteristics by their effect on the tax rate. Ageing comes first: a constant population raises the tax rate by 8.7 p.p. The design comes second: moving from earnings-related to flat benefits raises it by 6.2 p.p. France's flatter income distribution lowers the tax rate by 1.2 p.p.\ and France's flatter voting profile raises it by 0.9 p.p.

We then let the electorate choose the earnings link as well as the tax. Without a cost of redistribution the choice is a corner. The flat component of benefits is a pure transfer within the retired cohort, from retirees above mean income to those below, and an electorate that weighs retirees by their marginal utility wants all of it: we prove that when propensities to vote do not rise with income, the design chosen with the tax is fully Beveridgean, whatever the tax rate, the demography and the income distribution. Propensities that rise with income, as in the U.S., can overturn the sign but do not at U.S.\ inequality, and a design chosen one period ahead, or once and for all, lands at a corner as well.

An interior design requires flat benefits to be more expensive than earnings-related ones. We introduce a deadweight cost of the redistribution the flat component performs, read as the tax component of contributions: it is quadratic in the flat share of benefits, proportional to the dispersion of incomes and, per unit of revenue, rising with the size of the system. A single cost parameter is calibrated so that the 2020 U.S.\ electorate re-elects its own design, and it is tested on the UK and France, whose designs it orders as observed. With the cost in place, ageing makes the chosen design more Bismarckian, from $0.738$ to $0.825$ under a constant population, because a larger system makes flat benefits costlier to keep; this is the co-movement of spending and design in figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}. A compressed income distribution lowers the redistributive stakes and the cost of carrying them together and moves the design little, which is the absent relation with inequality in the same figure, while a flatter voting profile pulls the design toward flat benefits.

The results hold under CRRA preferences, read as a range of intertemporal elasticities of substitution, with one exception: a higher elasticity dampens the effect of the earnings link on taxes, from 9.0 p.p.\ at an elasticity of one half to 1.5 p.p.\ at two. The reason for this is that a higher elasticity lowers the young's resistance to taxation, so the calibration needs a smaller political weight of the old to match the observed tax rate, and the difference in benefits between rich and poor retirees counts for less.

The paper belongs to the literature on politico-economic equilibria of social security \parencite[e.g.][]{CooleyS99a1,Galasso99a1,Forni05a1,mkvss08,Song12a1}, surveyed by \textcite{GalassoP02a1}. As in \textcite{mkvss08} and \textcite{Song12a1}, under logarithmic utility the equilibrium has no strategic effects and taxes increase with the political power of retirees, income inequality and ageing; as in \textcite{UchidaOa1}, log-GHH preferences give closed forms, which we show extend to heterogeneous households and to systems that differ in two dimensions; and as in \textcite{mkvss05}, the effect of ageing under CRRA preferences is close to the logarithmic one after recalibration. On the design of the system, \textcite{Casamattaa1} study the political support of Bismarckian against Beveridgean benefits, \textcite{CondeRuizP07a1} the joint choice of size and design with the prediction on inequality that figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata} does not bear out, and \textcite{KoethenbuergerPP08a1} the efficiency--redistribution trade-off by which more redistributive systems are smaller, the relation our cost of redistribution reads from the other side. \textcite{Tabellini00a1} shows that within-cohort redistribution sustains pensions through a coalition of the old and the poor young, the force behind our corner result; \textcite{CremerDMP07a1} let voters choose the type and the generosity of the system and obtain an interior design only when some voters are myopic, in the role our income-rising propensities to vote play; \textcite{GalassoP04a1} find that ageing raises contribution rates and argue that it drives the UK and U.S.\ systems toward earnings-related benefits, the drift our model quantifies; and \textcite{BethencourtG08a1} treat public health care and pensions as political complements, which bears on our reading of Medicare as part of U.S.\ transfers to the old. On informality, \textcite{Levy08a1} argues that contributory social security valued below its cost taxes formal employment while non-contributory programmes subsidise informal work, the margin our Argentine application turns on.
