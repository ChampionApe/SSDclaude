\section{Numerical Methods}\label{sec:numerical}

This section describes how the politico-economic equilibria of sections \ref{sec:argentina}--\ref{sec:esc} are computed, with the algorithms and the code in the technical documentation \parencite{BergGE26doc}. The quantitative politico-economic literature computes a time-invariant policy function \parencite{KrusellRR99a1,Forni05a1,Song12a1} or compares steady states across demographic scenarios \parencite{Galasso99a1}. Along a demographic transition, however, the Markov policy function is date-specific, since the policymaker at $t$ faces its own mix of cohorts, $\nu_t$, and a continuation policy set further along the transition. The distinction is immaterial under log-GHH preferences without informal households (proposition \ref{prop:PEE:unique}), but not with a universal pension, under CRRA preferences or with informal savers, where the continuation policy enters the tax condition, or in the design choice, where a design chosen at $t$ is weighed against the electorate of $t+1$.\footnote{In our applications, the distinction has a modest effect on results: a time-invariant policy function, solved at each date's own $\nu_t$ and applied along the projected path, misses the tax rate by up to about 0.2 p.p.\ before the period that precedes $T$, and by up to 0.3 p.p.\ in the Argentine calibration at the lowest intertemporal elasticity, and misplaces the design by up to 0.074 (\oa{num-stationary}).} We therefore compute date-specific policy functions by backward induction from a date $T$, the end of a finite horizon or the start of a steady state in an infinite one. For a large $T$ the two agree away from $T$. Appendix \ref{app:T} confirms that our results are robust to our choice of $T$, 2200 for the rich economies and 2190 for Argentina.

In the general case with CRRA preferences, the distribution of labor supply remains independent of policy (part ii of proposition \ref{prop:LOG:EE}) and the distribution of past savings is pinned down by the current tax together with past aggregate savings (appendix \ref{app:EE:CRRA}). The distribution of past savings matters for the political problem, but since it depends only on the current tax and past aggregate savings we can eliminate it from the state space, keeping only the level of past savings $s_{t-1}$: we work with the first order condition in $\tau_t$ derived with the distribution of past savings held fixed, and then evaluate it at the one consistent distribution for each candidate tax and for each state $s_{t-1}$. Informal savers add to the state space the ratio $s_{t-1,0}/s_{t-1}$ of informal to formal savings, which also depends on $\tau_{t-1}$ (appendix \ref{app:PEE:LOG_s0}). When the policy function features one or more states, the current tax moves the states and affects future policy choices. We measure the marginal effect by numerical differentiation of the $t+1$ solution grid, which the backward induction has already computed.


\textcite{mkvss08} establish uniqueness in the limit of finite-horizon economies, but their argument does not carry over: their model has a representative household in each cohort, and so no distribution of savings to make consistent with the tax, and neither informal savers nor a choice of design. Under log-GHH preferences, with no informal households, or with hand-to-mouth ones and $\xi(1-2\alpha) \leq 1$, which our calibration satisfies by far, exactly one candidate passes at every date (proposition \ref{prop:PEE:unique} in appendix \ref{app:PEE}). At the distribution of savings consistent with a higher tax, retirees below mean income have saved less, which strengthens their support for the tax, but the income from their savings falls by less than their pension rises, so that the first order condition evaluated at consistent distributions is strictly decreasing in the tax. Under CRRA preferences or with informal savers we check uniqueness numerically, and the solver records at every state and date how many candidates pass.\footnote{The candidates are the corners and every root of the first order condition on a grid of taxes, and a candidate passes if it maximizes the political objective with its own consistent distribution of savings held fixed. Several passing candidates would be ranked by payoff, an assumption about equilibrium selection that none of our reported equilibria requires: exactly one passes at every state and date of every solve behind the paper's results (appendix \ref{app:T}).}

Each reported path starts from the steady state under the first period's policy. Parameters whose targets are equilibrium outcomes, such as the discount factor and the political weight of the old, are calibrated by re-solving the equilibrium path at each trial value until the targets hold in the calibration year.

Under log preferences the choice of the design in section \ref{sec:esc} adds no state either. The design chosen at $t$ applies from $t+1$ and depends neither on the design chosen in the previous period, which sets current benefits, nor on aggregate savings (proposition \ref{prop:esc:separability} in appendix \ref{app:esc:log}), and the tax at each candidate design is found as above, without a state. Each date's choice is therefore a search over a grid of designs. Under CRRA preferences neither property survives: the equilibrium is a backward recursion over policy functions of the state $(s_{t-1},\theta_t)$.
