\begin{abstract}
\noindent


The design of social security systems has implications for the political support of intergenerational transfers. 
We examine the effects of contributive vs.\ universal pensions, as well as the link between benefits and earnings, on the social security tax rate, labor supply, and capital accumulation. In an overlapping generations model featuring heterogeneous households, our results align with existing ones on the effects of retirees' relative political power, ageing, and income inequality. We find taxes to be lower when benefits are earnings-related and higher when pensions are universal. Calibrated to Argentina, the model accounts for about three fifths of the rise in pension spending over the 2005--2010 expansion of coverage. In the model calibrated to the U.S., ageing is the main determinant of pension spending and the earnings link the second, ahead of income inequality and voting patterns by a margin that narrows as the intertemporal elasticity of substitution rises. When the electorate also chooses the design of the system, an interior choice requires redistribution through pensions to be costly. Introducing such a cost, we find that ageing ties benefits closer to earnings, while the effect of income inequality on the design depends on the intertemporal elasticity of substitution. Applied to 26 OECD countries at an elasticity of one, the model has pension spending and the earnings link rise together as population growth falls, as the data do. At the U.S.\ political weight and propensities to vote it also ties the earnings link to inequality, which the data do not; at each country's own propensities it does not.
