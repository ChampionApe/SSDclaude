\section{Numerical Methods}\label{sec:numerical}

This section describes how the politico-economic equilibria of sections \ref{sec:argentina}--\ref{sec:esc} are computed, with the algorithms and the code in the technical documentation \parencite{BergGE26doc}. The quantitative politico-economic literature computes a time-invariant policy function \parencite{KrusellRR99a1,Forni05a1,Song12a1} or compares steady states across demographic scenarios \parencite{Galasso99a1}. Along a demographic transition, however, the Markov policy function is date-specific, since the policymaker at $t$ faces its own mix of cohorts, $\nu_t$, and a continuation policy set further along the transition. The distinction is immaterial in the equilibrium of proposition \ref{prop:LOG:PEE}, which carries no state, but not under CRRA preferences or with informal savers, where the continuation reaches the tax through the state, or in the design choice, where a design chosen at $t$ is weighed against the electorate of $t+1$.\footnote{In our applications, the distinction has a modest effect on results: a time-invariant policy function, solved at each date's own $\nu_t$ and applied along the projected path, misses the tax rate by roughly 0.2 p.p., and by up to 0.3 p.p.\ in the Argentine calibration at the lowest intertemporal elasticity, and misplaces the design by up to 0.074 (\oa{num-stationary}).} We therefore compute date-specific policy functions by backward induction from a date $T$ (appendix \ref{app:T}), the end of a finite horizon or the start of a steady state in an infinite one. For a large $T$ the two agree away from $T$. We set $T = 2200$ (2190 for Argentina): a steady state from $T$ on, or a $T$ two periods earlier or three periods later, moves the tax rates of the calibration year and the period after by at most 0.03 p.p., and the chosen design by less than 0.001 (table \ref{table:T:Horizon}). We report the finite-horizon solution, which approximates the Markov equilibrium in the limit of finite-horizon economies.

In the general case with CRRA preferences, the distribution of labor supply remains independent of policy (part ii of proposition \ref{prop:LOG:EE}) and the distribution of past savings is pinned down by the current tax together with past aggregate savings (appendix \ref{app:EE:CRRA}). The distribution of past savings matters for the political problem, but since it depends only on the current tax and past aggregate savings we can eliminate it from the state space, keeping only the level of past savings $s_{t-1}$: we work with the first order condition in $\tau_t$ derived with the distribution of past savings held fixed, and then replace it with the one consistent distribution for each candidate tax and for each state $s_{t-1}$. Informal savers add the ratio $s_{t-1,0}/s_{t-1}$ of informal to formal savings to the state space, which also depends on $\tau_{t-1}$ (appendix \ref{app:PEE:LOG_s0}).

When the policy function features one or more states, the current tax moves the states and affects future policy choices. We measure the marginal effect by numerical differentiation of the $t+1$ solution grid, which the backward induction has already computed. A first order condition can have several roots, some of them minima, and the optimum can lie at a corner where it has none, so we evaluate the condition on a grid of taxes and test the corners and all its roots. A candidate passes if it maximizes the political objective with its own consistent distribution held fixed. Along the equilibrium path, the largest gap between the computed tax rate and the root of the first order condition with its derivatives in the tax converged is 0.003 p.p.\ at $\rho = 0.5$ and 0.001 p.p.\ at $\rho = 2$ in the U.S.\ calibration, and 0.014 p.p.\ in the Argentine one (table \ref{table:T:Residuals} in appendix \ref{app:T}).

\textcite{mkvss08} establish uniqueness in the limit of finite-horizon economies, but their argument does not carry over: their model has a representative household in each cohort, and so no distribution of savings to make consistent with the tax, and neither informal savers nor a choice of design. Under log-GHH preferences, with no informal households, or with hand-to-mouth ones and $\xi(1-2\alpha) \leq 1$, which our calibration satisfies by far, exactly one candidate passes at every date (proposition \ref{prop:PEE:unique} in appendix \ref{app:PEE}). At the distribution of savings consistent with a higher tax, retirees below mean income have saved less, which strengthens their support for the tax, but the income from their savings falls by less than their pension rises, so that the first order condition evaluated at consistent distributions is strictly decreasing in the tax. Under CRRA preferences or with informal savers we check uniqueness numerically, and the solver records at every state and date how many candidates pass.\footnote{Several passing candidates would be ranked by payoff, an assumption about equilibrium selection that none of our reported equilibria requires: exactly one passes at every state and date of every solve behind our results, except at one state and date in the choice of the design, where none does. Appendix \ref{app:T} describes the exception and reports the counts by solve in table \ref{table:OA:NUM_Selection}, the \oa{num-selection} file by file.}

Each reported path starts from the steady state under the first period's policy. Parameters whose targets are equilibrium outcomes, such as the discount factor and the political weight of the old, are calibrated by re-solving the equilibrium path at each trial value until the targets of sections \ref{sec:argentina} and \ref{sec:oecd} hold in the calibration year.

Under log preferences the choice of the design in section \ref{sec:esc} adds no state either. The design chosen at $t$ applies from $t+1$ and depends neither on the design chosen in the previous period, which sets current benefits, nor on aggregate savings (proposition \ref{prop:esc:separability} in appendix \ref{app:esc:log}), and the tax at each candidate design is found as above, without a state. Each date's choice is therefore a search over a grid of designs. Under CRRA preferences neither property survives: the equilibrium is a backward recursion over policy functions of the state $(s_{t-1},\theta_t)$.
