\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Pensions are the largest transfer program of most governments. In 2020 public pension spending averaged about 8\% of GDP across the OECD and close to 4\% in emerging and middle-income economies.\footnote{The dispersion is wide even among rich countries: a bit more than 3\% of GDP in Ireland and Chile and more than 17\% in Italy and Greece \parencite{OECDpen}.} Systems differ along two key dimensions. First, some systems condition benefits on a contribution record while others offer universal entitlements. Second, Bismarckian systems tie benefits and past earnings closely together, whereas Beveridgean systems pay flat benefits. Both dimensions shape the labor supply of workers, the redistribution embedded in the system, and the coalition of voters that supports it.

Given their size, it is no surprise that the design of social security systems remains a central concern in policy debates. As populations age, policymakers must navigate difficult trade-offs among intergenerational equity, fiscal sustainability, and labor market efficiency. Moreover, pension arrangements are not determined by actuarial considerations alone: they are the outcome of political processes in which heterogeneous voters and organized interests shape the level and composition of benefits and contributions. A political-economy perspective is therefore critical for understanding why countries differ in the size of their systems and in their design. Figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata} shows how pension spending, population ageing, income inequality, and a Bismarckian index, the ratio of replacement rates at mean and at half mean earnings, correlate for 29 OECD countries. It shows that both pension spending and the Bismarckian index rise as population growth falls, and they rise together. Neither shows a strong correlation with income inequality. We build a dynamic politico-economic model and ask whether one political process can deliver both the size of the system and its design, and their relation to ageing and inequality.

We study the question in an overlapping generations model with capital accumulation and heterogeneous households. Households consume, save, and supply labor, and as voters, they choose among policies on labor income taxes and retirement benefits. Policy preferences vary by income and age; we model the resolution of these conflicts through probabilistic voting with elections taking place every period. Policy decisions not only impact economic outcomes, but also affect future policy decisions. Voters take these effects into account. We study Markov equilibria in which only fundamental state variables enter. Households differ in their productivity and propensity to vote. In addition, a share of households work in an informal sector or get income from home production, where labor is not taxed and savings do not enter the capital stock. Preferences are those of \textcite{GreenwoodHH88a1}, so that labor supply depends on the after-tax wage and how current effort raises future benefits. Benefits combine a flat component with an earnings-related one, the weight of the second, $\theta$, setting how Bismarckian benefits are, and informal households receive a universal pension whose level is the second design parameter.

We show that when utility is logarithmic, politico-economic equilibria retain analytical tractability despite the presence of heterogeneity and institutional richness. With hand-to-mouth informal households, the equilibrium tax is a function of parameters and demographics alone. The analytical frame clarifies how population ageing, retirees' political influence, pension design, and income inequality shape the equilibrium tax rate. A more Bismarckian benefit formula lowers the equilibrium tax, since it shifts benefits from poor retirees, who value them more at the margin, to rich ones, so that retirees as a group support a smaller system. The effect of a more universal pension is ambiguous: it raises the tax when informal retirees are poorer than formal ones and the pension is a comparable share of their consumption. When utility is constant relative risk aversion (CRRA), we show that the state space for equilibrium policies can be reduced to a single aggregate state and provide numerical solutions.\footnote{When informal households can save, the state space includes two states.}

We present two empirical applications of the model. First, we study Argentina as the case in which the design changed: from 2005 a series of tax amnesties raised the coverage of the elderly from 68\% to over 90\%, and pension spending rose by 1.9\% of GDP by 2010. Implementing this reform in our calibrated model accounts for about three fifths of that rise. The model also predicts modest declines in labor supply and savings, and a fall in the savings of the newly covered households relative to other households, consistent with what \textcite{Ruffo} estimate for the amnesties. Second, initially taking the design as given, we calibrate the model to the U.S.\ and explore how income distribution, voting profile, pension design, ageing, and leisure preferences shape the politico-economic equilibrium. We find that the size of the pension system increases strongly with ageing and with flat benefits and responds much less to other characteristics. We then construct a set of 26 synthetic countries based on our calibrated U.S.\ model, but with observed design, demography and income distribution representing the OECD figures in figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}.\footnote{We apply the model to 26 of the 29 countries and only for logarithmic utility, see section \ref{sec:oecd}.} This predicts pension spending that falls with population growth about as steeply as in the data and, like spending in the data, is unrelated to income inequality. 


We then let the electorate choose the earnings link as well as the tax. Without a cost of redistribution the choice is a corner. The flat component of benefits is a pure transfer within the retired cohort, from retirees above mean income to those below, and an electorate that weighs retirees by their marginal utility wants all of it: we prove that unless propensities to vote rise sharply with income, the design chosen with the tax is fully Beveridgean, whatever the tax rate, the demography and the income distribution.\footnote{We also test alternative timings, letting the electorate choose the design one period ahead of the benefits it governs or letting it choose the design permanently in one period, an exercise outside the Markov equilibria we study. At the U.S.\ calibration, we always find a corner solution (\oa{esc-timing}).} An interior design requires flat benefits to be more expensive than earnings-related ones. We introduce a deadweight cost of the redistribution the flat component performs, read as the loss from the implicit tax it levies on earnings \parencite{Summers89a1} and rising with the square of that tax as in \textcite{Harberger64a1}. A single cost parameter is calibrated so that the U.S.\ electorate re-elects its observed design. With this formulation, the model predicts that an ageing population chooses both a more Bismarckian design and higher pension spending, as in figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}, whereas the income distribution moves spending little and moves the design in a direction that depends on the intertemporal elasticity of substitution. Taken to the same 26 countries with the U.S.\ cost parameter as well, the model reproduces the signs of the relations among pension spending, the index and population growth, with a weaker response of the index to population growth than in the data. At the U.S.\ political weight and propensities to vote, more unequal countries choose less Bismarckian designs, a relation the data do not show; at each country's own propensities, the design is unrelated to inequality, as in the data.

The paper belongs to the literature on politico-economic equilibria of social security \parencite[e.g.][]{CooleyS99a1,Galasso99a1,Forni05a1,mkvss08,Song12a1}, surveyed by \textcite{GalassoP02a1}. As in \textcite{mkvss08} and \textcite{Song12a1}, in our model under log-GHH preferences and with hand-to-mouth informal households the equilibrium has no strategic effects and taxes increase with the political power of retirees and with ageing, and as in \textcite{Song12a1} they increase with income inequality when propensities to vote are equal; as in \textcite{UchidaOa1}, the equilibrium tax is a function of parameters and demographics alone; and as in \textcite{mkvss05}, the effect of ageing under CRRA preferences is close to the logarithmic one after recalibration. What we add is a pension system that differs in two dimensions, in coverage and in the earnings link, and workers who differ in productivity and in their propensity to vote. Neither adds a state when informal households are hand-to-mouth, and the calibrated model quantifies what each feature of the design does to the tax rate, labor supply and savings.

On the design of the system, \textcite{GalassoP02a1} leave open why some systems are Beveridgean and others Bismarckian, and why the Bismarckian ones are larger, two questions that a vote on the size of the system alone cannot answer. \textcite{Casamattaa1} study the political support of Bismarckian against Beveridgean benefits, \textcite{CondeRuizP07a1} the joint choice of size and design, \textcite{KoethenbuergerPP08a1} the trade-off by which more redistributive systems are smaller, and \textcite{CremerDMP07a1} the choice of type and generosity. In \textcite{CondeRuizP07a1} majority voting ties the design to inequality: high inequality leads to a small Beveridgean system and low inequality to a larger Bismarckian one. We embed the choice in a dynamic equilibrium with capital accumulation and labor supply, in which the design is chosen every period, one period ahead of the benefits it governs, and take it to the data. Across the countries of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata} the design is unrelated to inequality, and under the probabilistic voting of our model whether it is related depends on the propensities to vote.
