\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclu}

This paper studies how two features of a pension system, whether benefits require a contribution record and how closely they follow past earnings, shape its political support and through it taxes, savings and labor supply. The setting is an overlapping generations model with capital, heterogeneous households, an informal sector and probabilistic voting, first over the size of the system and then over its design as well.

With log-GHH preferences and hand-to-mouth informal households the equilibrium tax is a function of parameters and demographics alone. It rises with the political weight of the old, with ageing and with income inequality, and it falls when benefits are more closely tied to earnings, since earnings-related benefits move revenue from poor retirees to rich ones; universal pensions raise the tax when informal retirees are poorer than formal ones and the pension is a comparable share of their consumption. Calibrated to Argentina, the model accounts for about three fifths of the rise in pension spending that accompanied the expansion of coverage of 2005 to 2010, with the savings rate lower by 0.4 p.p.\ of GDP and the workweek by a quarter of an hour. In the calibrations to the U.S., France and the UK, acute ageing, which holds the population constant, is the main determinant of the U.S.\ tax rate and the earnings link the second, ahead of income inequality and voting patterns, at every intertemporal elasticity of substitution we consider. A higher elasticity shrinks the effects of ageing, the earnings link and the income distribution and enlarges that of the voting profile, so that at an elasticity of two the earnings link, at 2.0 p.p., is barely ahead of the voting profile, at 1.8 p.p.

When the electorate chooses the earnings link as well, costless redistribution puts the choice at a corner: with propensities to vote that do not rise with income the design chosen with the tax is fully Beveridgean, and at U.S.\ inequality each of the three timings we examine gives a corner at every intertemporal elasticity of substitution we check, the Beveridgean one in the Markov equilibria we study. An interior design needs flat benefits to be costlier than earnings-related ones, and we read the flat component as an implicit tax whose deadweight loss rises with the square of its rate, so that the share of revenue lost grows with the size of the system. With this cost calibrated on the U.S.\ design alone, ageing makes the chosen design more Bismarckian at every intertemporal elasticity of substitution, France's flatter voting profile makes it less so, away from France's own fully Bismarckian system, and a more equal income distribution moves it in a direction that depends on that elasticity. The U.S.\ cost parameter accounts for about a quarter of the distance of the UK and of France from the U.S.\ design, and the UK's own parameter is a third below the U.S.\ value at elasticities of one or below and a sixth above it at two. In the OECD cross-section, spending and the earnings link rise together as population growth falls, and neither follows inequality. Taken to 26 OECD countries with the U.S.\ parameters at an elasticity of one, the model reproduces the first pattern in sign, though the earnings link responds less to population growth than in the data, and it leaves spending unrelated to inequality. Unlike the data, however, it ties more Beveridgean designs to higher inequality.

Three limitations point to future work. The model has no risk; in \textcite{BergGEwp} we study a related environment in which health risk may prevent households from qualifying for a contributive pension, and find that households work and save more in response. Every quantitative result rests on GHH preferences, which rule out income effects on labor supply; the hours responses and the retirees' stake in an earnings-related design both work through labor supply and may change once such effects are allowed. And the cost specification cannot by itself deliver France's fully Bismarckian system, because under a cost quadratic in the redistribution performed the first unit of redistribution is free at the margin; a linear term in the cost, or a formality margin, toward which the informal sector of our Argentine model is a first step, would allow it. The same margin bears on the rise of platform work and other arrangements outside contributive coverage in rich economies, which the model can address.
