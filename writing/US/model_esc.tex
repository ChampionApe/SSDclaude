\subsection{Endogenous system characteristics}\label{\refsec:ESC}
Everything up to this point treats the pension design $\theta_t$ as a parameter, calibrated from the observed dispersion of replacement rates. This section makes it a political outcome. Two ingredients are needed, and they are logically independent: a \emph{cost} that keeps the choice off the boundary, and a \emph{timing} convention that says when the design is chosen and what it is expected to bind for.

The cost matters because without one the choice is degenerate. Under any of the timings below, $\theta_t$ redistributes a fixed pot among retirees whose marginal utilities differ, and a utilitarian aggregator with equal weights always prefers the flat system: the political objective is monotone and the equilibrium is the corner $\theta_t = 0$. This is not a numerical accident but a property of the objective, and it survives every timing convention we have examined.


\smalltitle{The deadweight cost of redistributive benefits.}
The flat component of a pay-as-you-go benefit taxes some contributors and subsidises others, and we make it costly for exactly that reason, in a reduced form that leaves the structure of the model intact. Write the benefit formula as
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:esc:benefits}
    b_t^i = \left[A(\theta_t,\tau_t)h_{t-1,i}\eta_{t-1,i} + B(\theta_t,\tau_t)h_{t-1}\right]\overline{b}_t,
\end{align}
with $\overline{b}_t$ still given by \eqref{\refeq:governmentBudget:bbar}. The baseline model is $\left(A,B\right) = \left(\theta,1-\theta\right)$, where the two coefficients sum to one and \eqref{\refeq:esc:benefits} reduces to the formula in \eqref{\refeq:governmentBudget}. With a cost they sum to less than one, and the form of the shortfall is derived rather than assumed.

\emph{The implicit tax.} Let $y_{t,i}\equiv y_{t,i}^{\eta}/\Gamma_{h,t}$ denote the labour income of type $i$ relative to the mean --- the $z_i^{\eta}$ of \eqref{\refeq:calibration:zeta} at vintage $t$ --- so that $\eta_{t,i}h_{t,i}/h_t = y_{t,i}$ by \eqref{\refeq:EE:hi} and $\sum_i\gamma_{t,i}y_{t,i}=1$. A retiree of type $i$ at $t$ draws $\left[\theta_t y_{t-1,i}+1-\theta_t\right]h_{t-1}\overline{b}_t$ on past earnings $w_{t-1}y_{t-1,i}h_{t-1}$; in expectation at the time of contributing, this is $\Pi_t\tau_t\left[\theta_t + (1-\theta_t)/y_{t-1,i}\right]$ per unit of those earnings, where $\Pi_t\equiv p_{t-1}\overline{b}_t/(\tau_tw_{t-1}) = \nu_tw_th_t/(w_{t-1}h_{t-1})$ by \eqref{\refeq:governmentBudget:bbar} is the pay-as-you-go return factor: the benefit per unit of earnings that a purely earnings-related system pays out of the same revenue. The flat component moves type $i$ away from that benchmark by
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:esc:implicitTax}
    t_{t,i} \equiv \Pi_t\,\tau_t\,(1-\theta_t)\left(1-\frac{1}{y_{t-1,i}}\right)
\end{align}
per unit of earnings: a tax on types above mean income, a subsidy to types below it, and a pure transfer in the aggregate, since $\sum_i\gamma_{t-1,i}y_{t-1,i}t_{t,i}=0$. This is the \emph{tax component} of the contribution in the sense of Summers (1989): the part of the payroll charge that buys the contributor no benefit, and the only part that distorts like a tax.

\emph{Which margin.} The wedge in \eqref{\refeq:esc:implicitTax} is an \emph{average} implicit tax: it compares what type $i$ receives per unit of its earnings with the benchmark. The marginal implicit tax is a different object. The flat component depends on aggregate and not on own hours, so an extra hour raises the benefit of every type by $\Pi_t\tau_t\theta_t$ per unit of earnings, and the marginal wedge of the flat component, $\Pi_t\tau_t(1-\theta_t)$, is common to all types. That wedge is already in the model: it is the group $\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}A\tau_{t+1}\Gamma_{s,t}$ in $\Theta_{h,t}$, through which hours respond to the design with the Frisch elasticity $\xi$, and GHH preferences carry no income effect through which the inframarginal transfers between types could move hours. A cost built on the hours margin would count that response twice and could not depend on the spread of incomes. The cost therefore stands for margins the model does not contain, on which the average wedge is the relevant one: participation, formality and the reporting of earnings. On a participation margin a worker compares being in and out of the system, so the wedge that matters is the participation tax, the average tax on the earnings at stake (Saez, 2002; Kleven and Kreiner, 2006). The evidence finds the tax component to matter on these margins. Disney (2004) splits pension contributions in OECD countries into a tax and a saving component and finds that the tax component lowers the activity rates of women, while the saving component raises them. Kumler, Verhoogen and Fr\'{\i}as (2020) find that under-reporting of wages to Mexico's social security agency fell for the workers whose benefits the 1997 reform tied more closely to reported wages. Bergolo and Cruces (2014) find that a Uruguayan benefit conditional on registered employment, but not on the earnings reported, raised registered employment and the under-reporting of earnings together.

\emph{The Harberger loss.} A set of proportional wedges $t_i$ on bases $e_i$, each base responding to its net-of-tax rate with a common elasticity $\varepsilon$, costs $\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\sum_i\gamma_it_i^2e_i$ to second order in the wedges (Harberger, 1964); on a participation or reporting margin $e_i$ is the earnings a type brings into the system and $\varepsilon$ the elasticity of that base. Per unit of mean past earnings the bases are $e_i = y_{t-1,i}$, so the loss is $\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\,\Pi_t^2\tau_t^2(1-\theta_t)^2\tilde{V}_{t-1}$ with
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:esc:Vtilde}
    \tilde{V}_{t} \equiv \sum_i\gamma_{t,i}\frac{\left(y_{t,i}-1\right)^2}{y_{t,i}},
\end{align}
while revenue per unit of mean past earnings is $\Pi_t\tau_t$. As a share of revenue the loss is therefore $\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\,\Pi_t\,\tau_t\,(1-\theta_t)^2\,\tilde{V}_{t-1}$: quadratic in the share of the system that is flat, linear in its size, and proportional to a Harberger-weighted dispersion of relative income that is zero when all incomes are equal and, being a function of $y^{\eta}/\Gamma_h$ alone, invariant to the normalisations of \eqref{\refeq:scaleInvariance} and \eqref{\refeq:hoursUnit}. The same tax component is the mechanism by which Koethenbuerger, Poutvaara and Profeta (2008) account for the stylised fact of Conde-Ruiz and Profeta (2007) that more redistributive systems are smaller; here it is read from the other side, as what makes a larger system costlier to keep redistributive. The quadratic counts the subsidy to types below the mean as a loss, as it does the tax on types above it. Where participation is already taxed, lowering the participation tax of low-income workers can reduce the total loss (Saez, 2002), so the symmetry belongs to the reduced form and not to the argument.

\emph{Specification.} The cost has the two parts of the leaky bucket of Okun (1975): a constant share of every unit the flat component carries, and a share that rises with the load, which is the Harberger loss above. The first needs the amount carried. The implicit tax \eqref{\refeq:esc:implicitTax} moves $y_{t-1,i}\left|t_{t,i}\right| = \Pi_t\tau_t(1-\theta_t)\left|y_{t-1,i}-1\right|$ per unit of mean past earnings to or from a retiree of type $i$, and since what is taken above the mean is what arrives below it, the sum over types counts every unit twice: the flat component carries
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:esc:Mabs}
    \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_i\gamma_{t-1,i}\,y_{t-1,i}\left|t_{t,i}\right| = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Pi_t\,\tau_t\,(1-\theta_t)\,M_{t-1}, \qquad M_{t} \equiv \sum_i\gamma_{t,i}\left|y_{t,i}-1\right|,
\end{align}
with $M_t$ the mean absolute deviation of relative income, so that a leak losing a constant share of every unit carried is a share of revenue proportional to $(1-\theta_t)M_{t-1}$. We adopt the exponential form
\begin{subequations}\label{\refeq:esc:AB}
\begin{align}
    \left(A,B\right) &= \left(f(\theta_t,\tau_t)\,\theta_t,\; f(\theta_t,\tau_t)\,(1-\theta_t)\right), \\
    f(\theta_t,\tau_t) &\equiv \exp\left\{-\tau_t\,(1-\theta_t)\left[\lambda_1M_{t-1} + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\lambda_2\,\tilde{V}_{t-1}\,(1-\theta_t)\right]\right\}, \label{\refeq:esc:fGeneral}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
so that the whole system carries the cost, the surviving share is $A+B=f\in(0,1]$, and $1-f$ is the share of revenue that does not arrive. To first order $1-f = \lambda_1\tau_tM_{t-1}(1-\theta_t) + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda_2\tau_t\tilde{V}_{t-1}(1-\theta_t)^2$: the second term is the Harberger share, with $\lambda_2$ in the place of $\varepsilon\Pi_t$,\footnote{The return factor multiplies every implicit tax in \eqref{\refeq:esc:implicitTax} and so scales the Harberger share once, while it cancels from the linear leak, whose amount and revenue both carry it. Since it carries $\nu_t$, it would make a structural $\lambda_2$ drift with demographics; we absorb it into $\lambda_2$, which is calibrated at the baseline date rather than derived. The parameters $\lambda$, $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ of this section are the cost parameters of the paper and are unrelated to the rescaling factor that \eqref{\refeq:scaleInvariance} happens to call by the same letter.} and the first loses a share $2\lambda_1\tau_t$ of every unit carried, by \eqref{\refeq:esc:Mabs}. Each term carries the dispersion of its own derivation: a leak proportional to the amount carried counts every unit alike and is weighted by the amount, $M$; a loss quadratic in each type's wedge counts a unit by the wedge it travels under, $\left|y_i-1\right|/y_i$, so that a given transfer counts for more against a smaller income, and is weighted by $\tilde{V}$. The paper's three variants write the parameters as a scale and a shape, $\lambda_1 = \lambda\kappa$ and $\lambda_2 = \lambda$: the quadratic cost is $\kappa = 0$ (code spec \texttt{'size'}), the linear cost is $\lambda_2 = 0$ with $\lambda_1 = \lambda$ (\texttt{'sizeL'}), and the linear-quadratic cost, the paper's preferred one, has both (\texttt{'sizeLQ'}, with $\kappa$ in \texttt{db['wedgeKappa']}). A fourth spec, \texttt{'sizeLQV'}, puts $\tilde{V}$ on the linear term as well, $\ln f = -\lambda\tau_t\tilde{V}_{t-1}(1-\theta_t)\left[\kappa + \tfrac{1}{2}(1-\theta_t)\right]$, and is kept for comparison; the two weights differ across economies through $M/\tilde{V}$ alone, $1.32$ for the U.S., $1.31$ for the UK and $1.93$ for France in the common-$X$ calibration. The exponential keeps $f$ positive for every parameter and every size of system and nests the baseline at $\lambda=0$. Every variant has five properties. Nothing is lost when nothing is redistributed: $f=1$ at $\theta_t=1$, and $f=1$ for every design when every relative income is one, where $M_{t-1}$ and $\tilde{V}_{t-1}$ vanish together and the two designs pay identical benefits. The loss per unit of redistribution rises with the size of the system, through $\tau_t$. The cost is \emph{proportional}: it scales both components alike and cancels from every ratio of benefits. Its logarithm is linear in $\tau_t$, so $\tau_t\,\partial_{\tau}\ln f = \ln f$ and the factor that the cost adds to the tax condition in \eqref{\refeq:esc:dlnc2i} is $1+\ln f$ whatever the variant. And the marginal cost of the first unit of redistribution is $\partial_{\theta}\ln f\,\big|_{\theta_t=1} = \lambda_1\tau_tM_{t-1}$: zero under the quadratic cost, which therefore leaves the slope of the political objective at the Bismarckian design as it is without a cost, and positive under the two variants with a linear term, which is what lets an electorate choose the corner $\theta_t=1$. The factor $\tau_t$ on the linear term is a choice, since by \eqref{\refeq:esc:Mabs} a constant share of the amount carried is, as a share of revenue, independent of the size of the system. Under that literal leak, $1-f \approx \lambda_1(1-\theta_t)M_{t-1} + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda_2\tau_t\tilde{V}_{t-1}(1-\theta_t)^2$, the value of $\lambda_1$ at which an electorate chooses the corner is independent of the size of the system to first order, and only the quadratic part dissipates at the margin of the tax, so that the factor in \eqref{\refeq:esc:dlnc2i} becomes $1+\ln f+\lambda_1(1-\theta_t)M_{t-1}$. With the factor $\tau_t$ that value is proportional to $1/\tau_{t+1}$, which lets the size of the system separate France from the UK at nearly the same $\tilde{V}$, and one factor $1+\ln f$ serves every variant. The government's budget is unchanged in form: it collects $\nu_t w_t \tau_t h_t$ and delivers $\left(A+B\right)h_{t-1}\overline{b}_tp_{t-1}$ per contributor, so $\overline{b}_t$ should be read as \emph{gross} revenue per unit of past labour and the fraction $1-f$ as the resources that do not arrive.

\emph{Dates.} $f$ takes the design and the tax of the date whose benefits it scales: $(\theta_t,\tau_t)$ in \eqref{\refeq:esc:benefits} and in the consumption of current retirees; $(\theta_{t+1},\tau_{t+1})$ wherever the young anticipate their own benefits --- in $\Gamma_{s,t}$, $\Theta_{h,t}$, $s_{t,i}/s_t$ and the bracket of $\tilde{c}_{1,t}^i$ --- with $\tau_{t+1} = \mathcal{T}_{t+1}(\theta_{t+1})$ the continuation tax, so that the young at $t$ anticipate both the design and the size of the system that will pay them, which is the Harberger reading. $\tilde{V}$ and $M$ carry the vintage of the cohort whose benefits the bracket prices, $\tilde{V}_{t-1}$ and $M_{t-1}$ in the benefit formula and $\tilde{V}_t$ and $M_t$ in the terms of the young; with a time-invariant productivity distribution the two vintages coincide, and they are kept apart so that a time-varying one would not silently mix them.

Two earlier reduced forms, $f(\theta) = \phi + (1-\phi)\theta^{p}$ applied to both components or to the flat one alone, attached the cost to the design rather than to the transfer the design performs, and so burned a share $1-\phi$ of revenue at $\theta=0$ even when all incomes were equal; the proportional one is kept as a comparison arm in \cref{\refnumsec:ESC}. What the present form keeps from them is the position on magnitude. The shape of the quadratic term of \eqref{\refeq:esc:fGeneral} follows the deadweight-loss logic of the tax component; its size does not. The natural benchmarks are participation and taxable-income elasticities: Chetty, Guren, Manoli and Weber (2011) propose a steady-state elasticity of about $0.25$ on the extensive margin, and Saez, Slemrod and Giertz (2012) put the best available estimates of the elasticity of taxable income between $0.12$ and $0.40$. With $\Pi_t$ of the order of $\nu_t$, $1.34$ in the U.S.\ calibration for 2020, elasticities of $0.1$ to $0.4$ correspond to $\lambda_2$ between about $0.13$ and $0.54$. The calibrated quadratic cost (\cref{\refnumsec:ESC:calibration}), $\lambda = 18.24$, $8.64$ and $1.73$ at $\rho = 0.5$, $1$ and $2$, is $34$ to $140$, $16$ to $64$ and $3$ to $13$ times that range, the linear-quadratic cost's $\lambda_2 = 5.24$ at $\rho = 1$ about $10$ to $40$ times, and $\lambda_2$ stands in as well for administrative, evasion and political costs of redistribution that the model does not contain. The linear parameter has no elasticity benchmark: it sets the share $2\lambda_1\tau_t$ of every unit carried that is lost whatever the wedge it travels under, the leak that administrative and political costs absorb, and a second economy's design pins it (\cref{\refnumsec:ESC:calibration}). A deadweight loss well above what hours elasticities imply is not without precedent: once avoidance is counted through the elasticity of taxable income, Feldstein (1999) puts the loss per dollar of income-tax revenue at more than twelve times Harberger's hours-based estimate. We do not take a stand on their microfoundation, and the reduced form is compatible with several: administrative cost that scales with the redistributive component, an avoidance or informality margin that responds to the part of the contribution that is a pure tax rather than deferred pay, or imperfect targeting of a means-tested benefit. The paper's own Argentine model contains a formality margin of exactly this kind, which is the closest structural analogue available to us.


\smalltitle{What the cost changes, and what it does not.}
Very little of the model needs rewriting, for a reason worth stating explicitly.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:esc:substitution}
In every equation of the economic equilibrium, the design enters only through the two groups $\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\tau_{t+1}\theta_{t+1}$ and $\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\tau_{t+1}(1-\theta_{t+1})$ (and their date-$t$ counterparts in the benefit formula). The cost is therefore \emph{exactly} the substitution $\theta_{t+1} \mapsto A(\theta_{t+1},\tau_{t+1})$ and $(1-\theta_{t+1})\mapsto B(\theta_{t+1},\tau_{t+1})$ in those groups, and $\theta_t \mapsto A(\theta_t,\tau_t)$, $(1-\theta_t)\mapsto B(\theta_t,\tau_t)$ in the benefit formula, and changes nothing else in the economic equilibrium. In the political equilibrium it adds one factor: because $A$ and $B$ depend on the tax that funds them, the term of the retired in the tax first order condition picks up $\partial_{\tau}\ln f$.
\end{proposition}

The claim can be read off \eqref{\refnumeq:auxiliary}: $\theta_{t+1}$ appears in $\Gamma_{s,t}$, $\Theta_{h,t}$ and $s_{t,i}/s_t$ and nowhere else, and always in one of the two groups. Applying the substitution,
\begin{subequations}\label{\refeq:esc:auxiliary}
\begin{align}
    \Gamma_{s,t} &= \frac{1}{1+\xi}\frac{\sum_i \gamma_{t,i}y_{t,i}^{\eta}\frac{B_{t+1}^i}{1+B_{t+1}^i}}{1+\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\tau_{t+1}\left(A(\theta_{t+1},\tau_{t+1})+B(\theta_{t+1},\tau_{t+1})\sum_i\frac{\gamma_{t,i}}{1+B_{t+1}^i}\right)},
    \label{\refeq:esc:auxiliary:Gammas} \\
    \Theta_{h,t} &= \left(\Gamma_{h,t}\right)^{\frac{1+\xi}{1+\alpha\xi}}\left(\frac{(1-\alpha)(1-\tau_t)}{\Gamma_{h,t}-\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}A(\theta_{t+1},\tau_{t+1})\tau_{t+1}\Gamma_{s,t}}\right)^{\frac{\xi}{1+\alpha\xi}},
    \label{\refeq:esc:auxiliary:Thetah}
\end{align}
and, in the log case where $B_{t+1}^i = \beta$ is common,
\begin{align}
    \frac{s_{t,i}}{s_t} &= \frac{y_{t,i}^{\eta}}{\Gamma_{h,t}} + \frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\frac{B(\theta_{t+1},\tau_{t+1})\tau_{t+1}}{1+\beta}\left(\frac{y_{t,i}^{\eta}}{\Gamma_{h,t}}-1\right).
    \label{\refeq:esc:auxiliary:si}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Second-period consumption picks up the substitution at date $t$ rather than $t+1$: the bracket $1+\theta_t\left(y_{t-1,i}-1\right)$ appearing in \eqref{\refeq:EE:ci} becomes $A(\theta_t,\tau_t)\,y_{t-1,i} + B(\theta_t,\tau_t)$. The two coincide when $A+B=1$, so the baseline is nested exactly and not merely as a limit. The one place where the cost does more than substitute is the term of the retired in the tax first order condition, \eqref{\refeq:PEELOG:v2i} and its CRRA counterpart in \eqref{\refeq:PEE}: the bracket there is multiplied by $\tau_t$, and $f$ now moves with $\tau_t$, so
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:esc:dlnc2i}
    \frac{d\ln\left(c_{2,t}^i\right)}{d\tau_t} = (1-\alpha)\frac{d\ln\left(h_t\right)}{d\tau_t} + \frac{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\left[A(\theta_t,\tau_t)\,y_{t-1,i}+B(\theta_t,\tau_t)\right]\left(1+\tau_t\,\partial_{\tau}\ln f(\theta_t,\tau_t)\right)}{\frac{s_{t-1,i}}{s_{t-1}}+\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\tau_t\left[A(\theta_t,\tau_t)\,y_{t-1,i}+B(\theta_t,\tau_t)\right]},
\end{align}
where $\partial_{\tau}\ln f(\theta_t,\tau_t) = -(1-\theta_t)\left[\lambda_1M_{t-1} + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda_2\tilde{V}_{t-1}(1-\theta_t)\right] = \ln f(\theta_t,\tau_t)/\tau_t$ by \eqref{\refeq:esc:fGeneral}, so that $1+\tau_t\partial_{\tau}\ln f = 1+\ln f(\theta_t,\tau_t)$ because $\ln f$ is linear in $\tau_t$: a larger system is worth less to a retiree at the margin than the pot it collects, by the share of the marginal contribution that the flat component dissipates. Nothing else in the tax first order condition sees $f$ under log preferences --- the term of the young, \eqref{\refeq:PEELOG:v1i}, contains no policy but $\tau_t$, and the labour-supply derivative \eqref{\refeq:PEELOG:dThetah} is free of the groups --- and under CRRA the numerical derivatives of the utilities of the young see $f(\theta_{t+1},\tau_{t+1})$ only through the continuation, as they already see $\theta_{t+1}$ and $\tau_{t+1}$.

A consequence of \cref{prop:esc:substitution} that matters for the propositions below: the cost does \emph{not} disturb the decoupling result of \eqref{\refeq:PEELOG:decoupling}. The first order condition for $\tau_t$ still involves $\theta_{t+1}$ only through the level of $\Theta_{h,t}$, which reaches it only through the old informal household --- absent here --- and the new factor in \eqref{\refeq:esc:dlnc2i} is dated $t$, so $z_t$ remains a function of $\left(\tau_t,\theta_t\right)$ alone.


\smalltitle{Identification of $\theta$ is not innocent.}
The replacement-rate ratio that identifies $\theta$ in \eqref{\refeq:calibration:theta} pins the \emph{ratio} of the two coefficients in \eqref{\refeq:esc:benefits} and nothing else:
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:esc:RR}
    \frac{B(\theta,\tau)}{A(\theta,\tau)} = \frac{1-RR_0}{RR_0\,h_1 - h_2}, \qquad h_j \equiv \frac{\Gamma_{h,t_0}X_{t_0,j}^{\xi}}{\eta_{t_0,j}^{1+\xi}},
\end{align}
where $j=1,2$ index the half-mean and mean-income groups. Under the proportional specification \eqref{\refeq:esc:AB} the common factor $f(\theta,\tau)$ cancels from \eqref{\refeq:esc:RR}, so $\theta$ is the same number as in the exogenous-design model whatever the cost parameter --- $0.738$ for the United States --- and the design and the cost are identified separately. This is a property of the specification and not of the exercise: a cost attached to the flat component alone, $(A,B) = (\theta,\, f\,(1-\theta))$, leaves $B/A = (1-\theta)f/\theta$, so that the same datum implies a different $\theta$ for every value of the cost parameter and the two are jointly identified. The object being matched is $RR_0$, not any particular value of $\theta$.


\subsubsection{Three timings}\label{\refsec:ESC:timing}
Let $\mathcal{W}_t$ denote the political objective of \cref{\refsec:PEELOG}, evaluated with the same weights that determine $\tau_t$. We consider three conventions for when the design is chosen. Throughout, $s_{t-1,i}/s_{t-1}$ is \emph{predetermined} at $t$: the electorate takes the inherited distribution of savings as given, exactly as it does when choosing $\tau_t$.

\smalltitle{Sequential.} $\theta_t$ is chosen at $t$ alongside $\tau_t$, and binds for that period only. Because benefits at $t$ are paid on labour supplied at $t-1$, and because current labour supply responds to $\theta_{t+1}$ rather than $\theta_t$, the design has no effect on any date-$t$ quantity other than the split of a fixed pot among retirees. Writing $A'$ and $B'$ for the partial derivatives of $A(\theta_t,\tau_t)$ and $B(\theta_t,\tau_t)$ with respect to $\theta_t$ at the given $\tau_t$, the marginal effect is therefore
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:esc:seqFOC}
    \dfrac{\partial \mathcal{W}_t}{\partial \theta_t} = \omega\sum_i\gamma_{t-1,i}p_{t-1}\mu_{t-1,i}\,
    \frac{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\tau_t\left[A'(\theta_t,\tau_t)\frac{y_{t-1,i}^{\eta}}{\Gamma_{h,t-1}} + B'(\theta_t,\tau_t)\right]}
         {\frac{s_{t-1,i}}{s_{t-1}}+\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\tau_t\left[A(\theta_t,\tau_t)\frac{y_{t-1,i}^{\eta}}{\Gamma_{h,t-1}}+B(\theta_t,\tau_t)\right]},
\end{align}
with, from \eqref{\refeq:esc:AB}, $A' = f + \theta_t f_{\theta}$ and $B' = (1-\theta_t)f_{\theta} - f$, where $f_{\theta} = f\,\tau_t\left[\lambda_1M_{t-1} + \lambda_2\tilde{V}_{t-1}(1-\theta_t)\right]$ by \eqref{\refeq:esc:fGeneral}. Without a cost, $A' = 1$ and $B' = -1$, the numerator is proportional to $y_{t-1,i}^{\eta}/\Gamma_{h,t-1}-1$, which averages to zero across types under the population weights alone, while the denominator is increasing in the same object. With the voting weights $\mu_{t-1,i}$ in the sum the average is not zero (they rise with income in the US calibration), so the sign is established numerically rather than by the argument above: evaluated on the solved baseline path, the sum is negative for every $\theta_t\in[0,1]$ at every dated period, under log preferences and at $\rho = 0.5$ and $2$ (\cref{\refnumsec:ESC}), and the choice is the corner $\theta_t=0$: lowering $\theta$ moves resources from retirees with low marginal utility to retirees with high marginal utility, and nothing offsets it. With a cost, $A'+B' = f_{\theta}\geq 0$, positive away from $\theta_t=1$ and equal to $\lambda_1\tau_tM_{t-1}$ there, which is zero under the quadratic cost, and the sign is no longer determined by the distribution alone.

\smalltitle{Leaded.} $\theta_{t+1}$ is chosen at $t$ and becomes a state at $t+1$. The design now reaches current labour supply, through $A(\theta_{t+1},\tau_{t+1})$ and $B(\theta_{t+1},\tau_{t+1})$ in \eqref{\refeq:esc:auxiliary}, and through it every date-$t$ quantity, but it no longer touches the benefits of current retirees:
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:esc:leadFOC}
    \dfrac{d\upsilon_{2,t}^i}{d\theta_{t+1}} &= (1-\alpha)\dfrac{d\ln\left(\Theta_{h,t}\right)}{d\theta_{t+1}}, &
    \dfrac{d\upsilon_{1,t}^i}{d\theta_{t+1}} &= (1+\beta_{t,i})\dfrac{d\ln\left(\tilde{c}_{1,t}^i\right)}{d\theta_{t+1}} + \beta_{t,i}\dfrac{d\ln\left(R_{t+1}\right)}{d\theta_{t+1}},
\end{align}
where each total derivative includes the response of the continuation policy $\tau_{t+1}$, and of $\theta_{t+2}$, to the design being chosen --- the former now also through the cost, since $f$ depends on $\tau_{t+1}$. Retirees are unanimous here --- the first expression does not depend on $i$ --- and favour a more contributive system, since a higher $\theta_{t+1}$ raises the return to an hour worked at $t$ and hence $h_t$, on which their benefits are levied. The young are divided, and the sign of the aggregate is a quantitative matter.

\smalltitle{Permanent.} At a reform date $t_0$ the electorate chooses a design expected to hold forever, $\theta_t = \theta$ for all $t\geq t_0$. Before $t_0$ the design is exogenous and constant at its calibrated value, and the vote is \emph{anticipated}: households arrive at $t_0$ knowing a design will be chosen, and what the electorate selects among is which of the equilibrium paths indexed by $\theta$ the economy follows from $t_0$ on. This is not an unanticipated reform, and the difference is not cosmetic --- it is what determines the value of the predetermined savings ratio the vote is taken against, as \cref{\refnumsec:ESC} sets out. All three channels operate at once: the sequential re-split of \eqref{\refeq:esc:seqFOC}, the labour-supply channel of \eqref{\refeq:esc:leadFOC}, and the continuation, which now moves the entire future path of taxes rather than one period of it. The marginal effects add,
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:esc:permFOC}
    \dfrac{d\upsilon_{2,t_0}^i}{d\theta} &= (1-\alpha)\dfrac{d\ln\left(\Theta_{h,t_0}\right)}{d\theta} + \mathcal{E}_{2,t_0}^{i,\theta}, &
    \dfrac{d\upsilon_{1,t_0}^i}{d\theta} &= (1+\beta_{t_0,i})\dfrac{d\ln\left(\tilde{c}_{1,t_0}^i\right)}{d\theta} + \beta_{t_0,i}\dfrac{d\ln\left(R_{t_0+1}\right)}{d\theta},
\end{align}
with $\mathcal{E}_{2,t_0}^{i,\theta}$ the summand of \eqref{\refeq:esc:seqFOC}. The electorate chooses $\tau_{t_0}$ and $\theta$ together, which appears to make the problem two-dimensional. It does not:

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:esc:concentration}
Under the permanent timing, the optimal tax at any candidate design is the ordinary politico-economic equilibrium tax at that design, $\tau^{\ast}(\theta) = \mathcal{T}_{t_0}(\theta)$, and the joint problem concentrates to a one-dimensional maximisation over $\theta$.
\end{proposition}

The argument is immediate once stated: $\partial\mathcal{W}_{t_0}/\partial\tau_{t_0} = 0$, taken with $s_{t_0-1,i}/s_{t_0-1}$ held fixed, \emph{is} the first order condition $z_{t_0}=0$ evaluated at $\theta_{t_0}=\theta$ --- including the factor $\left(1+\tau_{t_0}\partial_{\tau}\ln f\right)$ of \eqref{\refeq:esc:dlnc2i}, which $z_{t_0}$ carries. The permanent timing adds nothing to it, because $\theta$ is not a function of $\tau$. Likewise, for $t>t_0$ the design is fixed and the continuation is the ordinary equilibrium of \cref{\refsec:PEELOG} at a constant $\theta$ --- there is no recursion in the design to solve.


\subsubsection{Two structural properties of the log case}\label{\refsec:ESC:separability}
The log specification buys more than tractability here; it removes state variables from the design problem altogether.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:esc:separability}
Under log preferences, the date-$t$ political objective is additively separable,
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:esc:separability}
    \mathcal{W}_t = \mathcal{A}\left(\tau_t,\theta_t\right) + \mathcal{B}\left(\theta_{t+1}\right) + \mathcal{C}\left(s_{t-1}\right),
\end{align}
where $\mathcal{C}$ depends on no policy. Consequently the leaded choice of $\theta_{t+1}$ depends on neither $s_{t-1}$ nor $\theta_t$: the maximiser of \eqref{\refeq:esc:separability} is the maximiser of $\mathcal{B}$ alone.
\end{proposition}

To see \eqref{\refeq:esc:separability}, note that $\Theta_{h,t}$ in \eqref{\refeq:esc:auxiliary:Thetah} is a product of a factor in $\tau_t$ and a factor in $\left(\tau_{t+1},\theta_{t+1}\right)$, so $\ln h_t$ is a sum of a term in $\tau_t$, a term in $\theta_{t+1}$, and $\frac{\alpha\xi}{1+\alpha\xi}\ln(s_{t-1}/\nu_t)$. The same then holds for $\ln \tilde c_{1,t}^i = \frac{1+\xi}{\xi}\ln h_t + \ln\left[\cdot\right]$, whose bracket involves $\left(\theta_{t+1},\tau_{t+1}\right)$ only --- and $\tau_{t+1} = \mathcal{T}_{t+1}(\theta_{t+1})$ by decoupling, so $\theta_{t+1}$ only --- and for $\ln R_{t+1}$, which is affine in $\ln s_t$ and $\ln h_{t+1}$ and hence again in $\ln \Theta_{h,t}$ plus objects dated $t+1$ and later. Finally $\ln c_{2,t}^i = (1-\alpha)\ln h_t + \ln\left[s_{t-1,i}/s_{t-1} + \frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\tau_t\left(A(\theta_t,\tau_t)y^{\eta}_{t-1,i}/\Gamma_{h,t-1} + B(\theta_t,\tau_t)\right)\right] + \alpha\ln(s_{t-1}/\nu_t) + \text{const}$, whose second term involves $\left(\tau_t,\theta_t\right)$ and not $\theta_{t+1}$. Every dependence on $\theta_{t+1}$ therefore arrives through $\ln h_t$, additively.

\Cref{prop:esc:separability} is worth emphasising because it contradicts the natural reading of the leaded timing. One would expect $\theta_t$ to be a state variable at $t$, since it is chosen a period in advance and inherited; and it \emph{is} a state for the tax, through $z_t\left(\tau_t,\theta_t\right)$. It is not a state for the design: the electorate's preferred $\theta_{t+1}$ is the same whatever system it inherits. The property is specific to log preferences --- under CRRA the powers do not separate and both $s_{t-1}$ and $\theta_t$ re-enter --- and it is verified numerically rather than relied upon, in \cref{\refnumsec:ESC}.

\Cref{prop:esc:separability} does not extend to the permanent timing, where $\theta$ appears in $\mathcal{A}$ and $\mathcal{B}$ at once. There the predetermined ratio $s_{t_0-1,i}/s_{t_0-1}$ requires care on two counts. It is a function of the design in force at $t_0$, and yet the savings behind it are sunk when the vote is taken, so the electorate takes it as given: holding it fixed while varying the candidate is not an approximation but the definition of the problem. And because the vote is anticipated, the level at which it is held is the one the winning design implies, which makes the permanent equilibrium a fixed point rather than a single maximisation. \Cref{\refnumsec:ESC} gives the algorithm and reports what is at stake in getting either half wrong.
