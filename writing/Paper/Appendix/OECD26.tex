\section{The OECD cross-section}\label{app:oecd26}\setcounter{equation}{0}
This appendix documents the cross-country tests of sections \ref{sec:oecd} and \ref{sec:esc:crossSection}: the sample and the data, the U.S.\ at those data, where the political weight of the old and the cost parameter are calibrated, the second layer of the test, and the test under other measures of the design, other readings of the propensities to vote and a common political weight. Every economy is solved under log preferences, without the cost in section \ref{sec:oecd} and with it in section \ref{sec:esc:crossSection}, where the cost parameter of country $n$ is $\lambda_{n,t} = \varepsilon\,\Pi_{n,t}/R_{n,t}$ at each date. Exactly one candidate tax passes at every state and date of every solve (appendix \ref{app:T}). The \oa{esc-oecd26} repeats the test with the U.S.\ propensities to vote in every country.

\subsection{Sample and data}\label{app:oecd26:data}
The sample is the 21 countries of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}: the OECD members before 2000 in which informal employment is below a quarter of employment and one public pay-as-you-go system pays the bulk of retirement benefits. The first condition leaves out Korea, Mexico and Turkey.\footnote{Informal employment is the share of informal employment in total employment, indicator 8.3.1 of the Sustainable Development Goals, from ILOSTAT, in the latest year available, 2018 to 2020. It is $56\%$ in Mexico, $31\%$ in Turkey and $29\%$ in Korea. The next are the UK and the Czech Republic, at $20\%$ and $17\%$, where the measure mostly reflects self-employment that their pension systems cover. Canada, Japan, New Zealand and the U.S.\ have no such measure and stay in. Korea and Turkey also stand apart in their pension systems: Korea's public pension dates only from 1988, and Turkey's retirement age for men is 52.} The second holds where funded tiers pay less than a quarter of mandatory benefits in 2020 and the public pension is not tested against them, and leaves out Australia, Denmark, Iceland, the Netherlands and Switzerland. Funded pensions are private saving in the model, so where they are large, or where the public pension falls as they rise, the public benefit formula alone does not describe how retirement income depends on earnings. The test uses 20 of the 21 countries, since Luxembourg has no CSES survey from which to measure its propensities to vote.

The design is read off the benefit formula, in the same way for every country except the UK. We fit a least-squares line $a + c\,z$ through the gross public pension of a man with a full career at earnings $z$ of $0.5$, $1$ and $2$ times average earnings, under the rules of 2020 \parencite[table 4.2]{OECDpen}, and take the line's earnings-linked share at average earnings, $c/(a+c)$.\footnote{For New Zealand the lowest earnings level is $0.6$ times average earnings, as in the source. The formula is that of a worker who enters the labor market in 2020, and it describes the pensions paid in 2020 unless a reform is phasing in. The exception is the UK, where most retirees of 2020 draw the basic State Pension and the earnings-related State Second Pension, which the flat new State Pension replaced from 2016. Its $\theta$ is the State Second Pension's share of spending on the State Pensions and Pension Credit in 2019--20.} In the model this share is $\theta$: a retiree's benefit is proportional to $\theta y_i + 1-\theta$ (section \ref{sec:esc:cost}), a line in relative earnings whose earnings-linked share at mean earnings is $\theta$, with the cost of redistribution or without it (appendix \ref{app:esc:equilibrium}). A full-career worker at half of average earnings never draws the minimum top-ups and tested benefits that lie below his own pension, although many retirees do, and that spending is flat. We therefore multiply the share by $1-s$, where $s$ is the share of public old-age and survivors cash spending in 2020 that goes to the top-ups and tested benefits this worker does not reach.\footnote{The spending is that booked inside public old-age and survivors cash in the OECD Social Expenditure Database, taken from national administrative data and from the programme lines of the database; the technical documentation gives the source of each country's figure \parencite{BergGE26doc}. Italy's and Portugal's figures include the top-ups of invalidity pensions, and Belgium's leave out its minimum-pension supplement. In Norway and Poland the worker at half of average earnings reaches the minimum pension, so the formula already contains it and $s$ is zero; for Luxembourg no figure is available and $s$ is set to zero. Across the 21 countries $s$ is at most $0.082$, in Portugal.} The measure puts the U.S.\ at $0.544$, France at $0.822$ and the UK at $0.181$. Ireland and New Zealand, whose public pensions are flat, are at about $0$, and no country is at $1$. The Bismarckian index of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata} is $I = 1/(2-\theta)$ of this measure.

The other inputs are measured in the same way for every country, the U.S.\ included. The number of workers per retiree $\nu_t$ is the thirty-year growth of the total population in the medium variant of the United Nations' World Population Prospects 2024, from 1990 to 2080, with 1960 at the value of 1990 and the periods after 2080 at the value of 2080, as in the calibrations of section \ref{sec:oecd}; for France it is the plain ratio, without the correction for the retirement age of appendix \ref{app:US}. Pension spending is that of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}, public cash spending on old-age and survivors pensions in 2020, and the tax rate is spending over the labor share. The income distribution is that of the gross earnings of men in full-time employment in the OECD earnings database, in 2019 or the nearest year, through a lognormal fitted separately below and above the median to the ratios of the first and ninth deciles to the median. Households are grouped at the U.S.\ income percentiles of section \ref{sec:oecd}, so the three groups hold $58\%$, $18\%$ and $24\%$ of households in every country. The propensities to vote are each country's own, from the Comparative Study of Electoral Systems (CSES): turnout in a national election by quintile of household income, averaged over the percentiles of each income group.\footnote{The election is the one nearest 2020, within 2015 to 2022, with at least 30 respondents in each quintile. For Spain and Portugal, where no election qualifies, the elections are those of 2008 and 2009.} Everything else takes the U.S.\ values of section \ref{sec:oecd}: $\alpha = \xi = 0.30$, a taste for leisure common to all groups, which under log preferences sets hours but no rate, and the discount factor of the calibration without the cost, $\beta = 0.65$, imposed as on France in appendix \ref{app:US}. Table \ref{table:OECD26:omega} lists the inputs that differ across countries, except the propensities to vote.

\subsection{The U.S.\ at these data}\label{app:oecd26:anchor}
At these inputs the U.S.\ keeps the design of section \ref{sec:esc}, $\theta = 0.544$, since the measure is read off the benefit formula, and differs from its calibration of section \ref{sec:oecd} in two respects. Its dispersion of relative incomes is $0.333$, against $0.436$ at the CPS groups: the OECD series covers men in full-time work only and is fitted to three points of the distribution, so it misses the part-time and part-year earners that the CPS records. And its propensities to vote are those of the CSES, which rise less steeply with income than those of the CPS (table \ref{table:a_US:CalibUS}). With $\beta$ imposed, we calibrate $\omega$ to the U.S.\ tax rate and $\varepsilon$ so that the electorate of 1990 chooses the observed design for 2020, as in section \ref{sec:esc:calibration}, which gives $\omega = 1.26$ and $\varepsilon = 13.09$. At the CPS propensities and the same incomes, $\varepsilon$ is $10.78$ (figure \ref{fig:OECD26:thetaFitGrid}): where the poor vote relatively more often, the electorate leans further towards flat benefits, and a larger cost holds it at the observed design. At these values the cost takes $2.6\%$ of the revenue of 2020 at the observed design and would take $12.0\%$ at a flat benefit, a little less than at $\rho = 1$ in table \ref{table:US_ESC:calibration}. In the first layer of the test every country takes these values of $\beta$, $\omega$ and $\varepsilon$ (figure \ref{fig:OECD26:layer1}). Without the cost, the political weight that reproduces U.S.\ spending at these data is $1.19$, against $1.17$ in table \ref{table:US:CalibUSFR}, and every country takes it in the test of spending of section \ref{sec:oecd} (figure \ref{fig:OECD26:spending}).

\subsection{Each country's political weight}\label{app:oecd26:layer2}
In the second layer each country keeps the U.S.\ $\beta$ and $\varepsilon$, and its political weight of the old is calibrated so that its tax rate of 2020 at the observed design matches its pension spending, as $\omega$ is for the U.S.\ in section \ref{sec:oecd}. Its design is then chosen as in the first layer, and its spending is read on the path of that design, so it matches the data only where the predicted design is the observed one. Table \ref{table:OECD26:omega} reports the political weights and the predicted designs. The weight of the old runs from $0.95$ in Hungary to $1.68$ in Italy, against $1.26$ in the U.S\@. The predicted designs run from $0$ in Ireland, whose observed design is also at the Beveridgean corner, to $0.710$ in Italy, a narrower range than the observed one, which reaches $0.997$ in Hungary. For 17 of the 19 countries other than the U.S.\ the predicted design lies on the same side of the U.S.\ design as the observed one. The exceptions are Belgium, predicted above it at $0.605$, and Hungary, predicted below it at $0.522$. The UK is predicted at $0.503$ and France at $0.626$. Calibrated in full from their own household data and given the U.S.\ cost parameter, the two choose designs close to these, $0.493$ and $0.645$ (\oa{esc-countries}). The predicted designs keep the relations of the first layer with population growth, spending and the Gini (table \ref{table:OECD26:slopes}).

\subsection{The measure, the propensities to vote and the political weight}\label{app:oecd26:readings}
Table \ref{table:OECD26:thetaVariants} sets the paper's measure of the design, C, beside two others, each on the countries where it is defined and with the U.S.\ anchor recalibrated under it. Measure A extends C to every country whose public pension is not tested against funded tiers, which adds the Netherlands and Switzerland to the 21. Measure B takes the slope over all mandatory and quasi-mandatory tiers, public and funded \parencite[table 4.1]{OECDpen}, with the same correction for top-ups, adds the spending of the funded tiers to the tax and covers all 26 countries in which informal employment is below a quarter of employment. The three measures give nearly the same model slopes, with the same signs throughout, and no slope of the index differs across them by more than $0.07$. The data's slopes move more under B, which adds both the funded tiers and the five countries where they are large or the public pension is tested against them. On the countries of own turnout, the slope of the data's index on population growth is $-0.56$ under B, against $-0.77$ under C. Its slope on the Gini nearly vanishes, $0.19$ against $0.80$.

At the U.S.\ propensities to vote in every country, on each measure's countries, Luxembourg included, the model departs from the data on inequality under every measure. The index falls with the Gini in both layers, by about two standard errors in the first and not significantly in the second, while the data show an insignificant rise. Spending rises with the index less steeply than at each country's own propensities, at $27.79$ in the first layer under C against $16.33$ in the data. But the predicted designs barely track the observed ones (figure \ref{fig:OECD26:thetaFitGrid}): in the first layer under C the correlation between them is $0.17$, and the root mean squared error of the prediction, $0.34$, exceeds the $0.32$ of a prediction at the sample mean. Part of the shortfall is Luxembourg, which enters at the U.S.\ propensities and is predicted at $0.187$ in the first layer, for the reason given below.

Table \ref{table:OECD26:stylized} repeats the test under two stylized readings. The first sets every country in the first layer at the mean political weight of the second, $1.31$, in place of the anchor's $1.26$. It moves levels, not the cross-section. The mean predicted design rises from $0.527$ to $0.550$, against $0.593$ in the data. Mean spending rises from $10.2$ to $11.0\%$ of GDP, against $10.6\%$. The correlation between predicted and observed designs stays at $0.74$, and the slopes of the index on population growth and the Gini move by at most $0.03$, while spending rises with the index somewhat more steeply.

The second reading pools the turnout. The U.S.\ keeps its own CSES propensities; Belgium and Luxembourg, which enforce compulsory voting, vote at the same rate in every income group; and every other country takes the mean CSES profile of the countries that vote voluntarily. On the 20 countries of the paper's specification this matches own turnout: the correlations between predicted and observed designs differ by at most $0.03$, the mean design is the same, and the slopes keep their signs and significance. Outside the U.S., each country's own income gradient of turnout thus carries little that the pooled profile lacks. With Luxembourg, which the pooled turnout admits, the correlation in the first layer falls to $0.35$ (figure \ref{fig:OECD26:thetaFitGrid}). At the U.S.\ weight of the old, Luxembourg's fast-growing population, $1.65$ times its 1990 size in 2020, supports a tax of only $3.8\%$ at its observed design, under a third of the $12.9\%$ its spending implies. In so small a system a flat benefit would lose $2.7\%$ of revenue, against $12.0\%$ in the U.S\@. The electorate of 1990 therefore chooses nearly flat benefits for 2020, a design of $0.015$ against the observed $0.820$. In the second layer, where its weight of the old is calibrated to its spending, Luxembourg's predicted design is $0.471$ and the correlation is $0.78$. Luxembourg also flattens the data's slope of the index on population growth, from $-0.77$ to $-0.44$. The model's slope hardly depends on the reading of turnout, between $-0.31$ and $-0.23$ in the first layer.
