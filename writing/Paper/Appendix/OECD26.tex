\section{The OECD cross-section}\label{app:oecd26}\setcounter{equation}{0}
This appendix documents the test of section \ref{sec:esc:crossSection}: the sample and the data, the U.S.\ at those data, where the political weight of the old and the cost parameter are calibrated, and the second layer of the test, in which the political weight of the old is calibrated country by country. Every economy is solved under log preferences with the cost of section \ref{sec:esc:cost}, and exactly one candidate tax passes at every state and date of every solve (section \ref{sec:numerical}). The \oa{esc-oecd26} repeats the test on all 29 countries of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata} and with each country's own propensities to vote.

\subsection{Sample and data}\label{app:oecd26:data}
The sample is the 29 countries of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata} less those in which more than a quarter of employment is informal in the ILO's measure (ILOSTAT, 2018--2020): Mexico at $56\%$, Turkey at $31\%$ and Korea at $29\%$. The next are the UK, the Czech Republic and the Netherlands, at $20\%$, $17\%$ and $14\%$, where the measure mostly reflects self-employment that their pension systems cover. Australia, Canada, Japan, New Zealand and the U.S.\ have no such measure and stay in. Korea and Turkey also stand apart in their pension systems: Korea's public pension dates only from 1988, and Turkey's retirement age for men is 52. On the 26 countries the relations in the data keep the signs they have on the 29, except the flat relation between spending and the Gini (\oa{esc-oecd26-all}).

Every country, the U.S.\ included, is measured in the same way. The number of workers per retiree $\nu_t$ is the thirty-year growth of the total population in the medium variant of the United Nations' World Population Prospects 2024, from 1990 to 2080, with 1960 at the value of 1990 and the periods after 2080 at the value of 2080, as in the calibrations of section \ref{sec:oecd}; for France it is the plain ratio, without the correction for the retirement age of appendix \ref{app:US}. Pension spending and the index $I$ are those of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}, and the observed design is $\theta = 2-1/I$, the inverse of the mapping in section \ref{sec:esc:crossSection}, clipped to $[0,1]$. Australia, whose means-tested pension puts its index just below one half, and Ireland are therefore at the Beveridgean corner, and Austria, Finland, France, Hungary, Italy and Spain at the Bismarckian one. The income distribution is that of the gross earnings of men in full-time employment in the OECD earnings database, in 2019 or the nearest year, through a lognormal fitted separately below and above the median to the ratios of the first and ninth deciles to the median. Households are grouped at the U.S.\ income percentiles of section \ref{sec:oecd}, so the three groups hold $58\%$, $18\%$ and $24\%$ of households in every country. Everything else takes the U.S.\ values of section \ref{sec:oecd}: $\alpha = \xi = 0.30$, the propensities to vote from the CPS, a taste for leisure common to all groups, which under log preferences sets hours but no rate, and the discount factor of the calibration without the cost, $\beta = 0.655$, imposed as on the UK and France in appendix \ref{app:escResults:calibration}. Table \ref{table:OECD26:omega} lists the inputs that differ across countries.

\subsection{The U.S.\ at these data}\label{app:oecd26:anchor}
At these inputs the U.S.\ differs from its calibration of section \ref{sec:oecd} in two respects. Its design is $\theta = 0.735$, the inverse of its index, $39.2/49.6 = 0.790$, where section \ref{sec:esc} has $0.728$, since the means of the CPS groups lie close to, but not exactly at, half the mean and the mean. Its dispersion of relative incomes is $0.333$, against $0.436$ at the CPS groups: the OECD series covers men in full-time work only and is fitted to three points of the distribution, so it misses the part-time and part-year earners that the CPS records. France's is $0.150$, against $0.218$ in the Luxembourg Income Study (table \ref{table:OECD26:omega}). With $\beta$ imposed, we calibrate $\omega$ to the U.S.\ tax rate and $\lambda$ so that the electorate of 1990 chooses the observed design for 2020, as in section \ref{sec:esc:calibration}, which gives $\omega = 1.294$ and $\lambda = 11.109$, against $11.345$ at the CPS groups (table \ref{table:US_ESC:calibration}). Figure \ref{fig:OECD26:layer1} sets the first layer of the test, in which every country takes these values, beside the data.

\subsection{Each country's political weight}\label{app:oecd26:layer2}
In the second layer each country keeps the U.S.\ $\beta$ and $\lambda$, and its political weight of the old is calibrated so that its tax rate of 2020 at the observed design matches its pension spending, as $\omega$ is in section \ref{sec:oecd}. Its design is then predicted as in the first layer, which extends the test of appendix \ref{app:escResults:calibration} from the UK and France to 25 countries. Table \ref{table:OECD26:omega} reports the political weights and the predicted designs. The weight of the old runs from $0.947$ in Hungary to $1.761$ in Luxembourg, against $1.294$ in the U.S. The predicted designs run from $0.570$ in Ireland to $0.937$ in Finland, a narrower range than the observed one, and they keep the relations of the first layer with population growth and spending, while their relation with the Gini is no longer significant (table \ref{table:OECD26:slopes}). For 17 of the 25 countries the predicted design lies on the same side of the U.S.\ design as the observed one. The UK is among the exceptions: it is predicted at $0.805$, above the U.S., where appendix \ref{app:escResults:calibration}, at its own income groups and propensities to vote, places it below. When every country, the U.S.\ included, takes its own propensities to vote from the CSES, which in the UK rise less with income than in the U.S., the UK is predicted at $0.723$, below the U.S.\ (\oa{esc-oecd26-turnout}). France is predicted at $0.903$, short of its corner.
