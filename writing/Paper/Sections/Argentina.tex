\section{Pension System Reform in Argentina}\label{sec:argentina}\label{Argentina}

Argentina is a middle-income country that faces high levels of labor informality. In 1994 the social security system was reformed introducing strict contribution requirements, which resulted in about a third of individuals approaching retirement age in 2005 not being eligible for full benefits.\footnote{A pension reform in 1994 allowed workers the option between a fully-funded or a pay-as-you-go system. Regardless of this option, workers were required to have 30 years of contributions to receive benefits. In 2007 workers under the fully-funded system were given the option to return to a pay-as-you go system. Then in December 2008 the fully-funded pension funds were nationalized, and its beneficiaries were transferred to the pay-as-you-go system.} Since 2005 a number of reforms were introduced to increase pension coverage. In particular, tax amnesties added 2.7 million additional beneficiaries raising the coverage rate of the elderly from 68\% to 91\%, see figure \ref{fig:Arg}. This was achieved by allowing workers with less than the required 30 years of contributions to receive pensions.\footnote{See \textcite{CetrangoloG} and \textcite{Roffman} for details on recent pension reforms in Argentina, and \textcite{Ruffo} for an estimate of the effect of the tax amnesty on savings.} 
Thus, in a few years, Argentina experienced a significant increase in non-contributive pensions: 
Between 2005 and 2010 social security spending increased by 1.9\% of GDP with most of this increase, 1.8\% of GDP, due to the tax amnesty \parencite{CetrangoloG}.

\smalltitle{Calibration.}
We now describe the calibration of the model with informal households to Argentina before the pension reforms and estimate its predictions for the observed increase in $\epsilon$ due to the tax amnesties on equilibrium tax rates, labor supply and savings. We assume informal households work when young and save for retirement, facing the same rate of return on savings as formal households. 

To calibrate the income distribution and initial labor supply, we resort to the Argentine household survey Encuesta Permanente de Hogares (EPH). We use data from the last two quarters of 2006 and split formal workers, a status that we infer from social security contributions, into four quartiles (see appendix \ref{app:EPH} for details). For formal and informal workers we estimate average income and average hours worked. 
Before the tax amnesties, retirees over the age of 70 who had made contributions for a minimum of 10 years were eligible for basic pension coverage. This coverage consisted of 70\% of the minimum pension and a proportional amount from other contributions.\footnote{The basic pensions was set by law 24347 of year 1995 at 70\% of the first pillar, denoted ``prestaci\'on b\'asica universal'', plus the corresponding fully-funded or pay-as-you-go benefit. For simplicity we assume the latter on average was also 70\% of the benefits received by workers that had at least 30 years of contributions. Thus, basic pensions were 70\% of the benefits received by the least productive formal workers.} We calculated the pre-reform $\epsilon$ \begin{align*}
        \epsilon = 0.7 \left(1-\theta+\theta\frac{\eta^1h_t^1}{h_t}\right)\dfrac{\beta^{5/30} \frac{\mathrm{LE_{men}-5}}{\mathrm{LE_{men}}}+\beta^{10/30} \frac{\mathrm{LE_{women}-10}}{\mathrm{LE_{women}}}}{2},
    \end{align*}
where $\mathrm{LE_{men}}=14.45$ and $\mathrm{LE_{women}}=22.55$ are the expected lifetime for men and women, respectively, from their retirement ages (women retire at age 60, men at 65), such that the formula above corrects for the basic pension being received 5 or 10 years after the formal retirement age.\footnote{To estimate life expectancy at retirement age for Argentina we conduct a regression analysis using data for men and women's life expectancies at birth and at age 65 for 40 OECD countries for the year 2000. We then use the fitted regression and data from the World Bank on male and female life expectancy at birth in Argentina in 2000.} We set $\gamma_0$ to match the share of retirees that only received this pension prior to the reform, 32\% \parencite{CetrangoloG}. To identify the Bismarckian incentives, we use OECD data on the ratio of replacement rates, $RR$, at half mean and mean wages for males and females.\footnote{In a pure Bismarckian system the replacement rate would be the same at all wage levels, such that $RR=1$, while in a pure Beveridgean it would double if we halve wages, implying $RR=0.5$. Note that we combine men and women since we do not have data separately on the increase in coverage or government spending by gender.} Specifically, we adjust $\theta$ to ensure that
    \begin{align*}
        RR \equiv \dfrac{\theta+(1-\theta)\frac{h_t}{\eta_t^2h_t^2}}{\theta+(1-\theta)\frac{h_t}{\eta_t^1h_t^1}}= \dfrac{0.678}{0.803},
    \end{align*}
where the superscripts refer to the income quartiles, chosen such that $2 \eta_t^1h_t^1=\eta_t^2h_t^2=h_t$.


After the reforms, we set $\epsilon=1-\theta+\theta \eta^1h_t^1/h_t$ implying that, at their retirement age, all informal workers receive the same benefit as formal workers with the lowest productivity. To gauge the impact transitioning to more universal pension benefits, we calibrate the model to Argentina in 2005-2010 and take a period to be 30 years.
The capital income share is that of the \emph{formal} sector. In the model, informal households produce outside $Y_t$ and $K_t$, and national accounts book the output of unincorporated household enterprises, where most informal work sits, as mixed income, which a share computed as one minus the wage bill over GDP counts entirely as capital \parencite{Gollina1}. The economy-wide share for Argentina is 0.43 \parencite{Frankema}. If a share $y$ of GDP is informal mixed income, the formal-sector share is $(0.43-y)/(1-y)$: informal earnings of about 5\% of GDP, as in the household survey, give 0.40, while shadow-economy estimates of 20--25\% of GDP give 0.30. We take $\alpha = 0.35$, which also puts Argentina on the same footing as the Gollin-adjusted shares we use for the rich economies in section \ref{sec:oecd}.\footnote{The same logic would raise the capital-output target, since the Penn World Table stock is economy-wide and the informal sector is labor-intensive. We keep the measured ratio, because no source measures an informal capital share and the stock also includes housing, which cuts the other way.} In the baseline, we take the elasticity of labor supply, $\xi=0.30$, and explore values between $0.20$ and $0.40$ as robustness.\footnote{Changes in this parameter affect the calibration but have minor effects on the implications of pension reform. This is consistent with \textcite{Imrohoroglu} that finds aggregate labor supply to be insensitive to this elasticity.} To calibrate $\beta$, we target the capital-output ratio. A model period is thirty years and capital depreciates fully between periods, so the capital stock in place at $t$ is the previous generation's savings, $K_t = s_{t-1}/\nu_t$, and with Cobb--Douglas production $K_t/Y_t = \alpha/R_t$ --- a stock relative to one period, i.e.\ thirty years, of output. Thirty times that ratio is the capital-output ratio as the national accounts report it. In our model with $K_t/Y_t = \alpha / R_t$ this is essentially equivalent to targeting the thirty year interest rate, which for Argentina is not credibly measured. We target $K/Y = 3.23$, Argentina's ratio in 2010.\footnote{Capital stock and GDP at constant national prices (\texttt{rnna}, \texttt{rgdpna}) from the Penn World Table 11.0 \parencite{pwt}, retrieved through FRED. We read it at the calibration year, where the tax rate, the replacement-rate ratio, the coverage share and the household survey are all measured, rather than averaging.} Calibration of $\beta$ and $\epsilon$ are done numerically as $\beta$ depends on the politico-economic equilibrium path for savings and labor supply and $\epsilon$ depends on $\beta$.



We calibrate the income distribution and relative working hours across the income distribution using $\eta_j$ and $X_j$. Specifically, let $z_j$ denote income relative to the average formal household; with labor supply following $h_t^i/h_t = \eta_iX_i^{\xi}$ for formal households (cf. proposition \ref{prop:LOG:EE}), we solve the system
\begin{align}\label{eq:calibration_eta}
        z_i^{\eta} &= \dfrac{w_t(1-\tau_t)h_t^i \eta_i}{w_t(1-\tau_t)h_t} = \dfrac{\eta_i^{1+\xi}/X_i^{\xi}}{\sum_{j>0} \left(\gamma_ j\eta_j^{1+\xi}/X_j^{\xi}\right)}, \ \ \ \ \ i>0\\
         z_0^{\eta} &= \dfrac{w_t h_t^0 \eta_0}{w_t(1-\tau_t)h_t} = \dfrac{\eta_0^{1+\xi}/X_0^{\xi}}{(1-\tau_t)\sum_{j>0} \left(\gamma_ j\eta_j^{1+\xi}/X_j^{\xi}\right)}. \nonumber
    \end{align}
We normalize $\sum_i \gamma_i\eta_i^{1+\xi}/X_i^{\xi} = 1$ and solve equations above for a vector of productivities $\eta_j$.

    
To construct the time series for $\nu_t$ we follow \textcite{mkvss08} and use 30-year gross population growth rate and projections from census data. Finally, we use social security spending before the 2001 crisis of 7.1\% of GDP,\footnote{Data from ANSES. We use pre-crisis spending as it was relatively stable during that time, and after 2010 contributive pensions returned to their pre-crisis levels \parencite{CetrangoloG}. Following the 2001 crisis, pension spending was reduced (reaching 5.8\% of GDP in 2005) by only allowing minimum pensions to adjust for inflation.} to calibrate $\omega$. Since the payroll tax falls on formal labor income, which is a share $1-\alpha$ of formal output, spending of 7.1\% of GDP corresponds to an equivalent social security tax rate of $\tau_{2010} = 0.071/(1-\alpha) = 0.109$.

\smalltitle{Pension reform shocks.}
For our baseline we take the specification with log-GHH preferences and informal savings. Table \ref{table:Argentina:Universal} shows the effect of the pension reform on tax rates, savings, and labor supply. The Economic Equilibrium scenario refers to the case where we resolve for an economic equilibrium with universal pension benefits, but keep taxes constant. In general, we find higher tax rates, lower savings, and lower labor supply, with the primary driver of both savings and labor supply coming from endogenous policies: with taxes held fixed, formal savings rise, since informal households now hold a claim on the pension system and save less, and the fall in hours is a third of its full-effect size.

In 2010 the model predicts that a shift to a more universal pension system increases the equivalent social security tax rate by about 1.0 p.p., which is an increase in pension spending of about 0.6\% of GDP. The model thus accounts for about a third of the observed increase in pension spending in this period of around 1.9\% of GDP. The model predicts a drop of 0.2 p.p.\ of GDP in the savings rate, while average weekly labor supply drops by about 0.1 hours.\footnote{Findings consistent with \textcite{Ruffo}, that estimate that this reform had a negative impact on savings.} Informal households benefit from higher benefits and from a higher return to savings due to lower formal savings rates. The model predicts their savings drop by about 13\%. The tax response builds over time: by 2040 the tax rate is 1.7 p.p.\ above its pre-reform path. The reason for this is that informal households, now covered, retire with less private savings, and retirees with less private income support higher taxes.

\smalltitle{The effect of the intertemporal elasticity of substitution.}
We next consider the effect of general CRRA preferences and the model predictions from the pension reform. Given that our framework features no uncertainty, the more appropriate interpretation for $\rho$ is that it is the intertemporal elasticity of substitution (IES), i.e. it reflects households attitudes towards allocation of consumption over time, not across states of nature. The empirical evidence on the magnitude of the IES remains inconclusive.\footnote{\textcite{Guvenena1}, for instance, argues that taking into account limited asset market participation and heterogeneity in wealth holdings can help reconcile the relatively low estimates obtained from aggregate consumption data with the much higher estimates derived from output or wealth measures. Asset pricing models with Epstein-Zin preferences and either long-run risk or disaster risk show that to explain empirical facts both risk aversion and IES have to be larger than one (or lower than one but risk aversion is known to be larger than one). See \textcite{BansalYa1} and \textcite{Barroa1}.} In view of these considerations, we rely on the estimates obtained under logarithmic utility as our benchmark. Table \ref{table:Argentina:funcOfRho} repeats the analysis from making pensions universal after recalibration to the pre-reform economy and figure \ref{fig:ARG:EffectOfCRRA} collects the impact in 2010 and 2040 across $\rho$.

The table and figure show that the effects of the pension reform are nonlinear in $\rho$, in particular for values lower than one. When $\rho<1$ the effects of the pension reform are dampened across all metrics: at $\rho = 0.5$ the tax rate increases by only 0.1 p.p.\ in 2010, against 1.0 p.p.\ under logarithmic utility, and the savings rate does not move. The reason for this is that a low intertemporal elasticity makes the young resist taxation more strongly, so the recalibrated economy needs a larger political weight of the old to reproduce the observed tax rate and responds less to a change in benefits. For IES in the range $[1, 2]$, however, the effect on taxes is about 1.0 p.p.\ in 2010 and about 1.7 p.p.\ in 2040 throughout, the effect on labor supply is flat at about 0.1 hours, and the savings effect shrinks gradually, from 0.2 p.p.\ of GDP at $\rho = 1$ to 0.1 p.p.\ at $\rho = 2$. Informal savings fall by 13 to 14\% throughout this range. Thus, over $\rho\in[1,2]$, the more plausible range for our model framework, the predictions are not drastically different from the logarithmic benchmark.
