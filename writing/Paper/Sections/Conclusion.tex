\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclu}

This paper studies how two features of a pension system, whether benefits require a contribution record and how closely they follow past earnings, shape its political support and through it taxes, savings and labor supply. The setting is an overlapping generations model with capital, heterogeneous households, an informal sector and probabilistic voting, first over the size of the system and then over its design as well.

With log-GHH preferences and hand-to-mouth informal households the equilibrium tax is a function of parameters and demographics alone. It rises with the political weight of the old, with ageing and with income inequality, and it falls when benefits are more closely tied to earnings, since earnings-related benefits move revenue from poor retirees to rich ones; universal pensions raise the tax when informal retirees are poorer than formal ones and the pension is a comparable share of their consumption. Calibrated to Argentina, the model accounts for about three fifths of the rise in pension spending that accompanied the expansion of coverage of 2005 to 2010, with the savings rate lower by 0.4 p.p.\ of GDP and the workweek by a quarter of an hour. In the model calibrated to the U.S., acute ageing, which holds the population constant, is the main determinant of the tax rate and the earnings link the second, ahead of income inequality and voting patterns, at every intertemporal elasticity of substitution we consider; a higher elasticity shrinks the effects of ageing, the earnings link and the income distribution and enlarges that of the voting profile. In 26 synthetic OECD countries, the U.S.\ model with each country's demography, income distribution and pension design, pension spending falls with population growth about as steeply as in the data and, as in the data, is unrelated to income inequality.

When the electorate chooses the earnings link as well, costless redistribution puts the choice at a corner: with propensities to vote that do not rise with income the design chosen with the tax is fully Beveridgean, and at U.S.\ inequality each of the three timings we examine gives a corner at every intertemporal elasticity of substitution we check, the Beveridgean one in the Markov equilibria we study. An interior design needs flat benefits to be costlier than earnings-related ones, and we read the flat component as an implicit tax whose deadweight loss rises with the square of its rate, so that the share of revenue lost grows with the size of the system. With this cost calibrated on the U.S.\ design alone, ageing makes the chosen design more Bismarckian at every intertemporal elasticity of substitution, a voting profile as flat as France's makes it less so, and a more equal income distribution moves it in a direction that depends on that elasticity. In the OECD data, spending and the earnings link rise together as population growth falls, and neither follows inequality. The 26 synthetic countries, choosing their designs at an elasticity of one under the U.S.\ cost of redistribution, reproduce the first pattern in sign, though the earnings link responds less to population growth than in the data. At the U.S.\ political weight and propensities to vote, however, more unequal countries choose less Bismarckian designs, unlike in the data; at each country's own propensities the design does not follow inequality, as in the data.

Three limitations point to future work. The model has no risk; in \textcite{BergGEwp} we study a related environment in which health risk may prevent households from qualifying for a contributive pension, and find that households work and save more in response. Every quantitative result rests on GHH preferences, which rule out income effects on labor supply; the hours responses and the retirees' stake in an earnings-related design both work through labor supply and may change once such effects are allowed. And the cost specification cannot by itself deliver a fully Bismarckian system, as in six of the 26 countries, because under a cost quadratic in the redistribution performed the first unit of redistribution is free at the margin; a linear term in the cost, or a formality margin, toward which the informal sector of our Argentine model is a first step, would allow it. The same margin bears on the rise of platform work and other arrangements outside contributive coverage in rich economies, which the model can address.
