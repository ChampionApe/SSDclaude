\section{Endogenous Pension Design}\label{sec:esc}
Throughout the paper so far the Bismarckian index $\theta$ is calibrated and held fixed while the tax rate is chosen politically. This section lets the electorate choose the design as well. The design is set by the same probabilistic-voting objective $\mathcal{W}_t$ that sets $\tau_t$, in the model without informal households, so that one political process delivers both the size of the system and its shape. We find that ageing makes the chosen design more Bismarckian and that inequality moves it little, which is the pattern of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}. The section runs in four parts: the corner that costless redistribution produces (section \ref{sec:esc:corner}); the deadweight cost of redistribution that makes an interior design possible (section \ref{sec:esc:cost}); its calibration on the U.S.\ design and the test of that calibration on the UK and France (section \ref{sec:esc:calibration}); and the response of the chosen design to ageing, inequality and voting patterns (section \ref{sec:esc:results}). Derivations, the algorithms and the verification battery are in the technical documentation published alongside the paper.\footnote{\textcite{BergGE26doc}, section \textit{Endogenous system characteristics}: the model definitions and propositions for each timing of the choice, the derivation of the cost, the numerical algorithms, including the exact two-dimensional policy-function recursion under CRRA preferences, and the calibration protocol.}

\subsection{Costless redistribution: a corner}\label{sec:esc:corner}
We consider three timings of the choice, and each puts the design at a corner. If $\theta_t$ is chosen at $t$ together with $\tau_t$, it re-splits benefits that current retirees have already earned. Current labor supply depends on the design that will apply to the benefits it earns, $\theta_{t+1}$, so the choice moves no economic decision, and its marginal effect on the political objective runs entirely through current retirees,
\begin{align}\label{eq:esc:seqFOC}
   \dfrac{\partial \mathcal{W}_t}{\partial \theta_t} =\omega \sum_i\gamma_i\mu_i\frac{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\tau_t\left(y_i-1\right)}{\frac{s_{t-1}^i}{s_{t-1}}+\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\tau_t\left(1+\theta_t\left[y_i-1\right]\right)}, \qquad y_i \equiv \frac{\eta_{i}^{1+\xi}}{X_{i}^{\xi}\,\Gamma_{h}},
\end{align}
where $y_i$ is the labor income of type $i$ relative to the mean. The numerator is the change in the benefit of type $i$ and averages to zero across types: a more Bismarckian split moves a given total from retirees below mean income to retirees above it. The denominator is proportional to the consumption of a retiree of type $i$ and rises with income. With equal propensities to vote the sum therefore weights the gains of the rich by a lower marginal utility than the losses of the poor, and is negative at every $\theta_t \in [0,1]$ whatever the tax rate, the political weight of the old and the demography: the electorate chooses the Beveridgean corner. We prove this in proposition \ref{prop:esc:corner} (appendix \ref{app:PEE:corner}) for any propensities to vote that do not rise with income, equal ones included: the derivative is negative at every design and the design chosen with the tax is $\theta_t = 0$, whatever the tax rate, the political weight of the old, the demography, the income distribution and the intertemporal elasticity, and whatever design current retirees expected when they saved. Only propensities to vote that rise with income can overturn the corner. They do in the U.S.\ calibration of section \ref{sec:oecd}, so there the sign is a quantitative matter: evaluated at the calibrated 2020 equilibrium the expression is negative over the whole interval, at every date of the projection and at $\rho = 0.5$, $1$ and $2$, and the choice is the corner $\theta = 0$.

If instead $\theta_{t+1}$ is chosen at $t$, the design no longer touches the benefits of current retirees but moves current labor supply and savings, directly and through the anticipated response of $\tau_{t+1}$. With the political objective of \eqref{eq:LOG:PolObj} the marginal effect is
\begin{align}\label{eq:esc:leadFOC}
   \dfrac{\partial \mathcal{W}_t}{\partial \theta_{t+1}} = \sum_i\gamma_i\mu_i\left[\omega\,(1-\alpha)\dfrac{d\ln\Theta_{h,t}}{d\theta_{t+1}} + \nu_t\left((1+\beta)\dfrac{d\ln\tilde{c}_{1,t}^i}{d\theta_{t+1}} + \beta\dfrac{d\ln R_{t+1}}{d\theta_{t+1}}\right)\right],
\end{align}
where $\Theta_{h,t}$ is the labor supply function of appendix \ref{app:EE:LOG} and each total derivative includes the response of $\tau_{t+1}$, and of $\theta_{t+2}$, to the design chosen. Retirees are unanimous, since their term does not depend on $i$, and they favour a more contributive system: a higher $\theta_{t+1}$ raises the return to an hour worked at $t$ and with it the labor supply on which their benefits are levied. The young are divided between this forward-looking stake and the redistribution of their own future benefits, the force of \eqref{eq:esc:seqFOC} one period ahead. The equilibrium is a sequence of policy functions over the inherited design, identified by backward induction as in section \ref{sec:numerical}, and at observed U.S.\ inequality the redistributive force dominates: the choice is again the corner $\theta = 0$.

A design chosen once and for all enters every channel at once, and the outcome is again a corner: $\theta = 0$ under log preferences, and under CRRA preferences the Bismarckian corner $\theta = 1$ at high intertemporal elasticities, where the forward-looking channels are strong.\footnote{Near the elasticity at which the corner switches the electorate is close to indifferent between the two, so the switch is not sharply located. The joint choice of $\tau_{t_0}$ and $\theta$ reduces to a one-dimensional search, and the model with a permanent choice can be calibrated to the observed 2020 characteristics only under counterfactually compressed inequality; the technical documentation gives the algorithm and its checks.} The common lesson is that a costless choice never lands in the interior: some friction must make flat benefits more expensive than earnings-related ones for an interior design to survive.

\subsection{A deadweight cost of redistribution}\label{sec:esc:cost}
We introduce a deadweight cost of the redistribution that the flat component of benefits performs, so that the government budget becomes
\begin{align}\label{eq:esc:budget}
    \nu_t w_t\tau_th_t\, f(\theta_t,\tau_t) &= \sum_i \gamma_i b_t^i, \\
    f(\theta_t,\tau_t) &= \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\lambda\,\tau_t\,\tilde{V}\,(1-\theta_t)^2\right), \qquad \tilde{V} \equiv \sum_i\gamma_i\frac{(y_i-1)^2}{y_i}, \nonumber
\end{align}
where $\lambda \geq 0$ is the cost parameter and a share $1-f$ of revenue does not reach retirees. Nothing is lost when nothing is redistributed: $f = 1$ at $\theta_t = 1$, and $f = 1$ for every design when all incomes are equal, where a flat and an earnings-related benefit pay the same to everyone. The parameter $\lambda$ is a reduced form for whatever makes redistribution through the pension system expensive, and its discipline is the cross-country test of section \ref{sec:esc:calibration}: one value, calibrated on the U.S.\ design alone, has to place the designs of the UK and France.

The form follows from reading the flat component as a tax. Part of a pension contribution buys the contributor a benefit and part does not, and only the second part distorts like a tax, the tax component of contributions of \textcite{Summers89a1}. Relative to a purely earnings-related system paying the same revenue, the flat component moves the benefit of type $i$ by $\tau_t(1-\theta_t)(1/y_i-1)$ per unit of past earnings: a tax on types above mean income, a subsidy to types below it, and a pure transfer in the aggregate.\footnote{Up to the pay-as-you-go return factor $\Pi_t = \nu_t w_t h_t/(w_{t-1}h_{t-1})$, which multiplies every implicit tax alike and which we absorb into $\lambda$, so that an elasticity $\varepsilon$ corresponds to $\lambda = \Pi_t\varepsilon$. The factor is of the order of $\nu_t$, which is $1.34$ for the U.S.\ in 2020 (table \ref{table:US:Calib}). The technical documentation gives the derivation.} The model contains the hours response to this wedge, through the Frisch elasticity; the cost stands for the margins it does not contain, on which the evidence finds the tax component to matter: participation, where \textcite{Disney04a1} finds that the tax component of contributions in OECD countries lowers the participation of women while the saving component raises it, and reporting, where \textcite{KumlerVF20a1} find that Mexico's 1997 reform, which tied benefits more closely to reported wages, reduced the underreporting of wages. On both margins the wedge that matters is the average tax on the earnings at stake rather than the marginal one \parencite{Saez02a1,KlevenK06a1}, and the average implicit tax of the flat component differs across types.

The deadweight loss of a set of such wedges is the sum of their \textcite{Harberger64a1} triangles. With a common elasticity $\varepsilon$ it is $\tfrac12\varepsilon\,\tau_t(1-\theta_t)^2\tilde{V}$ as a share of revenue, which is $1-f$ in \eqref{eq:esc:budget} to first order with $\lambda$ in the place of $\varepsilon$.\footnote{The quadratic counts the subsidy to types below mean income as a loss, as it does the tax on types above it. Where participation is already taxed, lowering the participation tax of low-income workers can reduce the total loss \parencite{Saez02a1}, so the symmetry is a property of the reduced form rather than of the argument.} The loss is quadratic in the flat share $1-\theta_t$ because it is quadratic in each wedge; proportional to the dispersion of relative incomes $\tilde{V}$ because the wedges scale with the distance of incomes from the mean, so a compressed income distribution lowers the redistribution a flat benefit performs and the cost of performing it together; and linear in the size of the system $\tau_t$ per unit of revenue, since the loss rises with the square of the implicit tax while revenue rises with the tax itself. The last property makes a larger system costlier to keep redistributive, the efficiency--redistribution trade-off by which \textcite{KoethenbuergerPP08a1} account for the finding of \textcite{CondeRuizP07a1} that more redistributive systems are smaller, read from the other side. The shape of $f$ follows from the tax component, but its size does not: at $\rho \leq 1$ the calibrated $\lambda$ (table \ref{table:US_ESC:calibration}) is more than an order of magnitude above what participation and taxable-income elasticities of $0.1$ to $0.4$ would give \parencite{ChettyGMW11a1,SaezSG12a1}, and at $\rho = 2$ roughly three to thirteen times as large, so $\lambda$ also stands in for administrative, evasion and political costs of redistribution that the model does not contain.\footnote{The cost is proportional, scaling both components of the benefit alike. A cost on the flat component only delivers the same endpoints and differs in the interior, at a cost in identification, since $f$ then no longer cancels from the replacement-rate ratio and $\theta^{\ast}$ and $\lambda$ are identified jointly. A cost attached to the design itself rather than to the transfer it performs, $f(\theta) = \phi + (1-\phi)\theta^{p}$, stays in place when a compressed income distribution removes the redistributive stakes; appendix \ref{app:US:escTables} reports it as a robustness check.}

With the cost, the benefit formula carries $A(\theta_t,\tau_t) = f\,\theta_t$ and $B(\theta_t,\tau_t) = f\,(1-\theta_t)$ in place of $\theta_t$ and $1-\theta_t$, and since the design enters the economic equilibrium only through these two groups, the cost is exactly that substitution in the equilibrium functions of appendix \ref{app:EE:LOG}. Because the cost is proportional it cancels from the replacement-rate ratio that identifies $\theta$ from the OECD data, so the data target is unchanged, $\theta^{\ast} = 0.738$. In the tax condition the cost adds one factor: since $f$ falls with the tax that funds it, the benefit term of the retirees' marginal effect $\mathcal{E}_{2,t}^i$ in appendix \ref{app:PEE:LOG} is multiplied by $1+\tau_t\,\partial_{\tau}\ln f = 1+\ln f$, so a larger system is worth less to a retiree at the margin than the revenue it collects.

We let the design be chosen one period in advance, and use only this timing with the cost. A design chosen for the current period moves no labor supply or savings decision, so the cost would be the only force against the corner of \eqref{eq:esc:seqFOC}; a design chosen once and for all requires a commitment that the Markov equilibria of the rest of the paper do not have. The choice one period in advance keeps the Markov structure, it is the only timing in which the design affects labor supply and savings before it binds, the channel through which $\theta$ moves taxes and savings in section \ref{sec:oecd}, and it mirrors how reforms of benefit formulas are legislated, applying to future benefits and leaving benefits in payment untouched.

In this timing the design in force, $\theta_t$, is a state whose cost enters the benefits of current retirees and, through them, the tax condition; the design chosen at $t$, $\theta_{t+1}$, enters \eqref{eq:esc:leadFOC} through next period's groups $A(\theta_{t+1},\tau_{t+1})$ and $B(\theta_{t+1},\tau_{t+1})$, with $\tau_{t+1}$ the anticipated equilibrium tax. What makes an interior choice possible is how the two groups move with the design. Without the cost a more Bismarckian design shifts next period's benefits one for one from the flat to the earnings-related component, $\partial_{\theta}A = -\partial_{\theta}B = 1$, and the redistributive stake of the young dominates, as in section \ref{sec:esc:corner}. With the cost, $\partial_{\theta}A + \partial_{\theta}B = f_{\theta} \geq 0$, where $f_{\theta} = f\,\lambda\tau_{t+1}\tilde{V}(1-\theta_{t+1})$ at a given tax: a more Bismarckian design also raises the total the young will receive, since it dissipates less. This gain is zero at $\theta_{t+1} = 1$ and rises with the flat share, the dispersion of incomes and the size of the system the young anticipate, so the design condition can cross zero in the interior. The explicit expressions, and the proofs that the substitution and the state independence of the log case survive the cost, are in the technical documentation.

\subsection{Calibration and a cross-country test}\label{sec:esc:calibration}
The cost parameter is identified separately from the design, by requiring that the design in force in the calibration year, on a path where the political choice binds from the first period, is the observed one,
\begin{align}\label{eq:esc:calibration}
    \theta_{t_0} = \Theta_{t_0-1}\!\left(\theta^{\ast}\right) = \theta^{\ast},
\end{align}
with $(\beta,\omega)$ recalibrated at every trial value of $\lambda$. The calibration pins the design in the calibration year only; every later design is a prediction of the model. Table \ref{table:US_ESC:calibration} reports the calibrated cost across the paper's range of intertemporal elasticities, at the U.S.\ dispersion of relative incomes $\tilde{V} = 0.436$: $\lambda = 18.267$, $8.643$ and $1.724$ at $\rho = 0.5, 1, 2$, a fall by a factor of about ten. At those values the observed design loses $3.9\%$, $1.8\%$ and $0.4\%$ of revenue at the 2020 size of the system, and a fully flat system of the same size would lose $44\%$, $24\%$ and $5\%$. The reason for this is that a higher elasticity strengthens the forward-looking channels of \eqref{eq:esc:leadFOC}, so less friction is needed to hold the choice away from the Beveridgean corner. That the calibrated cost lies closest to estimated elasticities at $\rho = 2$ adds to the case of section \ref{sec:argentina} for $\rho \in [1,2]$ as the more plausible range.

The cost is calibrated on one datum, the U.S.\ design, so the UK and France are a test of the specification rather than of its fit. Table \ref{table:US_ESC:country} lets each electorate choose the design on its own calibrated path under the U.S.\ parameter $\lambda = 8.643$, at $\rho = 1$. The UK, with an observed design of $0.560$ and a tax rate of $11.9\%$, chooses $0.625$; France, with the corner $\theta = 1$ and $21.3\%$, chooses $0.749$; the U.S.\ re-elects $0.738$ by construction. The observed ranking of the three is thus reproduced from one parameter, though compressed. The reason for this is the size of the system: the UK at its own income cuts and France at the U.S.\ percentiles have nearly the same dispersion of relative incomes, $\tilde{V} = 0.223$ and $0.218$, about half the U.S.\ value, and what separates their chosen designs is the tax that funds them, which is the relation between the size of the system and its design that figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata} shows. Calibrating the UK's own cost, so that its electorate re-elects $0.560$, gives $\lambda = 7.264$ at $\rho = 1$, within $16\%$ of the U.S.\ value, and $17\%$ below it at $\rho = 0.5$; the agreement breaks at $\rho = 2$, where the UK's own cost, which falls less steeply in the elasticity than the U.S.\ one, lies well above it (appendix \ref{app:US:ukESC}). Regrouping the UK's households at the U.S.\ income percentiles, which lowers its $\tilde{V}$ to $0.065$, moves the choice under the U.S.\ parameter only to $0.644$, so the result does not turn on how the income groups are cut. France has no own cost: its observed design is the corner, and under a cost quadratic in the redistribution performed the first unit of redistribution is free at the margin, so no electorate chooses exactly $\theta = 1$.\footnote{A linear term in $1-\theta$ would restore that possibility at the price of a second parameter, which the UK's design could pin with France as the prediction; we leave it for future work.}

\subsection{Ageing, inequality and voting patterns}\label{sec:esc:results}
By construction the design in force in 2020 is $\theta^{\ast}$ at every $\rho$. Along the projected demographic path the chosen design then drifts toward earnings-related benefits as ageing proceeds, tracking the projected fall in $\nu_t$ and flattening when it does, while the tax rate rises a little above the path with the design held at $\theta^{\ast}$, since a more Bismarckian system dissipates less and is worth more to the electorate at the margin.\footnote{The design channel runs through hours, so the Frisch elasticity is the parameter to vary. Recalibrating $(\beta,\omega)$ and the cost at $\xi = 0.2$ and $0.4$ moves $\lambda$ by about one percent from its value at $\xi = 0.3$, to $8.736$ and $8.547$, and leaves the design in force in 2110 and the design chosen under acute ageing unchanged to the third decimal; only the level of the tax moves, by about $0.3$ p.p.\ in either direction by 2110.}

Each counterfactual below is a separate equilibrium path read at 2020, with the changed characteristic holding over the whole horizon and the political choice binding from the first period, so a row describes a country that has always had this mix of characteristics rather than the U.S.\ surprised in 2020. Tables \ref{table:US_ESC:ageing} to \ref{table:US_ESC:frenchAll} in appendices \ref{app:US:escTables} and \ref{app:US:ukESC} report each counterfactual with the design pinned at the U.S.\ value and with the design chosen, at $\rho \in \{0.5, 1, 2\}$; both readings carry the cost, so the distance between them is the political response.\footnote{We report the chosen design under the common-$X$ calibration of section \ref{sec:oecd} only; the design and the cost enter the political objective through relative incomes alone, which both calibrations match to the same data, so only the income-distribution rows would read differently against the vector-$X_i$ calibration of appendix \ref{app:US:vectorX}.}

Figure \ref{fig:US_ESC:overview} collects the whole set: an open marker is the reading with the design pinned, a filled marker the reading with the design chosen, and the length of the connector is what endogenizing the design adds. At $\rho \leq 1$ the design response moves hours by at most $0.1$ hours a week in every counterfactual, so the hours effect of a counterfactual is its exogenous one; at $\rho = 2$ the design moves far enough under French voting patterns to cost about half an hour a week, $38.6$ against $39.1$ hours with the design pinned (table \ref{table:US_ESC:voting}).

\smalltitle{Ageing.} Acute ageing, $\nu_t = 1$ throughout, raises the chosen design from $0.738$ to $0.825$ at $\rho = 1$, and to $0.816$ and $0.826$ at $\rho = 0.5$ and $2$ (table \ref{table:US_ESC:ageing}), so the response varies little with the elasticity, unlike the calibrated cost. Two mechanisms add up: a scarcer young generation raises the political weight of retirees, who are unanimous for a more contributive system in \eqref{eq:esc:leadFOC}, and the tax that ageing calls for makes the flat component costlier at the margin, since the loss in \eqref{eq:esc:budget} is proportional to $\tau_t$. Under acute ageing the tax rises from $14.4\%$ to $22.8\%$, so the loss per unit of redistribution rises by more than half, and this second channel is what makes the response first order. The tax and savings responses are essentially those of the exogenous-$\theta$ reading: at $\rho = 1$ the chosen design adds $0.3$ p.p.\ to a tax response of $8.1$ p.p., and between $0.1$ and $0.3$ p.p.\ across $\rho$, since a more Bismarckian system dissipates less and is worth a larger tax to the electorate. The pinned response is a little below the $8.7$ p.p.\ of section \ref{sec:oecd} (table \ref{table:US:ageing}), because with the cost in place a larger system is worth less to retirees at the margin.

\smalltitle{Income distribution.} The model's inequality is the dispersion of relative labor income across the three income groups at the chosen cuts, the $\tilde{V}$ of \eqref{eq:esc:budget}, a different object from the Gini coefficient of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}. Holding $\theta$ at the U.S.\ value, France's flatter income distribution lowers the equilibrium tax at $\rho = 1$ from $14.4\%$ to $13.8\%$ (table \ref{table:US_ESC:incomeDistr}), less than the $1.2$ p.p.\ of section \ref{sec:oecd}, because a compressed distribution also lowers $\tilde{V}$ and with it the share of revenue the flat component dissipates. When the electorate chooses the design it moves to $0.772$ at $\rho = 1$, and to $0.707$ and $0.918$ at $\rho = 0.5$ and $2$: interior at every elasticity and rising in it. This happens because the cost is proportional to the same dispersion of relative incomes as the redistribution a flat benefit performs, so compressing the distribution lowers the redistributive stakes and the cost of carrying them together; what remains is a small net effect whose sign depends on the elasticity, since the young's forward-looking stake in earnings-related benefits does not scale with the dispersion of incomes and gains ground when the redistributive stakes shrink. The design response leaves the tax where the pinned reading puts it at $\rho \leq 1$ and adds $0.2$ p.p.\ at $\rho = 2$, and savings move by less than $0.2$ p.p.\ of GDP. The model therefore does not tie the design tightly to inequality, in line with the weak relation between inequality and the Bismarckian index across countries in figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}.

\smalltitle{Voting patterns.} France's much flatter voting-weight profile pushes the design toward the Beveridgean corner, and this is the experiment whose strength depends most on the elasticity: the chosen design falls from $0.738$ to $0.653$ at $\rho = 1$, and to $0.687$ and $0.335$ at $\rho = 0.5$ and $2$ (table \ref{table:US_ESC:voting}). When the poor carry more relative voting weight, the redistributive force of \eqref{eq:esc:seqFOC} regains ground against the forward-looking stake; at $\rho = 1$ the response is of the same size as that of acute ageing, with the opposite sign. That the design falls furthest at the highest elasticity follows from the calibration: the cost that re-elects the observed design amounts to only $0.4\%$ of revenue at $\rho = 2$ against $3.9\%$ at $\rho = 0.5$ (table \ref{table:US_ESC:calibration}), so at a high elasticity the interior choice rests on a thin cushion and a stronger redistributive pull moves it a long way. In the exogenous reading a flatter voting profile makes the system larger, by $0.8$ p.p.\ at $\rho = 1$; the design response leaves that tax within $0.1$ p.p.\ at $\rho \leq 1$, because the less Bismarckian design chosen also dissipates more revenue, and lifts it to $16.7\%$ against $16.1\%$ at $\rho = 2$, where the design moves a long way and the cost is thin.

\smalltitle{French characteristics.} When all of France's household characteristics are imposed together, the voting profile prevails at every elasticity: the chosen design is $0.627$ at $\rho = 1$, and $0.667$ and $0.262$ at $\rho = 0.5$ and $2$, with the tax at $14.4\%$ in both readings at $\rho = 1$ (table \ref{table:US_ESC:frenchAll}); France's level of $X_i$ contributes nothing to the design or the tax, since a common rescaling of $X_i$ moves hours and nothing else (section \ref{sec:oecd}). What the characteristics do not deliver is France's own corner, for the reason section \ref{sec:esc:calibration} gives. The same exercise with the UK as the host economy, at the UK's own cost parameter, moves its design toward flat benefits under France's voting profile as well, by less, and leaves its tax rate within $0.1$ p.p.\ of the pinned reading in every row (appendix \ref{app:US:ukESC}), so on the UK the design response is confined to the design itself. The two forces that figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata} motivates thus come out as the data do: ageing moves the chosen design a long way, and inequality moves it little.
