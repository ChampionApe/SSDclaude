\section{Further results on the endogenous design}\label{app:escResults}\setcounter{equation}{0}
This appendix collects results on the endogenous design of section \ref{sec:esc} that the main text only points to: the calibration of the cost for the UK and the test of the U.S.\ calibration on the UK and France, and the path of the design along the projected U.S.\ demography.

\subsection{Calibration}\label{app:escResults:calibration}
Table \ref{table:UK_ESC:calibration} calibrates the cost on the UK as table \ref{table:US_ESC:calibration} does on the U.S.: $\lambda$ is the value at which the UK electorate re-elects its observed design, $\theta^{\ast} = 0.560$, with households grouped at the UK's own income cuts as in section \ref{sec:oecd}. The UK's parameter lies below the U.S.\ one at low elasticities, by $17\%$ at $\rho = 0.5$ and $16\%$ at $\rho = 1$, and above it at $\rho = 2$, by $61\%$. The cost weighs less in the UK. At $\rho = 1$ its observed design loses $1.8\%$ of revenue, as the U.S.\ one does, but a flat benefit of the same size would lose $9.2\%$, against $23.8\%$ in the U.S., because the UK's system is smaller, with a tax rate of $11.9\%$ against $14.4\%$, and its dispersion of relative incomes is about half the U.S.\ one, $0.223$ against $0.436$. As in the U.S., the cost raises the calibrated political weight of the old, from $1.16$ to $1.21$ at $\rho = 1$.

Table \ref{table:US_ESC:country} asks what the U.S.\ parameter implies for the other two economies at $\rho = 1$. Each economy keeps its own calibration of section \ref{sec:oecd} and takes $\lambda = 8.643$ from the U.S. The UK's electorate then chooses $0.625$, above its observed $0.560$, and France's chooses $0.749$, below its observed $1.000$, so the three designs are ordered as observed, with the U.S.\ at $0.738$ between them, although France chooses only $0.011$ more than the U.S. No finite value of the parameter makes France re-elect its own design, since the cost does not price the first unit of redistribution (section \ref{sec:esc:cost}). The UK and France have nearly the same dispersion of relative incomes, $0.223$ and $0.218$, the latter with France grouped at the U.S.\ income percentiles, while France's tax rate is almost twice the UK's, $21.3\%$ against $11.9\%$. Under one parameter, each unit of redistribution therefore costs France nearly twice as much as the UK, in line with France's more earnings-related choice. The last row of the table regroups the UK's households at the U.S.\ income percentiles, by a linear fit of the UK's group means rather than a recount of the survey (appendix \ref{app:US}). The regrouping reads the UK's design as $0.543$ and its dispersion of relative incomes as $0.065$; under the U.S.\ parameter the regrouped UK chooses $0.644$, and its own parameter is $6.519$.

\subsection{The design along the baseline path}\label{app:escResults:path}
Figure \ref{fig:US_ESC:path} follows the U.S.\ along its projected demography at $\rho = 1$, with the design chosen and with the design pinned at $\theta^{\ast}$. Since the calibration targets only the design of 2020, the path from 2020 on is a prediction. Along it the chosen design goes from $0.738$ in 2020 to $0.797$ in 2050 and stays at $0.809$ from 2110 on. It tracks the number of workers per retiree, which falls from $1.34$ to $1.07$, and settles one period after the demography does. The tax rate climbs from $14.4\%$ to $20.9\%$, $0.2$ p.p.\ above the path with the design pinned, since a more earnings-related system loses less of its revenue.\footnote{The path barely depends on the Frisch elasticity. Recalibrated at $\xi = 0.2$ and $0.4$, $\lambda$ changes by about one percent and the tax rate in 2110 by about $0.3$ p.p., while the design in 2110 and the design under acute ageing are the same to the third decimal (\oa{esc-path}).}
