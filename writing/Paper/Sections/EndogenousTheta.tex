\section{Endogenous Pension Design}\label{sec:esc}
Throughout the paper so far the Bismarckian index $\theta$ is calibrated and held fixed while taxes are chosen politically. This section endogenizes the relation between contributions and benefits. The design is chosen by the same probabilistic-voting objective $\mathcal{W}_t$ that sets $\tau_t$, in the model without informal households. We explore four variants, three related to the \emph{timing} of the choice (made contemporaneously, one period in advance, or once and for all), and a \emph{deadweight cost} of rasing funds for redistributive transfers. We find that with costless redistribution, all three timings make the design choice to be fully Beveridgean, i.e.\ $\theta = 0$, at observed US inequality. An interior choice requires redistribution through the pension system to be costly. Our preferred specification is therefore the choice \emph{one period in advance with a proportional deadweight cost}, calibrated so that the 2020 electorate re-elects the system it has. This section states the four variants, the argument for the preferred one, and the results; derivations, algorithms and the full verification battery are in the online technical documentation published alongside the paper.\footnote{\textcite{BergGE26doc}, section \textit{Endogenous system characteristics}. It contains the model definitions and propositions for each timing, the numerical algorithms (including the exact two-dimensional policy-function recursion under CRRA and the checks behind every structural claim used here), and the calibration protocol.}

\subsection{Three timings, one corner}
\smalltitle{Contemporaneous.} If $\theta_t$ is chosen at $t$ together with $\tau_t$, it only re-splits benefits already granted. Current labor supply depends on the \emph{next} period's choice, $\theta_{t+1}$, so there is no strategic channel, and the marginal effect on the political objective runs entirely through current retirees,
\begin{align}\label{eq:esc:seqFOC}
   \dfrac{\partial \mathcal{W}_t}{\partial \theta_t} =\omega \sum_i\gamma_i\frac{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\tau_t\left(\frac{\eta_{i}^{1+\xi}}{X_{i}^{\xi}}\frac{1}{\Gamma_{h}}-1\right)}{\frac{s_{t-1}^i}{s_{t-1}}+\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\tau_t\left(1+\theta_t\left[\frac{\eta_{i}^{1+\xi}}{X_{i}^{\xi}}\frac{1}{\Gamma_{h}}-1\right]\right)}\;\leq\;0 \quad\text{for } \theta_t\in[0,1].
\end{align}
The numerator averages to zero across types while the denominator is larger for high-income retirees, so lowering $\theta_t$ redistributes from retirees with low marginal utility to retirees with high marginal utility and the choice is in the corner of $\theta = 0$.

\smalltitle{Advance.} If instead $\theta_{t+1}$ is chosen at $t$, the design becomes a state variable and acquires genuine forward-looking content as it moves current labor supply and savings both directly (the economic-equilibrium effect) and through the anticipated response of $\tau_{t+1}$ (the politico-economic effect), and it enters the young's indirect utility through $\tilde{c}_{1,t}^i$ and $R_{t+1}$. The equilibrium is a sequence of policy functions over the inherited design, identified by backward induction as in section \ref{sec:numerical}. The redistributive force of \eqref{eq:esc:seqFOC} nonetheless still dominates such that at observed US inequality the choice is at the corner of $\theta = 0$ for reasonable labor supply elasticities.

\smalltitle{Permanent.} If a design chosen at $t_0$ is expected to hold forever, it enters all channels at once --- the sequential re-splitting at $t_0$, the forward-looking channel through $h_{t_0}$, and the whole continuation path. Two structural results make this relative simple to solve: the joint $(\tau_{t_0},\theta)$ choice \emph{concentrates} (the tax first-order condition at any candidate $\theta$ is the ordinary one, so the choice is a one-dimensional search), and because the vote is anticipated, the predetermined savings distribution is a fixed point as households save at $t_0-1$ against the design that will be implemented. Both results are established and verified in the technical documentation. The outcome is again the corner $\theta = 0$.\footnote{We note that the model with permanent $\theta$ choice can only be calibrated to the observed 2020 characteristics under counterfactually compressed inequality.}

The common lesson is that in this class of models, costless within-cohort redistribution is politically too attractive: some friction must make Beveridgean pensions more expensive than Bismarckian ones for an interior design to survive.

\subsection{Costly redistribution, and the preferred specification}
We introduce a deadweight cost of raising redistributive public funds: such that the government budget becomes
\begin{align}\label{eq:esc:budget}
    \nu_t w_t\tau_th_t\, f(\theta_t) = \sum_i \gamma_i b_t^i, \qquad f(\theta) = \phi + (1-\phi)\,\theta^{p}, \quad 0<p, \quad 0<\phi<1
\end{align}
so a fraction of revenue is lost when $\theta<1$, and this loss is higher the more Beveridgean the system ($f(1)=1$, $f(0)=\phi$). We place the wedge to be proportional to tax revenue, so that $f$ scales all benefits.\footnote{The alternative makes the deadweight cost to be associated only to the flat component of the benefit formula. It delivers the same endpoints and differs only in the interior, at the cost of identification, as $f$ no longer cancels from the replacement-rate ratio, so $\theta^{\ast}$ and $p$ become jointly identified rather than separately.} The economic equilibrium carries through with $f$ inserted into the equilibrium functions ($\Gamma_{s}$, $\Theta_{h}$, relative savings); the first-order condition features the terms $f'(\theta)$ and $\theta f'(\theta) + f(\theta)$ that let it cross zero in the interior. The explicit expressions are in the technical documentation.

Our preferred specification combines the choice one period in advance with the proportional cost, for three reasons. First, by construction the contemporaneous choice involves only redistribution (and its costs), and the permanent choice requires commitment expectations that are difficult to reconcile with the paper’s focus on Markov equilibria. The choice one period in advance keeps the Markov structure, and it is the only timing in which the design chosen affects current labor supply and savings before it binds, which is the channel through which $\theta$ moves taxes and savings in section \ref{sec:oecd}. Second, identification is clean under the proportional cost: $f$ cancels from the replacement-rate ratio that identifies $\theta$ from the OECD data, so the data targets is the same, $\theta^{\ast} = 0.738$, and the cost parameter is identified separately, by requiring that the political choice at the calibration year reproduces the observed one,
\begin{align}\label{eq:esc:calibration}
    \Theta_{t_0}\!\left(\theta^{\ast}\right) = \theta^{\ast},
\end{align}
with $(\beta,\omega)$ recalibrated at every trial value of $p$ and $\phi$ imposed.\footnote{The imposed $\phi$ matters mainly through the calibrated $p$: at $\rho = 1$, $\phi = 0.25/0.5/0.75$ give $p = 0.26/0.40/0.94$.} Third, the calibration pins the choice at the calibration year only. The design chosen at every later date is a prediction of the model, and how it drifts as population growth slows along the projected path is reported in the results below.

Table \ref{table:US_ESC:calibration} reports the calibrated cost across the paper's range of intertemporal elasticities. The wedge required for the electorate to re-elect the observed $\theta$ falls steeply in $\rho$. For $\rho = 0.5$ the deadweight cost amounts to roughly 13\% of raised funds and for $\rho = 2$ this falls to only about 1.5\%. This is because a higher elasticity strengthens the forward-looking (savings and labor supply) channels in a Bismarckian system, so less friction is needed to hold the choice away from the Beveridgean corner. We view this, together with how $\rho$ affected savings after pensions become universal in Argentina, as further evidence that calibrations with $\rho \in [1,2]$ are more empirically plausible.

%\smalltitle{Solution.} 
For $\rho = 1$ the equilibrium with the design chosen one period in advance is solved exactly. Under log preferences the equilibrium tax at any date, given the designs in force, is a scalar problem that depends neither on past savings nor on future taxes, as in section \ref{sec:numerical}, and the design chosen at $t$ is provably independent of both the savings state and the design inherited. Each date's choice is therefore a one-dimensional search over the design grid, with no state to carry. Under CRRA neither property survives: the exact solution is a backward recursion over policy functions on the two-dimensional state $(s_{t-1},\theta_t)$. We compute that recursion and use it to check a cheaper method that iterates on the equilibrium path instead: at each date the design for the next period is chosen by re-solving the equilibrium at every candidate, holding later designs at their current values, and the sweep is repeated until the path converges. The two methods agree to $\pm 0.01$ in the chosen design and to $2\times10^{-4}$ in the tax rate. The objective is flat near its maximum, which limits what any method can resolve, and the tables below use the path iteration. Details and the verification battery are in the technical documentation.

\subsection{Results}
By construction the electorate re-elects $\theta^{\ast}$ in 2020 at every $\rho$. Along the baseline demographic path the chosen design then drifts Bismarckian as ageing proceeds: at $\rho = 1$ from $0.738$ in 2020 to $0.748$ by 2050 and $0.773$ from 2110 on, tracking the projected fall in $\nu_t$ and flattening when it does. Each counterfactual below is a separate equilibrium \emph{path}: the changed characteristic holds over the whole horizon, the economy starts from its own steady state, and the political choice binds from the first period. A row therefore describes a country that has always had this mix of characteristics rather than the U.S.\ surprised in 2020 --- which is what makes it commensurable with France's own calibrated path, the comparison the exercise is for. Because the choice binds from the first period, the design in force in 2020 is itself an equilibrium outcome rather than an inherited datum, and every row is read at 2020. Each table compares the shocked economy with the design \emph{pinned} at the observed U.S.\ value (exogenous $\theta$) and with the design \emph{chosen} (endogenous $\theta$), at $\rho \in \{0.5, 1, 2\}$. Both readings therefore start from the same design, $\theta = 0.738$, and the distance between them is the political response to the changed characteristic and nothing else.

Figure \ref{fig:US_ESC:overview} collects the whole set. For every counterfactual and every $\rho$, an open marker is the reading with the design pinned at the U.S.\ value and a filled marker the reading with the design chosen, joined by a connector, so the length of each connector is what endogenizing the design adds. The first panel shows the design itself as a level: every pinned marker sits at $0.738$ by construction, and the connector is the political response. The tax and savings panels are deviations from the baseline, and a connector that collapses to a single dot --- ageing in the tax panel, leisure everywhere --- is a counterfactual whose design response is nil.

\smalltitle{Ageing.} Acute ageing ($\nu_t = 1$ throughout) raises the chosen design at every elasticity, and monotonically in $\rho$: from $0.738$ to $0.766$, $0.778$ and $0.846$ at $\rho = 0.5, 1, 2$ (Table \ref{table:US_ESC:ageing}). The direction is intuitive --- a scarcer young generation makes the deadweight cost of the Beveridgean component harder to carry --- and its strength in $\rho$ follows the calibrated cost itself, since a higher elasticity strengthens the young's forward-looking stake in a Bismarckian design. Quantitatively the design response is a side channel: taxes and savings move essentially as in the exogenous-$\theta$ reading, the endogenous design moving the tax by at most $0.13$ p.p.\ on a response of eight to ten. It is not even signed across the range --- the chosen design shaves the tax response at $\rho \leq 1$ and adds $0.05$ p.p.\ to it at $\rho = 2$. Part of the fiscal adjustment does happen through the system's shape rather than its size, but only a small part, and how it does is not robust to the elasticity.

\smalltitle{Income distribution.} This is the counterfactual the endogenous design changes most. Holding $\theta$ at the U.S.\ value, France's flatter income distribution lowers the equilibrium tax --- by $1.7$ p.p.\ at $\rho = 0.5$, falling to $0.8$ p.p.\ at $\rho = 2$ --- and raises savings. Let the electorate choose instead, and it goes to the fully Bismarckian corner $\theta = 1$ at \emph{every} $\rho$ (Table \ref{table:US_ESC:incomeDistr}): under a compressed distribution there is little left for a flat benefit to redistribute, and the deadweight cost of trying is unchanged.

Two things follow, and both cut against the exogenous reading. The tax is pulled back \emph{up} toward baseline --- at $\rho = 1$, $13.8\%$ against $13.2\%$ pinned, and at $\rho = 2$ all the way back to $14.4\%$, the baseline itself. With the deadweight cost in place equilibrium taxes are no longer decreasing in $\theta$; under the French distribution they increase in it, so a more Bismarckian design and a larger system arrive together. And the savings-rate gain is not merely reduced but erased: at $\rho = 1$ the pinned reading puts savings about $0.5$ p.p.\ of GDP above the baseline's $15.4\%$, while the chosen design puts them $0.1$ p.p.\ \emph{below} it. What reads as a substantial saving response to lower inequality under a fixed design is, on this model, a pension-design effect in disguise.

The model therefore ties the design to inequality far more tightly than the cross-country data do --- it puts every flatter-income economy at the corner --- which is the tension the introduction's cross-section poses.

\smalltitle{Leisure preferences.} French leisure preferences are a pure rescaling of $X_i$, which by the model's scale invariance moves hours and nothing else. Consistently, the chosen design does not move at any $\rho$ --- it reproduces the baseline's own chosen design to within $6\times10^{-4}$ --- and the endogenous rows differ from the exogenous ones only in hours (Table \ref{table:US_ESC:leisure}). This is the exercise's placebo: the machinery does not manufacture design responses where the theory says there are none, and the residual is a bound on what the method can resolve rather than an effect.

\input{Tables/US_ESC_Leisure}

\smalltitle{Voting patterns.} France's much flatter voting-weight profile pushes the design Beveridgean, and this is the experiment whose strength depends most sharply on the elasticity: the chosen design falls from $0.738$ to $0.662$ at $\rho = 0.5$, $0.533$ at $\rho = 1$, and $0.285$ at $\rho = 2$ (Table \ref{table:US_ESC:voting}). When the poor carry more relative voting weight the redistributive force of \eqref{eq:esc:seqFOC} regains ground against the forward-looking stake --- and it is the latter that scales with $\rho$. Taxes end above the pinned reading, by 0.3 p.p.\ at $\rho = 0.5$ and 1.3 p.p.\ at $\rho = 2$, in the same direction the mechanism implies: here the design moves \emph{down}, and a less Bismarckian system is a larger one.

\smalltitle{Both at once.} Imposing France's income distribution and voting profile together --- the pair the cross-country comparison actually confronts --- the two forces pull the design in opposite directions, and which wins is an elasticity question. The chosen design corners at $\theta = 1$ at $\rho = 0.5$, sits just below it at $0.972$ at $\rho = 1$, and at $\rho = 2$ is dragged well below the U.S.\ value to $0.508$. Adding French leisure preferences changes none of this, as the scale invariance requires: the all-three rows carry the same design and tax as the pair and differ only in hours.

So the model does not deliver a sign for what ``French characteristics'' do to the Bismarckian index. Between $\rho = 1$ and $\rho = 2$ the answer moves from ``almost fully Bismarckian'' to ``markedly less Bismarckian than the U.S.'', on a preference parameter the cross-section cannot pin and our calibration does not identify. That is a negative result worth stating plainly: the cross-sectional relation between inequality and pension design that figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata} declines to show is one this model can reproduce or reverse depending on the intertemporal elasticity, and we see no way to choose between them from the data used here.
