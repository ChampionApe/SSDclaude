\subsection{Robust policy search with bounds}\label{\refsec:RobustRoot}
The politico-economic equilibria below are identified from first order conditions rather than by maximising political objectives directly. This keeps the state space small, but a root problem inherits none of the safeguards of the maximisation it came from: a root may be a minimum, there may be several, and the optimum may sit at a corner where no root exists. This subsection collects the machinery that restores those safeguards, stated for a generic first order condition and implemented as a standalone package (\texttt{python/gridsearch}).

Throughout, let $F(\tau)$ denote a first order condition (in our applications the derivative of a political objective $\mathcal{W}$ with respect to the tax rate) that it is safe to evaluate on an interval $[l,u]$ with $0\leq l < u < 1$, and let $z(\tau) = F\left([\tau]_{[l,u]}\right)$ denote its bounded evaluation, where $[\tau]_{[l,u]} \equiv \min(u, \max(l,\tau))$.

\smalltitle{A bounded-root reparameterization.}
Gradient-based solvers are efficient when they apply, but they search all of $\mathbb{R}$ and $F$ is only meaningful on $[l,u]$. Instead of solving $F(\tau) = 0$ subject to bounds, we solve the unconstrained auxiliary problem
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:root}
    \psi(z,\tau) = z - |z| \underbrace{\left[a_0\min\left(\tau - l,0\right) + a_1\max\left(\tau - u, 0\right)\right]}_{\equiv g(\tau)} = 0,
\end{align}
where $a_0, a_1>0$ are technical parameters. This gives three regions:
\begin{align*}
    \psi\left(z,\tau\right) = \left\lbrace \begin{array}{ll} F(l)-|F(l)| a_0 (\tau - l), & \tau<l \\
    F(\tau), & \tau\in[l,u] \\
    F(u)-|F(u)|a_1(\tau-u), & \tau>u\end{array}\right., && \dfrac{\partial \psi}{\partial \tau} = \left\lbrace \begin{array}{ll} -|F(l)|a_0, & \tau<l \\
    \dfrac{\partial F}{\partial \tau}, & \tau\in[l,u] \\
    -|F(u)|a_1, & \tau>u\end{array}\right.
\end{align*}
$F$ is never evaluated outside $[l,u]$, yet $\psi$ is defined everywhere (affine with a weakly negative slope outside the interval), so an unconstrained solver can search freely. If $F$ identifies an interior optimum, solving $\psi\left(z(\tilde{\tau}),\tilde{\tau}\right) = 0$ and reporting the bounded solution $\tau = [\tilde{\tau}]_{[l,u]}$ recovers it.

The construction also detects corners. Suppose $F(\tau)>0$ throughout $[l,u]$ with $\partial F/\partial\tau <0$: a gradient-based algorithm started in the interior finds no root there, but for $\tau\geq u$ the auxiliary problem still has one, at $\tilde{\tau} = u+1/a_1$, which bounds to the corner solution $\tau = u$. Symmetrically, $F(\tau)<0$ throughout with $\partial F/\partial \tau>0$ yields $\tilde{\tau} = l-1/a_0$ and thus $\tau = l$.

\smalltitle{The grid.}
When $F$ is not known to be well behaved, and a political first order condition generally is not, we evaluate it on a grid rather than trusting a solver's path through it. Let $\mathcal{G}_{\tau} = \left\lbrace \tau^{(1)},...,\tau^{(M)}\right\rbrace$ with $\tau^{(1)} = l$ and $\tau^{(M)} = u$ denote a grid on the interval. A root finder applied to $\psi$ on a grid would need two outer nodes at a hair beyond the boundary solutions of \eqref{\refeq:root},
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:extendedGrid}
    \tilde{\tau}^{(0)} = l-\frac{1}{a_0}-\delta, \qquad \tilde{\tau}^{(M+1)} = u+\frac{1}{a_1}+\delta,
\end{align}
where a corner appears as an exact zero of $\psi$ (the package's root detection treats exact zeros as roots for that reason). The selection rule below makes them unnecessary: it puts both endpoints among the candidates directly, so the solvers of this model evaluate $F$ on $\mathcal{G}_{\tau}$ itself.

\smalltitle{Selecting among several roots.}
Solving $z = 0$ is only a necessary condition for the political optimum: an upward crossing of $z$ is a local \emph{minimum} of $\mathcal{W}$, several downward crossings may exist, and the optimum may sit at a corner where no root exists. The selection has to respect one more thing. In every application below $z$ is evaluated with a predetermined state substituted consistently with the point of evaluation (the savings shares $s_{t-1,i}/s_{t-1}$ at the tax that savers anticipated), so that, writing $\mathcal{W}(\tau; D)$ for the objective at a frozen state $D$ and $D(\tau)$ for the consistent one,
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:consistentFOC}
    z(\tau) = \partial_{\tau}\mathcal{W}\left(\tau; D\right)\big|_{D = D(\tau)}.
\end{align}
A zero of $z$ is a stationary point of $\mathcal{W}(\cdot\,; D(\tau^{\star}))$, which is the object wanted, so locating it from the interpolant of $z$ is legitimate. But $z$ is not the derivative of any one function of $\tau$. Its slope at a crossing, $z' = \mathcal{W}_{\tau\tau} + \mathcal{W}_{\tau D}D'$, is not the second-order condition, and its integral between two crossings, $\int z\,d\tau = \Delta\left[\mathcal{W}(\tau;D(\tau))\right] - \int \mathcal{W}_D D'\,d\tau$, is neither the objective at a frozen state nor the consistent one, so neither may be used to classify or rank candidates.

\emph{Candidates.} With $\hat{z}$ the piecewise-linear interpolant of $z$ through the nodes of $\mathcal{G}_{\tau}$, the candidate set is
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:candidates}
    \mathcal{K} \equiv \left\lbrace l,u \right\rbrace\cup\left\lbrace \tau\in(l,u)\mbox{ }:\mbox{ }\hat{z}(\tau) = 0\right\rbrace,
\end{align}
every crossing in either direction together with the two endpoints. Each candidate $c$ comes with its own predetermined state $D_c \equiv D(\tau_c)$, the one savers would have chosen had they anticipated it.

\emph{The objective at a frozen state.} For each candidate the first order condition is re-evaluated on the whole grid with the state frozen at $D_c$, and integrated by the trapezoidal rule through the nodes,
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:objectiveProfile}
    \mathcal{V}_c(\tau) \equiv \mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{old}}\left(l; D_c\right) + \int_l^{\tau}\partial_{x}\mathcal{W}\left(x; D_c\right)dx, \qquad \tau\in\mathcal{G}_{\tau},
\end{align}
where $\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{old}}$ is the retirees' term, a closed form. Then $\mathcal{V}_c(\tau) = \mathcal{W}(\tau; D_c)$ up to the young's term at $l$, which does not involve the state, so values compare across candidates as the objectives do. The integral is legitimate here, since nothing moves along it but the tax, and where the condition carries numerically smoothed derivatives (\cref{\refsec:PEE}) $\mathcal{V}_c$ carries the same ones, whereas the raw utility levels would reintroduce the kinks the smoothing removed. Nothing expensive is recomputed: the young's terms and every numerical derivative are already on the grid, and the retirees' term is a closed form in $(\tau, D_c)$ given hours, with $D_c$ formed from the grid values interpolated linearly at $\tau_c$.

\emph{The equilibrium test.} A candidate is an \emph{equilibrium} at the state if it is the global maximiser of its own frozen objective,
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:equilibriumTest}
    \mathcal{V}_c(\tau_c) \geq \max_{\tau\in\mathcal{G}_{\tau}}\mathcal{V}_c(\tau),
\end{align}
with $\mathcal{V}_c(\tau_c)$ at an interior candidate read from the quadratic through the three nodes nearest it, up to a relative tolerance of $10^{-9}$, and at a corner equal to its node value. Two forms at the grid's resolution supplement the reading for an interior candidate, and neither applies to a corner: the candidate passes if its nearest node attains the maximum of $\mathcal{V}_c$ (a quadratic read a hair off a node can fall below that node's exact value by more than the tolerance), and, where its stencil touches an infeasible cell and only a linear interpolant is available, if the maximising node lies within one cell of it. Candidates that pass and lie within one cell of each other are one equilibrium at the grid's resolution, represented by the interior crossing, which is located at sub-grid precision and moves continuously with the data. This is the definition: the electorate, taking the state as given, prefers $\tau_c$ to every other tax, and the state is the one consistent with $\tau_c$. An upward crossing fails at its own state, and a corner passes exactly when the objective at the corner's own state is maximised at the corner, which subsumes the boundary check.

\emph{Selection, and what it assumes.} The equilibria at a state need not be unique, and the multiplicity is intrinsic: even when $\mathcal{W}(\cdot\,;D)$ is strictly concave for every $D$, the map $\tau\mapsto\arg\max\mathcal{W}(\cdot\,;D(\tau))$ can have several fixed points, because the shares savers choose depend on the tax they anticipate. When more than one candidate passes \eqref{\refeq:equilibriumTest} we select the one with the highest value of the political objective in its own equilibrium, $\max_c \mathcal{V}_c(\tau_c)$. This selection is part of the definition of the equilibrium computed: the continuation policy that date-$t$ savers and the date-$t$ electorate take as given is built from the selected policy, so the backward induction assumes at every state and date that households expect the selected equilibrium, and the solution is unique only given the rule. The solvers therefore report, for every state and period, the number of candidates in \eqref{\refeq:candidates} and the number that pass \eqref{\refeq:equilibriumTest}; the latter never exceeding one means the rule never bound, and a selection that switched between well-separated equilibria across neighbouring states would put a jump into the policy function that the calibration differentiates through. Should no candidate pass, which the grid can produce at a tangential crossing, the state is flagged and the integral criterion $\arg\max_{\mathcal{K}}\int_l^{\tau}\hat{z}$ is returned for it, so that a recursion is not stopped by one state. When a feasibility mask excludes part of $\mathcal{G}_{\tau}$ (\cref{\refsec:PEE}), everything above is applied to the feasible sub-grid, with $l$ and $u$ replaced by its endpoints.

\smalltitle{Root problems that are not maximisations.}
The state fixed point of \cref{\refsec:PEE} is a self-consistency condition in which \emph{any} crossing is a solution, so neither the equilibrium test nor the selection is applied there. The design choice of \cref{\refsec:ESC} is not run through this machinery either: its objective is maximised directly, because whether that choice is interior is itself the question, but every candidate design is evaluated at one frozen predetermined state, for the reason the equilibrium test gives.

\smalltitle{Two implementation conventions.}
Convergence of any gradient-polished solution is judged on $\max\left|z\right|$ against an explicit tolerance, not on the solver's own success flag. And a residual is a convergence measure only at an \emph{interior} optimum: at a corner $z\neq 0$ is the correct answer, so residual diagnostics are restricted to periods (or states) whose selected policy is interior.
