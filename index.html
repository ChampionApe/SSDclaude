About this appendix

This online appendix accompanies Social Security Design and Its Political Support by Rasmus K. S. Berg and Martín Gonzalez-Eiras. It holds the tables and figures behind the paper’s quantitative sections, the robustness checks that the paper’s appendix G summarizes in one figure, and the numerical checks behind its results, with each figure set beside the tables that print its numbers. Every exhibit is built by the paper’s own pipeline from the model’s results, so the numbers are the paper’s to the last printed digit; replication lists where each one comes from.

Reading an exhibit. Point at a marker of a figure to read its value; click it to bring up the table that prints it, with its row highlighted. Point at a table row to find its markers in the figure. Where an exhibit exists under both calibrations of the households’ taste for leisure, a switch moves between the paper’s calibration, common \(X\), and the alternative, vector \(X_i\) (OA.3.2), and mark the cells that differ shows which numbers the choice moves. The address in the browser keeps the selection, so a link opens the same table and row. Under Source, each exhibit lists the files it reads, with downloads, the builder that makes it and the command that rebuilds it. A grey badge marks an exhibit of the paper itself; every other exhibit is numbered here, Table or Figure OA.k.

The print edition, a PDF under the download link of the sidebar, holds every table and figure that is not in the paper, with the same numbers.

Robustness at a glance

Figure G.1 of the paper, repeated here, sets its quantitative results against every specification the paper reports. Each panel is one result: how the tax rate responds to the Bismarckian index \(\theta\), to acute ageing and to France’s income distribution and voting patterns with the design given, and how the chosen design responds to the last three. Each marker is the same result under one specification: the intertemporal elasticity of substitution (IES) \(\rho\), the calibration of the taste for leisure, the host economy, and for the chosen design the cost specification and the Frisch elasticity \(\xi\). Results are changes against the host’s own baseline at the same IES, and the paper’s own reading, the U.S. under a common \(X\) at an IES of one with the cost of section 7, is the highlighted marker. Click a marker for the table that prints it.

\(\cdot\)

Figure G.1 of the paper: a marker across the zero line from the highlighted one reverses the paper’s sign. With the design given, the Beveridgean corner against the Bismarckian one, acute ageing and France’s voting patterns raise the tax rate in every specification; France’s income distribution lowers it on the U.S., and on the UK raises it under a common \(X\) and lowers it under the vector \(X_i\). With the design chosen, acute ageing makes the design more Bismarckian and France’s voting patterns less so throughout, while France’s income distribution moves it in a direction that changes with the IES and the host, and to the Bismarckian corner under the cost on the design.
