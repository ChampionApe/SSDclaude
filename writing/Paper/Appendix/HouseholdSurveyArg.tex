\subsection{The household survey}

The Encuesta Permanente de Hogares (EPH) of the Instituto Nacional de Estad\'istica y Censos (INDEC) is Argentina's main household survey of employment, hours and income, and the basis of its official labor market statistics. It is a continuous quarterly survey of the urban agglomerates, 31 metropolitan areas at present, in which households are drawn by a stratified multi-stage design. We use the last two quarters of 2006, a phase of sustained expansion after the recovery from the 2001--2002 crisis, at the start of the expansion of coverage under the amnesties (figure \ref{fig:Arg}) and before the Great Recession. The survey asks respondents whether they contribute to social security, which separates formal from informal workers, and we keep those aged 25 to 60.

\subsection{The pension system before and after the reform}

Before the tax amnesties, retirees over the age of 70 who had made contributions for a minimum of 10 years were eligible for basic pension coverage. This coverage consisted of 70\% of the minimum pension and a proportional amount from other contributions.\footnote{The basic pensions was set by law 24347 of year 1995 at 70\% of the first pillar, denoted ``prestaci\'on b\'asica universal'', plus the corresponding fully-funded or pay-as-you-go benefit. For simplicity we assume the latter on average was also 70\% of the benefits received by workers that had at least 30 years of contributions. Thus, basic pensions were 70\% of the benefits received by the least productive formal workers.} We calculated the pre-reform $\epsilon$ \begin{align*}
        \epsilon = 0.7 \left(1-\theta+\theta\frac{\eta^1h_t^1}{h_t}\right)\dfrac{\beta^{5/30} \frac{\mathrm{LE_{men}-5}}{\mathrm{LE_{men}}}+\beta^{10/30} \frac{\mathrm{LE_{women}-10}}{\mathrm{LE_{women}}}}{2},
    \end{align*}
where $\mathrm{LE_{men}}=14.45$ and $\mathrm{LE_{women}}=22.55$ are the expected life expectancy for men and women, respectively, from their retirement ages (women retire at age 60, men at 65), such that the formula above corrects for the basic pension being received 5 or 10 years after the formal retirement age.\footnote{To estimate life expectancy at retirement age for Argentina we conduct a regression analysis using data for men and women's life expectancies at birth and at age 65 for 40 OECD countries for the year 2000. We then use the fitted regression and data from the World Bank on male and female life expectancy at birth in Argentina in 2000.} We set $\gamma_0$ to the share of the population over 65 without a pension before the reform, 32\% in 2004 \parencite{Roffman}. To identify the Bismarckian incentives, we use OECD data on the ratio of replacement rates, $RR$, at half mean and mean wages for males and females.\footnote{In a pure Bismarckian system the replacement rate would be the same at all wage levels, such that $RR=1$, while in a pure Beveridgean it would double if we halve wages, implying $RR=0.5$. Note that we combine men and women since we do not have data separately on the increase in coverage or government spending by gender.} Specifically, we adjust $\theta$ to ensure that
    \begin{align*}
        RR \equiv \dfrac{\theta+(1-\theta)\frac{h_t}{\eta_t^2h_t^2}}{\theta+(1-\theta)\frac{h_t}{\eta_t^1h_t^1}}= \dfrac{0.678}{0.803},
    \end{align*}
where the superscripts 1 and 2 denote the quartiles whose mean incomes are closest to half the formal average and to the average, the first and the third, at $0.47$ and $1.00$ of the average in the survey.

\subsection{Technology, demographics, and the aggregate targets}

The capital income share is that of the formal sector. In the model, informal households produce outside $Y_t$ and $K_t$, and national accounts book the output of unincorporated household enterprises, where most informal work sits, as mixed income, which a share computed as one minus the wage bill over GDP counts entirely as capital \parencite{Gollina1}. The economy-wide share for Argentina is 0.43 \parencite{Frankema}. If a share $y$ of GDP is informal mixed income, the formal-sector share is $(0.43-y)/(1-y)$. Informal earnings of about 5\% of GDP, as in the household survey, give 0.40, while shadow-economy estimates of 20--25\% of GDP give 0.24 to 0.29. We take $\alpha = 0.35$, which also puts Argentina on the same footing as the Gollin-adjusted shares we use for the rich economies in section \ref{sec:oecd}. We set the Frisch elasticity of labor supply to $\xi = 0.30$. To calibrate $\beta$, we target the capital-output ratio. A model period is thirty years and capital depreciates fully between periods, so the capital stock in place at $t$ is the previous generation's savings, $K_t = s_{t-1}/\nu_t$, and with Cobb--Douglas production $K_t/Y_t = \alpha/R_t$ --- a stock relative to one period, i.e.\ thirty years, of output. Thirty times that ratio is the capital-output ratio as the national accounts report it. In our model with $K_t/Y_t = \alpha / R_t$ this is essentially equivalent to targeting the thirty year interest rate, which for Argentina is not credibly measured. We target $K/Y = 3.23$, Argentina's ratio in 2010.\footnote{Capital stock and GDP at constant national prices (\texttt{rnna}, \texttt{rgdpna}) from the Penn World Table 11.0 \parencite{pwt}, retrieved through FRED. We read it at the calibration year, where the tax rate, the replacement-rate ratio, the coverage share and the household survey are all measured, rather than averaging. Correcting for the shadow economy would raise the capital-output target, since the Penn World Table stock is economy-wide and the informal sector is labor-intensive. We keep the measured ratio, because no source measures an informal capital share and the stock also includes housing, which cuts the other way.} We calibrate $\beta$ and $\epsilon$ numerically, since $\beta$ depends on the politico-economic equilibrium path for savings and labor supply and $\epsilon$ depends on $\beta$.

To construct the time series for $\nu_t$ we follow \textcite{mkvss08} and use 30-year gross population growth rate and projections from census data. Finally, we use social security spending before the 2001 crisis of 7.1\% of GDP,\footnote{Total public pension spending, national and provincial schemes, 7.0\% and 7.2\% of GDP in 2000 and 2001 \parencite[table 13]{CetrangoloG}. We use pre-crisis spending as it was relatively stable during that time, and after 2010 contributive pensions returned to their pre-crisis levels. Following the 2001 crisis, pension spending was reduced, to 5.2\% of GDP in 2005, by only allowing minimum pensions to adjust for inflation.} to calibrate $\omega$. Since the payroll tax falls on formal labor income, which is a share $1-\alpha$ of formal output, spending of 7.1\% of GDP corresponds to an equivalent social security tax rate of $\tau_{2010} = 0.071/(1-\alpha) = 0.109$.

\subsection{Productivity and hours}

We use $\eta_j$ and $X_j$ to match the income distribution across formal households and the income and working hours of informal households. Specifically, let $z_j^{\eta}$ and $z_j^{x}$ denote income and hours worked of group $j$ relative to the population-weighted average over the formal households, so that $\sum_{i>0}\gamma_iz_i^{\eta} = \sum_{i>0}\gamma_iz_i^{x} = 1$; informal households are measured against the same formal average. With labor supply $h_t^i/h_t = (\eta_i/X_i)^{\xi}/\sum_{j>0}\gamma_j\eta_j^{1+\xi}/X_j^{\xi}$ for formal households (cf.\ proposition \ref{prop:LOG:EE}) and $h_t^0 = (\eta_0w_t^0/X_0)^{\xi}$ for informal households, where $w_t^0$ is the informal wage, we solve the system
\begin{align}\label{eq:calibration_eta}
        z_i^{\eta} &= \dfrac{w_t(1-\tau_t)h_t^i \eta_i}{w_t(1-\tau_t)h_t} = \dfrac{\eta_i^{1+\xi}/X_i^{\xi}}{\sum_{j>0} \gamma_ j\eta_j^{1+\xi}/X_j^{\xi}}, \qquad i>0,\\
        z_i^{x} &= \frac{h_t^i}{\sum_{j>0}\gamma_jh_t^j} = \frac{(\eta_i/X_i)^{\xi}}{\sum_{j>0}\gamma_j(\eta_j/X_j)^{\xi}}, \qquad i>0, \nonumber\\
         z_0^{\eta} &= \dfrac{w_t^0 h_t^0 \eta_0}{w_t(1-\tau_t)h_t}, \qquad z_0^{x} = \frac{h_t^0}{\sum_{j>0}\gamma_jh_t^j}. \nonumber
    \end{align}
Because only relative income and hours are observed, both the level of efficiency units and the unit in which hours are measured are free. We spend the first on the normalization $\sum_{i>0}\gamma_i\eta_i^{1+\xi}/X_i^{\xi} = 1$, and take the taste for leisure to be common across the formal groups, $X_i = X$ for $i>0$. The formal income conditions then invert in closed form, $\eta_i = \left(z_i^{\eta}\right)^{1/(1+\xi)}X^{\xi/(1+\xi)}$, and they satisfy the normalization at any $X$, so the level of $X$ is what fixes the unit in which hours are counted. We pin it down by requiring average formal hours in the baseline year, $\sum_{i>0}\gamma_ih_t^i$, to equal the average workweek of formal workers in the survey, 42.5 hours expressed as a share of discretionary time. Relative hours across the formal quartiles are then a prediction rather than a target, rising with relative income at elasticity $\xi/(1+\xi)$, and section \ref{sec:argentina} compares the prediction with the survey. The aggregate $h_t$ weights hours by productivity and is not average formal hours; the two differ by the factor $\sum_{i>0}\gamma_i(\eta_i/X_i)^{\xi}$, which the informal hours condition in \eqref{eq:calibration_eta} carries. That condition and the informal income condition pin down $\eta_0$ and $X_0$ given the equilibrium $h_t$ in the baseline year, and we solve for them jointly with $\beta$ and $\omega$ at $X = 1$. The level of $X$ then follows in one step from the solved hours, and $\eta_0$ and $X_0$ are re-derived at it. That last step leaves the equilibrium untouched, since the informal household reaches every aggregate through $\eta_0^{1+\xi}/X_0^{\xi}$, which the unit of hours does not move.

\subsection{The vector-\texorpdfstring{$X_i$}{Xi} calibration}\label{app:EPH:vectorX}
The alternative to a common taste for leisure treats the survey's relative hours as data at every point of the formal distribution: with both $z_i^{\eta}$ and $z_i^{x}$ imposed, \eqref{eq:calibration_eta} identifies one $X_i$ per quartile, and the level of hours is then not identified. Since the productivity distribution reaches every aggregate only through $\sum_{i>0}\gamma_i\eta_i^{1+\xi}/X_i^{\xi}$, which both calibrations normalize to one, $\beta$, $\omega$ and every equilibrium outcome of section \ref{sec:argentina}, the reform at every $\rho$ and its dependence on $\epsilon$ and $\theta$ included, are identical under the two; only the productivity and leisure parameters and the reading of relative hours differ. That calibration, and the reform at every $\rho$ under both, are reported in the \oa{arg-calibration}.
