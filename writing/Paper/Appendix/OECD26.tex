\section{The OECD cross-section}\label{app:oecd26}\setcounter{equation}{0}
This appendix documents the cross-country tests of sections \ref{sec:oecd} and \ref{sec:esc:crossSection}: the sample and the data, the U.S.\ at those data, where the cost parameter is calibrated, each country's political weight of the old, and the test under other population data and other measures of the design. Every economy is solved under log preferences, without the cost in section \ref{sec:oecd} and with it in section \ref{sec:esc:crossSection}, where the cost parameter of country $n$ is $\lambda_{n,t} = \varepsilon\,\Pi_{n,t}/R_{n,t}$ at each date. Exactly one candidate tax passes at every state and date of every solve (appendix \ref{app:T}). The \oa{esc-oecd26} shows each variant in full, and the test without Luxembourg and with every country in the model at the U.S.\ political weight.

\subsection{Sample and data}\label{app:oecd26:data}
The sample is the 21 countries of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}: the OECD members before 2000 in which informal employment is below a quarter of employment and one public pay-as-you-go system pays the bulk of retirement benefits. The first condition leaves out Korea, Mexico and Turkey.\footnote{Informal employment is the share of informal employment in total employment, indicator 8.3.1 of the Sustainable Development Goals, from ILOSTAT, in the latest year available, 2018 to 2020. It is $56\%$ in Mexico, $31\%$ in Turkey and $29\%$ in Korea. The next are the UK and the Czech Republic, at $20\%$ and $17\%$, where the measure mostly reflects self-employment that their pension systems cover. Canada, Japan, New Zealand and the U.S.\ have no such measure and stay in. Korea and Turkey also stand apart in their pension systems: Korea's public pension dates only from 1988, and Turkey's retirement age for men is 52.} The second holds where funded tiers pay less than a quarter of mandatory benefits in 2020 and the public pension is not tested against them, and leaves out Australia, Denmark, Iceland, the Netherlands and Switzerland. Funded pensions are private saving in the model, so where they are large, or where the public pension falls as they rise, the public benefit formula alone does not describe how retirement income depends on earnings.

The design is read off the benefit formula, in the same way for every country except the UK. We fit a least-squares line $a + c\,z$ through the gross public pension of a man with a full career at earnings $z$ of $0.5$, $1$ and $2$ times average earnings, under the rules of 2020 \parencite[table 4.2]{OECDpen}, and take the line's earnings-linked share at average earnings, $c/(a+c)$.\footnote{For New Zealand the lowest earnings level is $0.6$ times average earnings, as in the source. The formula is that of a worker who enters the labor market in 2020, and it describes the pensions paid in 2020 unless a reform is phasing in. The exception is the UK, where most retirees of 2020 draw the basic State Pension and the earnings-related State Second Pension, which the flat new State Pension replaced from 2016. Its $\theta$ is the State Second Pension's share of spending on the State Pensions and Pension Credit in 2019--20.} In the model this share is $\theta$: a retiree's benefit is proportional to $\theta y_i + 1-\theta$ (section \ref{sec:esc:cost}), a line in relative earnings whose earnings-linked share at mean earnings is $\theta$, with the cost of redistribution or without it (appendix \ref{app:esc:equilibrium}). A full-career worker at half of average earnings never draws the minimum top-ups and tested benefits that lie below his own pension, although many retirees do, and that spending is flat. We therefore multiply the share by $1-s$, where $s$ is the share of public old-age and survivors cash spending in 2020 that goes to the top-ups and tested benefits this worker does not reach.\footnote{The spending is that booked inside public old-age and survivors cash in the OECD Social Expenditure Database, taken from national administrative data and from the programme lines of the database; the technical documentation gives the source of each country's figure \parencite{BergGE26doc}. Italy's and Portugal's figures include the top-ups of invalidity pensions, and Belgium's leave out its minimum-pension supplement. In Norway and Poland the worker at half of average earnings reaches the minimum pension, so the formula already contains it and $s$ is zero; for Luxembourg no figure is available and $s$ is set to zero. Across the 21 countries $s$ is at most $0.082$, in Portugal.} The measure puts the U.S.\ at $0.544$, France at $0.822$ and the UK at $0.181$. Ireland and New Zealand, whose public pensions are flat, are at about $0$, and no country is at $1$. The Bismarckian index of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata} is $I = 1/(2-\theta)$ of this measure.

The other inputs are measured in the same way for every country, the U.S.\ included. The number of workers per retiree $\nu_t$ is the thirty-year growth of the total population in the medium variant of the United Nations' World Population Prospects 2024, from 1990 to 2080, with 1960 at the value of 1990 and the periods after 2080 at the value of 2080, as in the calibrations of section \ref{sec:oecd}, corrected for each country's gap to the U.S.\ in the normal retirement age of men as France's is in appendix \ref{app:US}.\footnote{The gap is that of men retiring in 2020 for 1990 and 2020, and that of men entering the labor market in 2020 from 2050 on \parencite[figure 3.7]{OECDpen}. A country whose normal age is $g$ years below the U.S.\ one has $\nu_t(30-g)/(30+\nu_t g)$, the rule of appendix \ref{app:US}, and one whose age is $g$ years above it has the mirror image, $(30\nu_t+g)/(30-g)$, which counts the years its older cohort works beyond the U.S.\ age. The U.S.\ ratio is unchanged, and France's is that of appendix \ref{app:US}.} Pension spending is that of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}, public cash spending on old-age and survivors pensions in 2020, and the tax rate is spending over the labor share. The income distribution is that of the gross earnings of men in full-time employment in the OECD earnings database, in 2019 or the nearest year, through a lognormal fitted separately below and above the median to the ratios of the first and ninth deciles to the median. Households are grouped at the U.S.\ income percentiles of section \ref{sec:oecd}, so the three groups hold $58\%$, $18\%$ and $24\%$ of households in every country. The propensities to vote are each country's own, from the Comparative Study of Electoral Systems (CSES): turnout in a national election by quintile of household income, averaged over the percentiles of each income group.\footnote{The election is the one nearest 2020, within 2015 to 2022, with at least 30 respondents in each quintile. For Spain and Portugal, where no election qualifies, the elections are those of 2008 and 2009.} Luxembourg has no CSES survey; since it enforces compulsory voting, its three groups vote at the same rate. Everything else takes the U.S.\ values of section \ref{sec:oecd}: $\alpha = \xi = 0.30$, a taste for leisure common to all groups, which under log preferences sets hours but no rate, and the discount factor of the calibration without the cost, $\beta = 0.65$, imposed as on France in appendix \ref{app:US}. Table \ref{table:OECD26:omega} lists the inputs that differ across countries, except the propensities to vote.

\subsection{The U.S.\ at these data}\label{app:oecd26:anchor}
At these inputs the U.S.\ keeps the design of section \ref{sec:esc}, $\theta = 0.544$, since the measure is read off the benefit formula, and differs from its calibration of section \ref{sec:oecd} in two respects. Its dispersion of relative incomes is $0.333$, against $0.436$ at the CPS groups: the OECD series covers men in full-time work only and is fitted to three points of the distribution, so it misses the part-time and part-year earners that the CPS records. And its propensities to vote are those of the CSES, which rise less steeply with income than those of the CPS (table \ref{table:a_US:CalibUS}). With $\beta$ imposed, we calibrate $\omega$ to the U.S.\ tax rate and $\varepsilon$ so that the electorate of 1990 chooses the observed design for 2020, as in section \ref{sec:esc:calibration}, which gives $\omega = 1.26$ and $\varepsilon = 13.09$. At these values the cost takes $2.6\%$ of the revenue of 2020 at the observed design and would take $12.0\%$ at a flat benefit, a little less than at $\rho = 1$ in table \ref{table:US_ESC:calibration}. In the model every country takes these values of $\beta$ and $\varepsilon$ and, in place of the U.S.\ political weight, $\omega = 1.20$, the mean of the weights that match each country's pension spending at its observed design (appendix \ref{app:oecd26:layer2}). Without the cost, the political weight that reproduces U.S.\ spending at these data is $1.19$, against $1.17$ in table \ref{table:US:CalibUSFR}, and the test of spending of section \ref{sec:oecd} gives every country the mean of the weights that reproduce each country's spending without the cost, $1.17$ (figure \ref{fig:OECD26:spending}).

\subsection{Each country's political weight}\label{app:oecd26:layer2}
The model with each country's own political weight, own $\omega$ for short, keeps the U.S.\ $\beta$ and $\varepsilon$ but calibrates each country's political weight of the old so that the country's tax rate of 2020 at its observed design matches its pension spending, as $\omega$ is for the U.S.\ in section \ref{sec:oecd}. Each electorate then chooses its design as in section \ref{sec:esc:crossSection}, and spending is read on the path of that design, so it matches the data only where the predicted design is the observed one. Table \ref{table:OECD26:omega} reports the political weights and the predicted designs. The weight of the old runs from $0.86$ in Hungary to $1.46$ in Austria, against $1.26$ in the U.S.; its mean over the 21 countries, $1.20$, is the weight every country takes in the model. The predicted designs run from $0$ in Ireland, whose observed design is also at the Beveridgean corner, to $0.711$ in Italy, a narrower range than the observed one, which reaches $0.997$ in Hungary. For 17 of the 20 countries other than the U.S.\ the predicted design lies on the same side of the U.S.\ design as the observed one. The exceptions are Belgium, predicted above it at $0.605$, and Hungary and Luxembourg, predicted below it at $0.522$ and $0.473$. The UK is predicted at $0.503$ and France at $0.626$. Calibrated in full from their own household data and given the U.S.\ cost parameter, the two choose designs close to these, $0.493$ and $0.645$ (\oa{esc-countries}). The predicted designs keep the relations of table \ref{table:OECD26:slopes} with population growth, spending and the Gini (the starred rows of panel A of table \ref{table:OECD26:variants}).

\subsection{Other population data and other measures of the design}\label{app:oecd26:readings}
Table \ref{table:OECD26:variants} sets the paper's test beside its variants, and figures \ref{fig:OECD26:thetaFitPopulation} and \ref{fig:OECD26:thetaFitMeasure} the predicted designs. Panel A changes the population data: workers per retiree are left uncorrected for the retirement age, or corrected for each country's gap to the U.S.\ in the effective age of labor-market exit in place of the normal age \parencite[figure 6.14]{OECDpen}. With own $\omega$ the test hardly moves, since the political weights are calibrated to spending: no slope changes by more than $0.05$, and the correlation between predicted and observed designs stays at $0.77$. The model keeps the signs of the three relations that are significant in the data, and its slopes on the Gini stay insignificant, but its predicted designs move more. At the exit age the correlation is $0.68$, against $0.72$ at the normal age. Uncorrected, it falls to $0.36$, because of Luxembourg. Its population grew to $1.65$ times its 1990 size by 2020, which at the mean political weight supports a tax of only $5.3\%$ at its observed design, about two fifths of the $12.9\%$ its spending implies, and its electorate of 1990 chooses a design of $0.144$ for 2020, against the observed $0.820$. Luxembourg's normal retirement age is four years below the U.S.\ one, and the correction brings its workers per retiree in 2020 to $1.17$ and its predicted design to $0.461$. Without Luxembourg, on the 20 countries with a CSES survey, the correlation in the model is $0.74$ with $\nu$ uncorrected, between the $0.69$ of the exit age and the $0.76$ of the normal age on the same countries (the last rows of panel A and the \oa{esc-oecd26-luxembourg}).

Panel B changes the measure of the design. Each measure is its own cross-section, the data and the model calibrated to it, at the paper's population data. The public tier on 23 countries extends the paper's measure, the public tier, to every country whose public pension is not tested against funded tiers, which adds the Netherlands and Switzerland. All mandatory tiers takes the slope over all mandatory and quasi-mandatory tiers, public and funded \parencite[table 4.1]{OECDpen}, with the same correction for top-ups, adds the spending of the funded tiers to the tax and covers all 26 countries in which informal employment is below a quarter of employment. The public tier on 23 countries leaves the test as it is, with correlations between predicted and observed designs of $0.69$ in the model and $0.75$ with own $\omega$. All mandatory tiers keeps the signs of the significant slopes. Its data add both the funded tiers and the five countries where they are large or the public pension is tested against them, and in them the slope of the index on population growth is $-0.38$, against $-0.44$, while its slope on the Gini nearly vanishes, $0.26$ against $0.88$. The model predicts the design less well, with a correlation of $0.37$, partly because of Iceland, which only this measure covers and which the model puts at $0.168$ against an observed $0.813$; without Iceland the correlation is $0.54$. With own $\omega$, where Iceland's political weight is calibrated to its spending, its design is $0.490$ and the correlation is $0.68$.
