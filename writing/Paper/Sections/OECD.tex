\section{Social Security in Rich OECD Countries}\label{sec:oecd}\label{sec:quant:OECD}

The model developed in this paper is well suited to rich OECD economies. First, given current labor-market conditions and institutional design, we can abstract from informality and focus on a single policy dimension that shapes the politico-economic equilibrium: the extent to which benefits are earnings-related.\footnote{\textcite{WhitmanRa1} estimated that only around 4\% of individuals aged 62--84 in 2010 in the U.S.\ would never collect Social Security. And for the vast majority of these cases the reason would be lacking the 40 quarters/10 years of covered earnings needed to qualify.} Second, the framework can be used to study the implications of emerging work arrangements with low job security, such as platform work, zero-hour contracts, and certain forms of self-employment. Although these jobs currently represent a small share of total employment, their incidence may rise, particularly given current challenges from automation and artificial intelligence. A central concern is that fragmented and insecure careers could leave a non-negligible fraction of workers unable to meet contributory eligibility requirements by retirement. Our model can be used to quantify the consequences of such a shift and to evaluate how introducing some form of universal coverage would alter the equilibrium.

We take the first of these applications and compare three rich economies that differ markedly on their level of pension spending, demographics, income inequality, and pension characteristics: the U.S., France, and the UK. We show that to a large extent our model can fit all three countries. More interestingly, we then explore the main determinants of the politico-economic equilibrium taxes and find that ageing is the most significant with pension characteristics being the second most important factor. Inequality in income or in voting patterns are of minor importance.\footnote{In the US, the poor tend to vote much less than the rich, in contrast with the other two countries.}


\smalltitle{Calibration.}
To calibrate the model with no informal households, we use data for men between ages 25 and 60. The only parameter for pensions is $\theta$, and to calibrate it, we proceed as for Argentina and use OECD's replacement rates at mean income and half-mean income. For all three countries, we use three income groups. The first two are chosen such that their income corresponds to half-mean and mean, respectively, and the remainder of the population grouped in a third high income group. Given that France has a fully Bismarckian system with $\theta=1$, this distinction is not necessary, and we can group individuals using income percentiles from the other two countries.\footnote{This feature is particularly useful when we want to explore the counterfactual politico-economic equilibrium in the U.S.\ if it had the level of income inequality of France.}

Two parameters describe each income group's productivity and taste for leisure, $\eta_i$ and $X_i$, and \eqref{eq:calibration_eta} identifies them only up to the unit in which hours are measured: aggregate hours carry no scale of their own, so relative income pins down the combination $\eta_i^{1+\xi}/X_i^{\xi}$ and something further is needed to separate the two. We take $X_i = X$ common across income groups and use the observed average workweek to fix its level. Relative hours across income groups are then a \emph{prediction} of the model rather than an input, and the average workweek is the one hours statistic the calibration is asked to match. The alternative is to use relative hours as data and identify a full vector $X_i$, at the cost that the level of average hours is no longer identified and only its ratio to the baseline means anything; we report every table and figure below under that variant as well, in appendix \ref{app:US:vectorX}. The two calibrations share $\beta$, $\omega$, the equilibrium tax rate and the savings rate exactly, so nothing in the paper's main argument turns on the choice. What differs is anything defined \emph{through} $\eta$ or $X$, which is to say the counterfactuals that borrow France's income distribution or its leisure preferences.

To calibrate income, productivity, and hours worked we use the CPS March Supplement 2019 for the U.S., the Luxembourg Income Study for France, and the Harmonised BHPS Wave 17 for the UK. For the first two countries the data corresponds to the year before the pandemic for which data collection was not compromised by lockdown measures. For the UK, the data is for year 2006-7.\footnote{There were two more waves of these survey before being discontinued but we use Wave 17 as later surveys were collected, at least partially, during the Great Recession.}

To calibrate $\beta$ for the U.S., we use the \textcite{mkvss08} estimate of the gross interest rate net of the average growth of multifactor productivity. For France and UK we impose the same discount factor as calibrated for the U.S. Data on spending as a share of GDP corresponds to year 2020 and comes from \textcite{OECDpen}. Since our model is one of intergenerational transfers, not of overall government spending, we add Medicare spending to pensions for the U.S.\footnote{We do the same for the calibration of $\theta$ described above. We assume Medicare benefits are not correlated with income, thus they tend to make overall transfers more Beveridgean than pure pensions.} While it is true that France and UK's governments are in charge of health expenditures for their retired populations, in these two countries they also cover health spending {\it before} retirement. We impose the same technology on all three countries and take $\alpha=0.30$.\footnote{Averaging the adjustment 2 and adjustment 3 estimates in \textcite{Gollina1} gives capital income shares of close to 0.3 for France and the U.S., with the UK having a slightly lower value.}

Population growth rates come from census data and population projections. For France we correct for their lower retirement age.\footnote{Since in 2020 the retirement age in France was 63.5 years instead of 66 as in the U.S.\ or UK we take the rate of growth of population in 2020 to be \[
\frac{\mathrm{Population} \ 2020 *(30-2.5)/30}{\mathrm{Population} \ 1990 + \mathrm{Population} \ 2020 *2.5/30}.
\]
We do a similar adjustments for later years taking into account that the gap between retirement ages is expected to shrink from 2.5 to 2 years.} Finally, since it is well known that in the U.S.\ the poor have much lower voting participation rates than the well off, we allow for different weights in the political objective functions based on this. We use data from the U.S.\ Census  Voting and Registration in the Election of November
2020. For France and UK we use data from the Comparative Study of Electoral Systems.\footnote{Given that for these two countries voting patterns are more equal across income groups, we could have abstracted from this feature.}

Table \ref{table:US:Calib} presents the main calibration parameters for the three countries. It shows large differences in population structure with the U.S.\ having the highest rate of population growth and France the lowest. The highest income inequality observed in the U.S.\ is reflected in a ratio of high to low productivity about a third higher than in France. Conversely, the average preference for leisure is roughly three quarters higher in France, and twice as high in the UK, as in the U.S. Despite these differences, and the fact that pensions systems differ across countries, the calibrated political weight of the old is basically the same in the U.S.\ and France, being a bit lower in the UK.

\smalltitle{Determinants of pensions, savings, and labor supply.}
We now turn to a number of counterfactual analyses to study how different characteristics affect pension spending, savings, and labor supply. Specifically, we consider the U.S. model and change its pensions characteristics, population growth, income inequality, and voting patterns. In all of these exercises we will be guided by what we observe in France and UK, particularly the former as we can construct the same groupings of workers, and becaused the calibrated $\omega$ is very similar to that of the U.S. Throughout, the savings rate is savings relative to GDP, and each table reports it as the change against the baseline in percentage points of GDP. We start with the degree with which pension benefits are linked to earnings and consider the two polar cases of $\theta=0,1$ as counterfactuals.

The results reported in table \ref{table:US:pensChars} show that going from a fully Bismarckian system to a fully Beveridgean one would increase the politico-economic tax rate by about 6.2 p.p. The same change would reduce the savings rate by about 1.6 p.p.\ of GDP. Holding taxes fixed, an increase in $\theta$ lowers savings, but the endogenous fall in taxes more than offsets this and the net effect is positive. For labor supply, the economic equilibrium and political effects of increasing $\theta$ are aligned, and the tax response contributes about as much as the direct effect.

\input{Tables/US_PensChars}

In a second counterfactual, we study the effects of population ageing. Previous studies of the politico-economic equilibrium of pension spending have highlighted the effect of ageing. For example, \textcite{mkvss08} predicts an increase in spending in the United States towards the middle of this century of around 3.6 p.p.\ because of the slowdown in population growth.\footnote{\textcite{ssvsde15} extends predictions on the effect of ageing on social security transfers to other rich OECD countries and shows that economic growth would be compromised if these countries could not manage to increase the retirement age when facing increases in longevity.}
In our data, including population projections not reported here, ageing is more acute in France than in UK, and significantly more than the U.S. For simplicity we consider two scenarios. In the first one, {\it mild ageing}, we simply halve current and future projected population growth rates for the U.S. In the second, {\it acute ageing}, we impose a constant population, i.e.\ $\nu_t=1$ for all $t$.

Table \ref{table:US:ageing} shows the effects of imposing these demographic scenarios that roughly correspond to data for our two European countries. Acute ageing would increase the tax rate by 8.7 p.p.\ and mild ageing would increase it by 4 p.p. Interestingly, this mild ageing scenario almost corresponds to the mentioned projections in \textcite{mkvss08}.\footnote{In \textcite{mkvss08} $\nu_{2000}=1.38$ and projected $\nu_{2080}=1.23$. Thus, a reduction of about 15 p.p., similar to the reduction of 17 p.p.\ in the mild ageing scenario analyzed in this paper.} With log-GHH preferences, the only effect of ageing on savings is through the endogenous response of the tax rate; in the acute ageing scenario this results in a drop of about 2 p.p.\ of GDP in savings. The effects on labor supply are mild with hours worked decreasing by at most 0.7 hours. In this case, the direct effect of ageing in economic equilibrium is a capital deepening effect that increases wages and labor supply; as taxes increase, however, the full effect is a modest drop.

Table \ref{table:US:otherShocks} shows the effect of imposing other characteristics from the French model in the U.S. model: income distribution and voting patterns.\footnote{For these counterfactuals we do not distinguish the direct from the full effects as they are not informative, working in the same direction and being quantitatively minor.} Each is a change in that characteristic alone. In particular, the income-distribution counterfactual holds $\theta$ at its U.S.\ value, so that the pension system's design is not changed alongside the income distribution.\footnote{Our identification reads $\theta$ off the ratio of replacement rates at mean and half-mean income, so under France's flatter income distribution the same observed ratio would imply a considerably less Bismarckian system. Re-estimating $\theta$ would bundle a change in $\theta$ into a counterfactual about inequality; and pension characteristics is analyzed in table \ref{table:US:pensChars}.} % Doing so would raise the equilibrium tax rate from 13.2\% to 13.8\%, since a less Bismarckian system raises taxes for the reason table \ref{table:US:pensChars} makes explicit. The bundled version of this counterfactual would thus report about 0.6 p.p.\ of a pension-design effect as if it were an effect of inequality.}
For both counterfactuals, the effect on taxes and savings is limited. Taxes increase by a bit less than one p.p.\ if the poor voted as often as they do in France, and would decrease by a bit more than one p.p.\ if income inequality was as low as in France. For the labor supply, the effects of income inequality and voting patterns are likewise minor. The last two rows put the borrowed characteristics in perspective: imposing all of France's household characteristics at once --- its income distribution, voting weights and level of leisure preferences --- takes the U.S.\ essentially all the way to France's workweek but leaves the tax rate well short of France's own; the rest is France's demography, its fully Bismarckian design and the political weight of its old.

\smalltitle{The effect of the intertemporal elasticity of substitution.}
We next consider the effect of general CRRA preferences on the counterfactuals above. As in section \ref{sec:argentina}, we read $\rho$ as the intertemporal elasticity of substitution, since the environment features no uncertainty, and we continue to rely on the estimates obtained under logarithmic utility as our benchmark. This choice is further supported by the fact that most of our counterfactual exercises exhibit only limited sensitivity to variations in the IES. The exception is that we find significant differences regarding the relation between earnings and benefits, see table \ref{table:US:CRRA:pensChars}. If $\rho>1$ the effect of changes in this parameter is dampened, while the opposite happens if $\rho<1$.

\input{Tables/US_CRRA_PensChars}

Recall that the mechanism through which a reduction in $\theta$ raises equilibrium taxes operates by providing greater benefits to the poorer segment of the elderly. This analysis, however, takes the level of resistance to taxation by the current young as given. But, an increase in the elasticity of intertemporal substitution {\it reduces} the young's resistance to taxation. Thus, to explain the initial level of taxes the model requires a {\it lower} relative power of the old, $\omega$. Consequently, when evaluating different values of $\theta$, the difference in benefits between rich and poor elderly receives less weight. This explains the dampened response observed in table \ref{table:US:CRRA:pensChars} when $\rho>1$.

As expected, the response of the savings rate and labor supply follow that of taxation, i.e.\ we get less sensitivity to $\theta$ for $\rho>1$ and more sensitivity for $\rho<1$. We report the result for the other counterfactuals in tables \ref{table:US:CRRA:ageing} and \ref{table:US:CRRA:otherShocks} in the appendix, which also repeats every table and figure of this section under the vector-$X_i$ calibration discussed above. The effects of ageing are largely unaffected by $\rho$, consistent with findings in \textcite{mkvss08}. For income distribution the changes remain several times smaller than for ageing. The main change is when the poor are more likely to vote in which case, when $\rho>1$, the tax rate increases twice as much as in the baseline. The reason for this is that when we increase the voting weight of the poor we do so for both the poor young and old. And when $\rho>1$ the latter's preference for higher taxes finds less resistance from the former than when $\rho=1$.

%\smalltitle{Interpretation.}
It is important to emphasize that the model should be understood as a stylized representation that synthesizes, rather than reproducing real-world features in detail through the calibration of certain key parameters. For instance, the parameter capturing the relative political weight of older generations is meant to absorb a wide set of institutional realities, including the mechanisms by which legislation is introduced, debated, and passed, as well as broader constitutional arrangements such as whether the political system is presidential or parliamentary. In a similar way, the parameter reflecting the preference for leisure is not only an abstract measure of individual utility from non-work time, but also serves as a proxy for institutional factors that affect labor supply decisions. These include, for example, the degree of unionization, the prevalence of collective bargaining, the scope of labor market regulations, or statutory holidays. Thus, we should interpret the large differences in calibrated leisure preferences as including these other institutional differences across our three countries.

Our study suggests the underlying drivers of taxes and intergenerational transfers must first be examined. Ageing could then explain the increase in taxes in Europe but, according to our model, this would fail to explain the decrease in hours worked unless the labor supply elasticity is much larger than we calibrate it to be.\footnote{This is the key assumption underlying the results in \textcite{Prescotta1}. Note that with GHH preferences, the effect of changes in after tax labor income on labor supply is generally higher than with separable preferences.}

We now return to figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata} that shows the tight relation between pension spending and how Bismarckian systems are with population ageing, while showing no clear relation between these two variables and income inequality. Our model shows that, consistent with previous related work, ageing is a prime determinant of social security taxes. It also shows that the link between income inequality and taxes is several times weaker.\footnote{We note that the U.S.\ has the highest Gini coefficient in our sample, 0.59, with France, at around 0.43, being below the mean.} The relation between $\theta$ and population growth suggests that, as countries age and face pressures to increase taxes, they might respond by making their pension benefits more tightly linked to earnings; according to our model an increase in $\theta$ dampens the effects of ageing. Section \ref{sec:esc} takes up that question by letting the electorate choose $\theta$ as well as $\tau$.
