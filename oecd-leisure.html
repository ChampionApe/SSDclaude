B4. Identification of X

As for Argentina (A3), the paper takes the taste for leisure \(X\) common to the income groups and pins its level by each country’s average workweek; the alternative identifies one \(X_i\) per group from the observed relative hours. In every country the two calibrations share the discount factor, the political weight of the old, the tax rate, savings and aggregate hours. They also share every counterfactual that changes neither the productivities nor the taste for leisure: the design, ageing and France’s voting patterns. The tables and the figure show only what differs.

The calibration

Two rows of the paper’s calibration table differ, the taste for leisure and the productivity spread. Under the vector \(X_i\) the taste for leisure rises with income in all three countries: observed hours rise less with income than the common \(X\) predicts, the high group working fewer hours than predicted. To reproduce the same relative incomes with those hours, the productivity spread widens.

Group by group, the vector \(X_i\) are a fraction of the common \(X\), and their level carries no information of its own. Under the common \(X\) the level is the unit of hours: it is set so that the model’s average workweek is the observed one, relative to the U.S. for the UK and France, and the productivities scale with it. Under the vector \(X_i\) the relative hours fix only the ratios of the \(X_i\), and the unit is a normalization. That is why the level differs so much between the two while every aggregate is the same.

The counterfactuals that differ

France’s income distribution is imposed through the productivities and its leisure preferences through the taste for leisure, so these are the rows the treatment of leisure moves, with the row that imposes all of France’s characteristics at once. On the U.S. the vector \(X_i\) strengthens the effect of France’s income distribution at every IES and makes that of its leisure preferences on hours a little smaller. On the UK the effect of France’s income distribution on the tax rate changes sign. The vector \(X_i\) read part of the observed income dispersion off relative hours, which are much flatter in the UK than in France, so at the UK’s income cuts France’s productivity spread falls below the UK’s, where under the common \(X\) it lies above.
