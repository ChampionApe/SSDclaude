\section{Calibration -- the U.S.\ and France}\label{app:US}\setcounter{equation}{0}
This appendix documents the data and the household heterogeneity behind the calibrations of the U.S.\ and France, whose parameters are in table \ref{table:US:CalibUSFR}. The U.S.\ is the calibrated economy of sections \ref{sec:oecd} and \ref{sec:esc}, and France's household data supply the counterfactuals of section \ref{sec:oecd}. Both calibrations are also used in the \oa{esc-countries}, where France and the UK choose their designs under the U.S.\ cost of redistribution. The UK is calibrated in the same way, from its own household survey, in the \oa{oecd-uk}. Each country has three income groups of men aged 25 to 60. The tables report, group by group, the population share $\gamma_i$, the taste for leisure $X_i$ and the productivity $\eta_i$ that the calibration assigns, and the propensity to vote $\mu_i$ taken from the data. Under the calibration of section \ref{sec:oecd} the taste for leisure is common across groups, so the $X_i$ row repeats one number, fixed by the country's average workweek, and the income distribution is carried by $\eta_i$ alone; the \oa{oecd-leisure} describes the alternative, with one $X_i$ per group identified from relative hours.

The parameters in the lower panel of table \ref{table:US:CalibUSFR} are common to the two countries. The discount factor $\beta$ matches the U.S.\ thirty-year gross interest factor $R_{2020} = 2.443$, the gross interest rate net of average multifactor productivity growth estimated by \textcite{mkvss08}, and is imposed on France. The Frisch elasticity is $\xi = 0.30$, as for Argentina, and the capital share $\alpha = 0.30$.\footnote{Averaging adjustments 2 and 3 of \textcite{Gollina1} gives capital income shares close to 0.3 for France and the U.S.} Pension spending is public cash spending on old-age and survivors pensions in 2020 from the OECD Social Expenditure Database, the measure of figure \ref{fig:US:OECDdata}. The number of workers per retiree $\nu_t$ is thirty-year gross growth of the total population in the estimates and medium-variant projections of the United Nations' World Population Prospects 2024, corrected for France as described below. The \oa{oecd-ies} reports the calibration at every intertemporal elasticity of the model's grid.

\subsection{The U.S.}
For the U.S.\ we use the March Supplement 2019 of the Current Population Survey (CPS). The low and medium income groups are cut so that their mean incomes are half the mean and the mean, and the remaining households form the high income group. The cuts set the population shares and the productivities, not the design: $\theta$ is the earnings-linked share of the public pension under the pension rules of 2020 \parencite{OECDpen}, measured in the same way for every country but the UK (appendix \ref{app:oecd26:data}). The propensity to vote is from the Voting and Registration Supplement of the CPS of November 2020, which the U.S.\ Census Bureau tabulates in Voting and Registration in the Election of November 2020.

Table \ref{table:a_US:CalibUS} shows the two features that set the U.S.\ apart. Productivity rises steeply across the groups, from $0.82$ in the low group to $2.52$ in the high one, and the propensity to vote rises with income, from $0.47$ to $0.77$, so the political objective weighs high-income households well above their population share. The low group holds $58\%$ of households.

\subsection{France}
For France we use the 2018 wave of the Luxembourg Income Study. France's design, $\theta = 0.822$, does not depend on the income cuts either, so we cut the groups at the U.S.\ percentiles, which puts the two countries' household types in one-to-one correspondence and makes the counterfactuals of section \ref{sec:oecd} a type-by-type replacement. The propensity to vote is from the Comparative Study of Electoral Systems (CSES).\footnote{\url{https://cses.org/}.} Since the normal retirement age of men retiring in 2020 was 64.5 years in France, against 66 in the U.S. \parencite[figure 3.7]{OECDpen}, we take France's population growth in 2020 to be
\begin{align*}
\frac{\mathrm{Population}\ 2020 \times (30-1.5)/30}{\mathrm{Population}\ 1990 + \mathrm{Population}\ 2020 \times 1.5/30},
\end{align*}
and the other dates in the same way, with a gap of 1.5 years in 1990 and 2020 and of 1 year from 2050 on, the gap between the normal retirement ages of men entering the labor market in 2020, 66 against 67.

At the same percentiles France's productivity profile is flatter than the U.S.\ one (table \ref{table:a_US:CalibFR}): it runs from $1.09$ to $2.49$, a ratio of the high to the low group of $2.29$ against $3.08$ in the U.S.\ (table \ref{table:US:CalibUSFR}). The propensity to vote is nearly flat, between $0.81$ and $0.86$. The common taste for leisure is $6.48$ against $4.07$ in the U.S., which is how the calibration accounts for France's shorter workweek.
