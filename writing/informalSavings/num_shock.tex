\subsection{Unanticipated reform experiments}\label{\refsec:shock}
A reform experiment asks what happens if the pension system changes at a date $t_0$ in a way nobody had priced in. The leading case is a \emph{universalisation} of the system at the calibration date, the reform the Argentina calibration of \cref{\refsec:calibration} is built around.

\smalltitle{What ``universal'' means, and why it is a pure parameter change.}
Benefits in \eqref{\refmodeleq:governmentBudget} are
\begin{align*}
    b_t^i = \left[\theta_t h_{t-1,i}\eta_{t-1,i} + (1-\theta_t)h_{t-1}\right]\overline{b}_t,
    \qquad\qquad b_t^0 = \epsilon_t h_{t-1}\overline{b}_t,
\end{align*}
so $\overline{b}_t$ and $h_{t-1}$ cancel out of the ratio of the informal and the formal benefit, and any statement of the form ``type $0$ should receive what type $j$ receives'' pins $\epsilon_t$ as a function of parameters alone. Using $h_{t-1,i}\eta_{t-1,i}/h_{t-1} = \eta_{t-1,i}^{1+\xi}/(X_{t-1,i}^{\xi}\Gamma_{h,t-1})$, the two readings we consider are
\begin{subequations}\label{\refeq:shockEps}
    \begin{align}
        \epsilon_t &= \theta_t\frac{\eta_{t-1,j}^{1+\xi}}{X_{t-1,j}^{\xi}\Gamma_{h,t-1}} + (1-\theta_t)
        &&\text{(type $0$ receives the whole benefit of type $j$),} \label{\refeq:shockEps:match}\\
        \epsilon_t &= 1-\theta_t
        &&\text{(type $0$ receives the non-contributive component only).} \label{\refeq:shockEps:flat}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
In \eqref{\refeq:shockEps:match} the informal household is treated as though it had type $j$'s earnings history; in \eqref{\refeq:shockEps:flat} it receives only the part of the formal benefit that is proportional to aggregate hours, the narrower reading of a universal pillar. Neither touches $\theta_t$: universality is a statement about who is covered, not about how contributive the formal benefit is. At the calibrated pre-reform $\epsilon$ the two lie on opposite sides of the status quo, \eqref{\refeq:shockEps:match} at $j=1$ raising $\epsilon$ and \eqref{\refeq:shockEps:flat} lowering it, so every response changes sign between them.

\smalltitle{The experiment is a second model whose horizon starts at $t_0$.}
Solve the baseline path of \cref{\refsec:PEEpath} over $t=1,\dots,T$ at the calibrated parameters. Then construct a second model that is identical except that its horizon is $t_0,\dots,T$, renumbered so that $t_0$ is its first active period, replace $\epsilon$ on it by \eqref{\refeq:shockEps}, and re-solve it, backward recursion and forward walk both, taking as given
\begin{align}\label{\refeq:shockState}
    \left(s_{t_0-1},\ \iota_{t_0-1},\ \frac{s_{t_0-1,i}}{s_{t_0-1}},\ h_{t_0-1}\right) \quad\text{read off the \emph{baseline} path.}
\end{align}
Everything that makes the reform unanticipated is in \eqref{\refeq:shockState}: the generations that acted before $t_0$ did so under the old $\epsilon$, their decisions are not revised, and what they left behind is the only channel through which the pre-reform world reaches the post-reform one. From $t_0$ onward the reform is common knowledge and permanent. The initial fixed point \eqref{\refeq:initialFixedPoint} is \emph{not} used on the copy, since its steady-state assumption is precisely what a surprise reform violates.

The inherited shares replace the consistency step of \eqref{\refeq:consistentFOC} in the copy's first period. The generation old at $t_0$ saved under the baseline's expectations, so its shares do not move with the candidate $\tau_{t_0}$: the $t_0$ first order condition, the frozen objective of every candidate and $c_{2,t_0}^i$ hold them at the baseline's values, and from $t_0+1$ on the shares are again consistent with each candidate tax. Under CRRA the inherited shares belong to the seed $s_{t_0-1}$, so the copy's first-period policy function is meaningful at that savings state only, the one the walk starts from. $h_{t_0-1}$ enters only the benefit level $\overline{b}_{t_0}$. If $t_0$ is the baseline's own first active period, nothing is inherited and the copy keeps the baseline's steady-state assumption.

\smalltitle{$\epsilon$ does not enter only through the benefit formula.}
By \eqref{\refmodeleq:governmentBudget:bbar}, $\kappa_t$ is a function of $\epsilon_{t+1}$, and $\kappa$ enters $\Gamma_{s,t}$, $\Theta_{h,t}$, $s_{t,i}/s_t$, the coefficient $A_t$ of \eqref{\refeq:auxiliary:Ainf} and the benefit level $\overline{b}_t$. The reform therefore rewrites $\epsilon$ and $\kappa$ together; with $\kappa$ left at its pre-reform value the reformed household problem would be solved against the unreformed government budget, and nothing would flag it. At the boundary, $\overline{b}_{t_0}$ is divided by $\kappa_{t_0-1}$, which depends on $\epsilon_{t_0}$, the reformed value, since the generation already old at $t_0$ is paid under the new rules; the copy rebuilds this one lagged value rather than inheriting the baseline's.

\smalltitle{Two checks before any reform number is read.}
An error in the state read off the baseline, the restriction of the horizon or the re-solve would show up as a plausible-looking reform effect rather than as a failure. The control runs the whole construction with $\epsilon$ \emph{unchanged}: the copy must then reproduce the baseline path's own tail from $t_0$ onward. It does so to the resolution of the tax grid $\mathcal{T}$ rather than to the tolerance of the re-solve, since the copy's $t_0$ condition holds the shares where the baseline's substitutes them: the two conditions share their root but not their interpolants on $\mathcal{T}$. The second check follows from the reform's definition: the ratio $b_t^0/b_t^j$ on the \emph{solved} reform path must equal $\epsilon_t$ divided by the bracket in \eqref{\refeq:shockEps:match}, exactly one under \eqref{\refeq:shockEps:match} itself. Read off the solved path rather than off the formula that produced $\epsilon$, it fails if the reference type has been mis-indexed or if $\epsilon$ and $\kappa$ are out of step.
