B2. Other countries

What shapes pensions, savings and labor supply in the U.S. and the UK

The figure repeats the paper’s figure 6.1 for the U.S. and, below it on the same axis, for the UK. Each host keeps its own calibration and changes one characteristic over the whole horizon. The characteristics are its design, moved to the two corners; its population growth, halved (mild ageing) or set to zero (acute ageing); or France’s income distribution, voting patterns or taste for leisure, with France’s households cut at the host’s income percentiles. On both hosts ageing and the design move the tax rate most, except on the U.S. at an IES of two, where France’s voting patterns move it by more than either corner of the design. Ageing and France’s voting patterns move the UK’s tax rate by less than the U.S. one at every IES. Flat benefits raise it by less up to an IES of one, while fully earnings-related ones lower it by more at every IES. Two rows change sign between the hosts. France’s income distribution raises the UK’s tax rate where it lowers the U.S. one, because at the UK’s income cuts France is the more unequal of the two (appendix F). France’s taste for leisure lengthens the UK’s workweek where it shortens the U.S. one, since France’s average taste for leisure lies between the two hosts’.

The UK at U.S. income groups

The paper cuts the UK’s households at the UK’s own income groups, which identify θ from the replacement rates at half-mean and mean income. It cuts France at the U.S. percentiles, so that its households replace the U.S. ones type by type, and regroups France at the UK’s cuts for the UK as host. The table sets the groupings side by side. Regrouping changes what the calibration reads: θ is read off the replacement rates at other cuts, ω follows, and the productivity spread is that of other groups. At the U.S. cuts the UK’s productivity spread is much narrower than at its own, while France’s at the UK’s cuts is wider than at the U.S. ones. The UK at the U.S. cuts is a linear fit of the UK’s group means against cumulative population shares rather than a recount of the survey, so its spread is an approximation. The tabs give the two regroupings group by group.

The figure imposes the counterfactuals of the first figure on the UK under the two groupings, one above the other, France’s households cut at the same groups as the host’s. Ageing moves the two alike, since it does not depend on how households are grouped. The design and France’s voting patterns move the regrouped UK much less. At the U.S. cuts its incomes are compressed, so a flat benefit redistributes little, and its own turnout varies less across the groups than at its own cuts, so France’s flat turnout changes less. France’s income distribution, conversely, raises the regrouped UK’s tax rate by much more, because next to that compressed distribution France’s is unequal.
