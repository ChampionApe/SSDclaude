\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclu}

This paper studies how two features of a pension system, whether benefits require a contribution record and how closely they follow past earnings, shape its political support and through it taxes, savings and labor supply. The setting is an overlapping generations model with capital, heterogeneous households, an informal sector and probabilistic voting, first over the size of the system and then over its design as well.

With log-GHH preferences and hand-to-mouth informal households the equilibrium tax is a function of parameters and demographics alone. It rises with the political weight of the old, with ageing and, when propensities to vote are equal, with income inequality, and it falls when benefits are more closely tied to earnings, since earnings-related benefits move revenue from poor retirees to rich ones. Calibrated to Argentina, the model accounts for about three fifths of the rise in pension spending that accompanied the 2005--2010 expansion of coverage. In the model calibrated to the U.S., ageing to a constant population is the main determinant of the tax rate and the earnings link the second, at every intertemporal elasticity of substitution we consider. In 26 synthetic OECD countries, pension spending falls with population growth about as steeply as in the data and, as in the data, is unrelated to income inequality.

When the electorate chooses the earnings link as well, costless redistribution puts the choice at a corner, the Beveridgean one in the Markov equilibria we study. An interior design needs flat benefits to be costlier than earnings-related ones, and we read the flat component as an implicit tax whose deadweight loss rises with the square of its rate. With this cost calibrated on the U.S.\ design, ageing makes the chosen design more Bismarckian at every intertemporal elasticity of substitution, while the effect of a more equal income distribution depends on that elasticity. The 26 synthetic countries reproduce the joint rise of spending and the earnings link as population growth falls, though the earnings link responds less than in the data. Whether the design follows inequality depends on the propensities to vote: at the U.S.\ political weight and propensities, more unequal countries choose less Bismarckian designs, unlike in the data, and at each country's own propensities the design does not follow inequality.

Three limitations point to future work. The model has no risk; in \textcite{BergGEwp} we study a related environment in which health risk may prevent households from qualifying for a contributive pension. Every quantitative result rests on GHH preferences, which rule out income effects on labor supply, while both the hours responses and the retirees' stake in an earnings-related design work through labor supply. And a cost quadratic in the redistribution performed cannot deliver a fully Bismarckian system, as in six of the 26 countries; a linear term in the cost, or a formality margin such as the informal sector of our Argentine model, would allow it.
